What is this chapter about?

The first fifteen chapters read like a health report: using tens of thousands of survey responses, they tell you what a fast-and-stable company looks like. But the sharpest question is still unanswered — how do I get there from where I actually am? This is the only chapter in the book that answers it. And its answer is counterintuitive: don't draw a roadmap.

An analogy first

Most people start getting fit by buying a twelve-week plan. But the plan was written for somebody else's body — your real weak link might be sleep, and the plan has you adding mileage. What actually works looks like this: check where you are today, commit only to what next week should look like, try one small change, swap it if it doesn't move anything. The path gets walked, not drawn in advance. This chapter takes that and applies it to an entire company.

Why the roadmap route fails

Corporate change has two default scripts. The first is hire someone to draw a roadmap: five levels, we're at three, two years to reach five. The trouble is the ladder is written for the industry average, and every company is stuck somewhere different — somebody else's level three may not be your bottleneck at all, so climbing it is massaging a part that doesn't hurt.

The second is the big reorg: new reporting lines, new org chart, a kickoff. The seats change, but how people actually work at nine in the morning doesn't — and within six months the old habits grow back along the new chart.

The third route: three people, three jobs

The core of the chapter is splitting "improvement" across three kinds of people, and splitting it strictly:

The people at the top do exactly two things — say where we are going (a direction on a multi-year scale, not a task list), and then clear the road: fund it, kill the approvals that block it, and keep priorities from changing every week. They do not hand down solutions.

The people in the middle coach, daily — and only by asking: what does it actually look like right now? What should it look like next? What are you going to try? What did last time teach you? They hold no correct answer; their job is to force the other person's answer out.

The people who change how the work is done are the people doing it — only they know which step hurts. Improvement isn't a separately funded "transformation project"; it's part of the day job.

One more rule: commit to the next small target only, never plan the whole path. Nobody knows how to get from "today" to "what next month should look like" — you can only try a step, look, and try again.

Then what was ING's big reorg?

The book's example is the Dutch bank ING, whose most famous move was exactly a dramatic reorg: several thousand head-office staff left without a position, everyone reapplying. But the chapter's point is not "go do a reorg" — it is that the reorg only set the conditions; what made it fast was the small stuff that happened afterwards, every day: business and engineering sitting in one squad, work no longer handed across desks, managers on the floor asking those four questions.

One honest caveat: this chapter rests on one story about one bank, not on the tens of thousands of survey responses behind the earlier chapters. It's experience worth having — it is not statistical evidence.

Remember this one line

Getting better can't be done off a roadmap: the top sets direction and clears obstacles, the middle coaches by asking and never answering, and the changes are made by the people doing the work. Commit to one small target at a time and discover the rest by experiment. There is no day the transformation is finished — the improvement habit you drilled is the output.

In one line

The first fifteen chapters established what a high-performing organization looks like. Chapter 16 is the only chapter that answers "so how do I become one" — and its answer is: not a roadmap, but a routine everyone practices daily. Leaders set direction and create conditions; managers coach by asking on the floor; the improving itself belongs to the people doing the work. ING Netherlands is the book's only full case study.

Glossary

  • The four key metrics: the book's measure of delivery performance — deployment frequency, lead time for changes (commit to production), change fail rate, time to restore service. The first two measure speed, the last two stability.
  • Transformational leadership: the five dimensions measured in Ch11 — vision, inspirational communication, intellectual stimulation, supportive leadership, personal recognition. It creates conditions, not results.
  • Generative culture: the best of Westrum's three organizational types — information reaches whoever needs to see it, and failures are investigated rather than pinned on someone.
  • Lean: the Toyota-derived management approach — shrink batch sizes, make problems visible immediately, let the people on the floor improve continuously.
  • Improvement Kata: Mike Rother's four-step loop distilled from Toyota — set direction, grasp the current condition, define the next target condition, converge on it through experiments. A kata is drilled into instinct, not filled in on a form.
  • Target condition: what the process should look like next — a measurable, time-boxed description of a state, not a list of tasks.
  • Maturity model vs capability model: the first grades organizations into levels climbed in a fixed order; the second offers a set of capabilities and lets each organization attack its own weakest one. The book firmly backs the second.
  • Squad / tribe / chapter: ING's organizational units, borrowed from Spotify — a squad is an end-to-end team of about nine, a tribe is a group of squads in one business domain, a chapter is a cross-squad guild of one discipline (all testers, say).
  • WIP (work in progress): how much unfinished work is open at once. The more is open, the slower each piece finishes.

Where it sits

This is the only chapter of Part III, "Transformation" — and the only chapter written by outside authors, the Lean IT practitioners Steve Bell and Karen Whitley Bell. It follows Part I's statistical findings (Ch1–11) and Part II's research methodology (Ch12–15), and is followed by the book's conclusion. Its real-world counterpart is concrete: any organizational change that starts with the words "we're going to do a DevOps transformation".

The problem the chapter takes on

The first fifteen chapters deliver a correlation map: continuous delivery, loosely coupled architecture, lean management, generative culture, transformational leadership — all correlate with the four key metrics. The trouble is that correlation doesn't tell you where to put your foot. A CTO finishing Chapter 15 holds two dozen capabilities. What does she do first thing Monday?

Reality offers two default answers, and this chapter convicts both.

The first is the maturity-model roadmap. Buy a five-level ladder, run an assessment, schedule two years. Its fatal flaw is that it fixes the order: the ladder is built on industry averages, while every organization is stuck somewhere different — climb it and you may spend twelve months on a capability that was never your bottleneck. Worse, it implies a finish line, and the book's central claim is that there isn't one.

The second is the big reorg. The chart does change overnight, but how people prioritize at nine in the morning, how a change reaches production, who gets called when it breaks — none of that daily behaviour moves, and within months the old habits regrow along the new chart.

What happens if you don't solve it? The common ending: the metrics get dressed up (deployment frequency lifted by slicing meaningless releases thinner), the transformation office turns from catalyst into a new approval gate, and two years later you hand in a "transformation complete" report and an organization much like the one you started with.

The core, in depth

1. Three layers: leaders create conditions, managers coach, improvement belongs to the doers

The chapter's hardest structural claim is that improvement splits across three layers, strictly — each layer does only its own part, and crossing the line is where it breaks.

Leaders do two things: set direction and create conditions. The direction is a multi-year challenge ("any change can reach production safely within a day"), not a task list. Creating conditions means funding it, killing the external approvals in the way, and the most underrated one — keeping priorities stable. Chapter 11 already measured this: transformational leadership creates conditions but not results — weak leadership all but guarantees low performance, strong leadership guarantees nothing. So the move a leader should fear most is stepping in with the solution.

Managers coach on the floor, daily, and only by asking. Four questions, over and over: what is the current condition, in data rather than impressions? What is the next target condition? What will you try? What did the last experiment teach you? A coach's value is not in knowing the answer but in forcing the other person's answer out — the moment the coach supplies solutions, the learning stops with him and never enters the team.

Improvement belongs to the people doing the work, because only they know which step hurts. The corollary is sharp: improvement should not be a separately funded "transformation project" or a centre of excellence; it is part of the day job. Outsourcing it to a dedicated team announces that the people on the floor need not think.

2. The Improvement Kata: plan the next target condition, never the path

So what does "improve daily" concretely look like? The model the book recommends is Mike Rother's Improvement Kata, distilled from Toyota — a four-step loop:

  • ① Direction / challenge — multi-year, given by leaders. E.g. "any change can reach production safely within a day of commit".
  • ② Grasp the current condition — in data, not impressions. E.g. lead time for changes is currently 11 days at the median and 39 days at p90, of which 8 days sit in a manual change-approval board.
  • ③ Set the next target condition — on a 2–4 week scale, and it is a state, not a task list. E.g. "in four weeks, low-risk changes go through automated approval and median lead time is under 3 days".
  • ④ Converge by experiment — days or hours per step, one small change at a time; whatever the result, it updates your reading of reality and you return to ②.

The soul of the thing lives between ② and ③: the path from the current condition to the target condition is genuinely unknowable in advance. Roadmap-driven transformation errs by pretending it is knowable and filling two years with it. The kata's move is to admit you don't know, commit only to the next target condition, and walk the path out through a chain of small experiments. That is what "kata" means — something drilled until it becomes instinct, not a template to fill in.

3. What ING got right wasn't the reorg — it was pulling delivery into one team

ING Netherlands is famous for its extreme 2015 reorganization: thousands of head-office staff placed without a position and required to reapply, the organization rebuilt into squads, tribes and chapters. But reading this chapter as "go do a reorg" is the most common misreading. The reorg only set the conditions; performance came from what landed in the daily work afterwards:

  • End-to-end squads: business, development and operations inside one squad, work no longer handed across desks — the organizational counterpart of Chapter 5's "can you test alone, can you ship alone".
  • Engineering leaders who are engineers: managers on the technical line come from engineering and can read the system, rather than coordinating projects. Otherwise "creating conditions" decays into chasing dates.
  • Self-service platform: teams get environments, pipelines and releases without filing a ticket and waiting — the most concrete form of "removing obstacles".
  • Improvement happens on the floor: managers stand there daily asking the four questions, instead of reviewing a dashboard once a quarter.

Separating these four from the reorg matters: the reorg was one-off and barely transferable; these four are continuous, and you can start a little of each tomorrow.

4. Transformation is a capability, not a project with an end date

The easiest line to skip and the most consequential: there is no day on which the transformation is finished. A maturity model suggests you graduate at level five; a capability model means the environment keeps moving — yesterday's bottleneck is cleared and tomorrow grows a new one. The output is not a metric hitting a number but an organization that has acquired the habit of getting better by itself: any team handed a new bottleneck knows how to read reality, set the next target condition, and experiment its way across.

That also gives a useful self-check: if your transformation has a closing report and a celebration, you probably built a project rather than a capability.

Why it matters / in real systems

This chapter is the bridge from "research findings" to "what I do on Monday morning". It pays off in three places: designing organizational change (stop handing in five-level roadmaps), the daily behaviour of a management job (coaching questions are trainable, concrete acts, not the slogan "show leadership"), and interviews and architecture reviews (asked "how did you drive an improvement", a repeatable reality → target condition → experiment chain lands far harder than "I rolled out the XX platform").

FROM THE FIELD
  • ING Netherlands (public corroboration of the book's case): in January 2015 roughly 3,500 head-office employees were placed on "mobility" and required to reapply for a role; the new organization was 13 tribes and about 350 nine-person squads; selection weighted culture and mindset above knowledge and experience. These numbers give the order of magnitude of the "reset the conditions" side of the chapter — and a reminder of what it costs.   • Google / DORA (2021): "Inclusive teams with a generative culture were half as likely to experience burnout during the COVID-19 pandemic." That is the quantified flip side of this chapter's claim that a leader's output is conditions rather than solutions. Pitfalls and disputes

  • The chapter's biggest weakness: its evidence is not the same grade as the first fifteen chapters. Part I's conclusions rest on tens of thousands of survey responses and Part II's psychometrics; this chapter is one case, one bank, written by outside consultants who were in it. It is experience worth having, but citing it as statistical evidence is dishonest — discount it a notch as you read.
  • Treating the ING reorg as a copyable recipe. It presupposed genuine existential pressure and a fully committed executive team, and it cost large-scale attrition and a clear trough. What the book actually advocates is the daily practice that followed, not the punch that opened it.
  • Cargo-culting squads and tribes. The vocabulary comes from Spotify, and Spotify insiders — agile coach Joakim Sundén among them — have said publicly that the widely circulated model was closer to a snapshot or an idealization, that Spotify kept changing and never simply ran on it. Copying the nouns most reliably yields the old organization under new names.
  • Turning coaches into another layer of middle management. Once "coach" becomes a role you report to, get signatures from and schedule meetings with, it has moved from removing obstacles to creating them. The test is simple: does this role reduce or increase the number of hops a team passes through to finish one thing?
  • Running the kata as a form-filling exercise. Handing in a "target condition sheet" every fortnight with no real experiment and nothing ever falsified is the most common way this spins in place. The value is in the drilled instinct, not the document.
  • Period limits: written in 2018, before platform engineering, developer experience (DevEx), the SPACE framework and DORA's own 2023–2024 findings filled in detail on this skeleton. The skeleton itself — direction, conditions, coaching, experiments — has not aged.

Takeaways

① The only "how" chapter in the book (Part III's sole chapter), written by outside authors Steve Bell and Karen Whitley Bell, with ING Netherlands as the case.

② Core claim: improvement can't run off a roadmap; it runs off a routine everyone drills daily — because the path from here to the target is unknowable in advance.

③ Three layers: leaders set direction and create conditions, managers coach by asking and never answering, improvement belongs to the people doing the work. Any layer crossing the line stops the learning.

④ The four-step kata: direction (years) → grasp the current condition (in data) → next target condition (2–4 weeks, a state not a task list) → small experiment (days/hours) → back to reality.

⑤ Maturity model vs capability model: the first fixes the order and implies a finish line; the book backs capabilities — go at your own weakest link first.

⑥ What transfers from ING is not the reorg but four things: end-to-end squads, engineering leaders who are engineers, a self-service platform, and improvement happening on the floor.

⑦ Transformation has no completion date: the output is not a metric hitting a number but an organization that improves itself. A closing report usually means you built a project.

⑧ Honest boundary: this is an n=1 case narrative, weaker evidence than the preceding fifteen chapters. Cargo-culted squad/tribe names and coaches becoming a new approval layer are the two most common implementation accidents.

⑨ External corroboration: DORA 2021 measured generative culture halving burnout; DORA 2024 measured unstable priorities cancelling out strong leadership. Both point at one sentence: a leader's output is conditions.
