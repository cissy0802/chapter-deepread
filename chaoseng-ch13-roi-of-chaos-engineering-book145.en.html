The ROI of Chaos Engineering

Chaos Engineering: System Resiliency in Practice · Ch 13 · The ROI of Chaos Engineering · 2020

中文 →

What is this chapter about?

Imagine you run a small team at a video-streaming company whose job is to break the system on purpose, a little, to find weak spots. At year end your boss asks: we spent all this money — what did we get? The honest answer is: "Nothing happened." And that is the hardest result in the world to prove.

This chapter tackles a very practical question: how do you show non-technical people that prevention is worth paying for?

A strange thing first

You'd expect that as the system gets sturdier, outages keep falling, so comparing outage counts proves the point. In reality, outages often drop for a few months — and then creep back.

Not because the practice stopped working, but because once people feel safe, they speed up: they ship features more often and change code more boldly. Like a driver whose brakes just got fixed and who, without noticing, drives a bit faster. The safety margin you gained gets traded for speed.

Why the old way is hard

  • You can't count what didn't happen. No fire this year — thanks to the fire drill, or just luck? You never get to see the world where you skipped the drill.
  • Big outages are rare. A handful a year; one more or one fewer says little about who deserves credit.
  • The gains get spent. As above: things feel safer, people go faster, and the outage count drifts back.

The core idea: grade it like a training course

The author's insight: what a chaos exercise really produces is a more accurate map in engineers' heads of how the system works. If the real product is people learning, then borrow the old way of grading a training course — the way you'd judge a driving school, in four steps:

  • Did the students like it? After the exercise, ask: was it worth your time?
  • Did they learn anything? Did someone discover "the system isn't what I thought"?
  • Did they change what they do? Did they fix a weak spot or add a backup plan because of it?
  • Did results improve? Does the service go down less and lose less money?

The higher the step, the closer to the money your boss cares about — and the harder to pin down. But the lower three are easy to record, so you can stack up evidence step by step.

What it buys you / how to choose

There is also a bonus people often miss: an exercise pulls teams that rarely talk into the same room. They get to know each other and learn whom to call — priceless when a real outage hits.

One honest cost: the top step can only ever be estimated; you will never get an exact bill.

Remember it in one line

Don't use "a few fewer outages" as your report card — speed will eat it. Treat chaos as training and collect evidence up four steps: liked it, learned, changed, results.

In one sentence

You think chaos engineering pays off as "fewer incidents," but incident count is the worst report card: the counterfactual is invisible, the sample is tiny, and the organization re-spends the margin it gained on speed. The sturdier approach is to treat chaos engineering as training and measure it level by level with the Kirkpatrick model: reaction, learning, behavior, results.

Glossary

  • ROI (return on investment): how much benefit an expense buys; the usual frame for justifying a budget to leadership.
  • Incident: a user-visible failure or degradation in production that needs humans to respond.
  • Counterfactual: "what would have happened if we hadn't done it" — the parallel path you can never observe directly.
  • Safety margin: how far a system is from the edge of failure; the bigger it is, the more surprise it can absorb.
  • Mental model: an engineer's internal picture of how the system works and where it breaks; chaos experiments test exactly this.
  • Game day: a scheduled exercise where several teams practice failures together in production or a production-like environment.
  • Steady state: the normal value of a business metric when the system is healthy; chaos experiments use it to tell whether anything went wrong.
  • Error budget: the unreliability allowed by an availability target — e.g. a 99.9% target leaves about 43 minutes of downtime per month.

Where it sits

Chapter 13 opens Part IV, "Business Factors," and is written by Casey Rosenthal (former manager of Netflix's Chaos Engineering team and one of the book's two lead authors). Part III covered how people learn from experiments; this chapter translates that same insight into the language of management. Next come Ch14 on open science and Ch15 on the maturity model.

What problem this chapter solves

A dedicated chaos team needs headcount, a platform, and slices of production traffic for experiments, and every year it must answer "is it worth it?" at budget time. Unlike a new feature, its output is a non-event: success means nothing happened.

Without a credible measure, the consequences are concrete: either the team is cut at the first budget squeeze, or it is pushed into claiming "incidents down 30% this quarter" — and the moment incidents rebound next quarter, the same number is used to prove it doesn't work. A bad metric hurts more than no metric.

Core ideas, explained

1. Why "fewer incidents" doesn't hold up

The intuitive math: 10 incidents a month before chaos, 7 after, so 3 saved × cost per incident = ROI. The book points out at least three cracks:

  • The counterfactual is invisible. Incident counts move with release cadence, seasonal traffic, reorgs and much more; you can't pull out the "no chaos" world as a control.
  • The sample is too small. Truly severe incidents may number in single digits per year; a difference of a few sits inside normal noise and proves no causation.
  • The benefit is ephemeral. This is the chapter's most counterintuitive point: once the system is sturdier, the organization reinvests the extra margin — more frequent releases, bolder changes, faster features — pushing the system back toward the risk boundary until incidents return to their old level.

The third point does not say chaos is useless; it says the benefit changed form: at the same incident level, the organization now moves faster. In safety science, Rasmussen's "dynamic safety model" describes exactly this drift — economic and efficiency pressure keeps pushing a system toward its safety boundary. Judging chaos by incident count means looking only at the balance after the gain was spent.

2. Change the frame: chaos engineering is learning

The chapter's pivot rests on a view that runs through the whole book: the real product of a chaos experiment is an updated mental model — discovering that the system does not work the way you thought. If the product is learning, you can borrow the most established framework for evaluating learning: the four-level model Donald Kirkpatrick proposed for corporate training in the 1950s. The training industry has used it for decades, and its strength is that it doesn't demand a dollar figure in one step; it stacks evidence level by level.

3. Kirkpatrick's four levels, mapped to chaos engineering

  • L1 Reaction: did participants find the experiment or game day worthwhile? The cheapest measure is a post-exercise survey. It proves no value, but if even participants think it wasted their time, the higher levels are moot.
  • L2 Learning: did participants learn something new? Count how often the expectations written before an experiment were overturned, and how many previously unknown dependencies or behaviors surfaced. This plugs straight into Ch9, "Creating Foresight": write expectations down and learning becomes recordable.
  • L3 Behavior (transfer): did the learning turn into action? Fixes filed after experiments, new timeouts and fallbacks, updated runbooks, reversed architecture decisions. This level can already be counted automatically from the ticketing system.
  • L4 Results: did organizational outcomes improve — availability, incident severity, time to recover, business steady-state metrics? It is the most valuable and the most exposed to the three cracks above, so the book's stance is: don't bet on this level alone; complete the causal chain with the three below it.

The beauty of the framework is that it breaks an unanswerable question into answerable ones: you can't say how much loss you avoided, but you can say (numbers illustrative) "40 experiments this quarter overturned 12 expectations and led to 9 fixes, 3 of which closed single points of failure with no fallback."

4. Collateral ROI

The book also calls out a kind of return that is easy to miss: everything outside the experiment itself. Preparing a game day often brings teams that rarely interact into one room — for the first time they align their understanding of the system and learn whom to call when things break. Arguing over what to inject exposes missing docs and knowledge silos; experiments often stumble on problems unrelated to the hypothesis (an alert nobody receives). These gains are hard to price, but they pay out in how well people coordinate during the next real incident.

Key trade-offs and choices

No measure is perfect; the trade-off is between ease of measurement, credibility, and resistance to gaming.

MeasureEasePersuasivenessMain cost / trap
Incident count before/afterHigh, data existsLooks strong, is weakNo counterfactual, tiny sample; once speed eats the gain it "proves" chaos is useless
Bugs found × estimated lossMediumMedium, nice numberFalse precision: "loss it could have caused" is all assumptions; counting bugs invites gaming
L1 ReactionVery high, one surveyWeakSatisfied ≠ useful; only a threshold signal
L2 LearningMedium, needs written expectationsMediumDepends on people honestly recording "I was wrong" — needs a blameless culture
L3 BehaviorMedium-high, tie to ticketsFairly strongMany fixes ≠ right fixes; separate "done because of the experiment" from "was planned anyway"
L4 ResultsLowStrongest (if attributable)Same counterfactual and rebound problems; only stands up as the end of an L2/L3 chain

Rule of thumb: talk L2/L3 to engineering, talk the "L3 → L4 chain" to leadership, and never claim credit with L4 or incident counts alone.

  • Early stage (a few experiments a month): nail L1 and L2 collection first — write expectations before each experiment, record which ones were overturned. It costs almost nothing.
  • At scale: wire experiment results to tickets to count L3 automatically; pair with L4 metrics such as error budgets to say "which fixes map to which risks."
  • Facing finance: if you must give a dollar figure, present it as a range with stated assumptions, not a point estimate — one precise number that gets debunked drags down all your credibility.

Why it matters / in real systems

The chapter's value reaches beyond chaos engineering: all preventive work shares the same dilemma — security, testing, tech-debt cleanup, capacity headroom all look like "nothing happened" when done well. Kirkpatrick-style layered evidence is a general way for such work to survive budget reviews. Asked in an interview or architecture review "how do you prove reliability investment pays off?", a good answer doesn't quote an incident-reduction percentage; it lays out the learning → behavior change → results chain and points out up front that incident counts rebound as teams speed up.

Industry evidence

  • Netflix describes Chaos Monkey's value as changed behavior, not fewer incidents. Its open-source repo says: "Exposing engineers to failures more frequently incentivizes them to build resilient services." That is Kirkpatrick's L3: the return lands in engineers' design habits. Netflix · Chaos Monkey README (github.com/Netflix/chaosmonkey, verified during this run)
  • Google turned "margin for speed" into policy. The gist of Google SRE's position: reliability should not aim for 100%; the part beyond the availability target is an error budget that can be deliberately spent on launching features, with launches slowed only when it runs out. That is the flip side of this chapter's "the gain gets spent" — rather than rebounding passively, spend it visibly and on purpose. Google · Site Reliability Engineering (O'Reilly, 2016), chapter "Embracing Risk" (paraphrased)
  • Netflix defines steady state with a business metric. Netflix's chaos experiments judge health by stream starts per second (SPS) rather than CPU or error rates — so experiment results speak L4's business language natively. Basiri et al., "Chaos Engineering," IEEE Software, 2016

Pitfalls and debates

  • "Fewer incidents = ROI." The most common and most dangerous: it makes the team's survival hinge on a number that naturally rebounds.
  • "Ephemeral gain = no value." The opposite: a rebound often means the margin was successfully converted into delivery speed; the gain just got booked to someone else's account.
  • Goodhart's trap. Make "bugs found" or "experiments run" a KPI and teams will farm easy small findings while deep combinational risks go untouched.
  • L1 satisfaction ≠ value. A lively exercise with happy participants doesn't mean anyone learned, let alone changed the system.
  • Kirkpatrick itself is contested. Training-evaluation researchers have long argued the causal chain between levels doesn't necessarily hold — high L1 doesn't guarantee good L4. Applied to chaos engineering, it is likewise a framework for organizing evidence, not a proof.
  • The chapter is argument-heavy, data-light. It gives no reusable industry benchmarks or published ROI figures; each organization has to build its own baseline.

Key takeaways

① Chaos engineering's output is a non-event, the hardest thing to prove to the business.

② Before/after incident counts are unreliable: invisible counterfactual, too few severe incidents.

③ The benefit is ephemeral: once sturdier, the organization speeds up, spends the margin, and incidents rebound.

④ A rebound isn't failure; the gain became delivery speed — Google's error budget institutionalizes this.

⑤ Change the frame: experiments produce updated mental models, so evaluate them like training.

⑥ Kirkpatrick's four levels: reaction, learning, behavior, results — higher is more valuable and harder to attribute.

⑦ Hold up L4 with an L2/L3 evidence chain; don't bet on incident counts, and don't give falsely precise dollar figures.

⑧ Collateral ROI: game days connect teams and expose knowledge silos, paying out in the next real incident.
