Architecture Characteristics Defined: What the "-ilities" Actually Mean

Fundamentals of Software Architecture · Ch 4 · Mark Richards & Neal Ford · 2020

中文 →

What is this chapter about?

Open a food-delivery app and order dinner. The requirements doc says "browse dishes, place an order, pay." Nobody writes "must not freeze when millions of people order at 6 p.m.," "payment must never go wrong," or "adding group-buying next year should be easy." Those unwritten demands often shape a system's skeleton more than the written features do. Chapter 4 gives them names and boundaries.

An analogy: building a house

The owner says "three bedrooms, two living rooms" — that's the feature list. But if the house sits on a fault line it must survive earthquakes; up north it must stay warm; if a floor might be added later, the frame must carry the load. The owner may never say any of it, yet the architect has to think of it — and those choices change the foundation and beams, not something you fix with a coat of paint. The book calls these "how to build it" demands architecture characteristics.

Why are they so hard to pin down?

Everyone uses these words daily: fast, stable, scalable, secure. Yet "fast" means one quick response to one person and many people served at once to another. With no shared dictionary, meetings turn into people talking past each other. Worse, some demands are never voiced at all because they feel obvious — a bank must be secure, a stock-trading system must be fast.

Three gates and three families

The book offers a sieve. To count as an architecture characteristic, a demand must pass three gates at once: it is not the business feature itself; it changes the system's skeleton; and it truly matters to success. Take payments: hand them to an outside payment service and ordinary care is enough; handle the money yourself and you must wall off a separate part just to guard it — only the second changes the skeleton.

What passes the sieve falls roughly into three families: how it runs (is it up, is it fast, does it cope with crowds), how it's built (easy to change, extend, install), and what cuts across everything (security, privacy, legal rules, accessibility).

What it gives you / how to choose

The key lesson: these qualities pull against each other. The tighter the locks, the slower the system — like flying a helicopter, where each hand and foot works a different control and moving one shifts all the others. So don't chase "best at everything"; aim for the combination that is least bad for this particular business. One honest caveat: the book's list is only a starting point — each team still has to agree on what the words mean.

One line to remember

Features decide what a system does; architecture characteristics decide how it stays standing. They pull against each other, so pick only the few that truly matter and aim for "least worst," not "best."

In one sentence

You think "nonfunctional requirements" are extras bolted on after the features — in fact they decide the skeleton. FoSA Chapter 4 renames them architecture characteristics, separates them from domain requirements and ordinary design with three criteria, distinguishes implicit from explicit ones, offers operational, structural and cross-cutting lists — and ends on the punchline that characteristics constrain each other, so an architect can only aim for the least worst architecture.

Glossary

  • Requirements / domain: what the business wants the system to do, e.g. "place order," "refund"; the domain is the body of knowledge of that business.
  • Availability: the fraction of time the system is usable when needed, often expressed as "number of nines."
  • Latency / throughput: how long one request waits / how many requests are handled per unit of time.
  • Disaster recovery: the ability and procedures to restore systems and data after a data-center-scale failure.
  • Encryption: turning data into a form only key holders can read; it costs extra computation.
  • Module / service: an independent chunk of code in a system / a chunk of program that can be deployed and run on its own.
  • SLA / SLO: an external promise / internal target for service level, e.g. "99.9% monthly availability."
  • Compliance: meeting legal and regulatory requirements such as GDPR (EU data protection) or Sarbanes-Oxley (US financial reporting).

Where it sits

Chapter 4 of Part I, "Foundations," opening a four-chapter thread on characteristics: this chapter defines them; Chapter 5 shows how to dig them out of requirements; Chapter 6 covers measuring and governing them; Chapter 7 their scope. In practice: the "why this architecture" page of a design review, and the "nonfunctional requirements" follow-up in interviews.

What problem the chapter tackles

Chapter 1 defined architecture as structure + architecture characteristics + decisions + principles. The trouble is that "characteristics" is a mess of vocabulary: some call them nonfunctional requirements, others quality attributes. The authors dislike both — the first is self-denigrating, sounding like "not a feature, so not important"; the second sounds like an after-the-fact quality assessment rather than a design input.

What goes wrong if you don't fix this? Two classic failures. One is omission: the requirements doc is full of features, nobody writes "Black Friday peak" or "payments must never be lost," and after launch the skeleton can't cope — and skeletons are almost impossible to retrofit. The other is greed: wanting every "-ility" and ending up with a generic monster that tries to support everything and does nothing well. This chapter fixes the vocabulary and boundaries first, so Chapter 5's "which ones to pick" has ground to stand on.

Core ideas, explained

① Three criteria: what counts as an architecture characteristic

The book's definition: a requirement is an architecture characteristic only if it meets all three criteria —

  • It specifies a nondomain design consideration: requirements say what the application should do; characteristics state the operational and design criteria for success — how to implement and why. A target performance level, for instance, is often absent from the requirements doc yet is a criterion for success.
  • It influences some structural aspect of the design: does it need special structural support? The book's example is payments. With a third-party payment processor, no special structure is needed — normal security hygiene suffices. If the application processes payments in-house, the architect may carve out a separate module, component or service to isolate it — that is structural.
  • It is critical or important to application success: a system could support countless characteristics, but each one adds design complexity, so keep only those that truly matter.

The criteria earn their keep by exclusion: "the UI should look nice" doesn't change the skeleton — it's a design issue; "support refunds" is a domain requirement; "consistent log format," if not critical, shouldn't be promoted to an architecture characteristic.

② Implicit vs. explicit

Explicit characteristics appear in the requirements doc or specific instructions, such as "support 100,000 concurrent users." Implicit characteristics rarely get written down yet are necessary for success: availability, reliability and security are nearly always among them — no business stakeholder writes "please don't get hacked." Some implicit ones are domain-specific: the book cites a high-frequency trading firm, where architects must design every system for low latency because milliseconds are money, while architects in other domains never need that level. This is exactly why architects need domain knowledge: implicit characteristics can only be "heard" by someone who understands the business.

③ A deliberately incomplete list: operational, structural, cross-cutting

The book stresses that the list can never be complete — teams and industries invent their own characteristics. But it groups the common ones into three families:

For the easily confused operational characteristics, the book's working definitions: availability — how long the system must be available, and if 24/7, steps to get it running quickly after a failure; continuity — disaster recovery capability; recoverability — business continuity requirements, e.g. how quickly the system must be back online after a disaster, which drives backup strategy and duplicated hardware; reliability / safety — whether the system must be fail-safe, whether it is mission critical in a way that affects lives, whether failure would cost the company large sums; robustness — handling errors and boundary conditions while running, such as a lost internet connection or a power outage; scalability — continuing to operate as users or requests grow. Performance is more than "fast": the book lists stress testing, peak analysis, analysis of how often functions are used, capacity and response times, and warns that performance acceptance can be a project of its own taking months.

Turning "nines" into numbers makes it concrete: 99.9% allows about 8.8 hours of downtime a year, 99.99% about 53 minutes, 99.999% about 5 minutes. Each extra nine shrinks the allowance tenfold while the redundancy, automated failover and drills behind it grow steeply — which is why availability "changes the skeleton."

Structural characteristics concern the code itself: configurability (can end users easily change configuration through usable interfaces), extensibility (how important it is to plug in new functionality), installability, reuse (can components be shared across products), localization (multiple languages, currencies, units of measure), maintainability (how easy it is to apply changes and enhancements), portability (must it run on more than one platform or database), and upgradeability (can old versions be upgraded quickly and easily).

Cross-cutting characteristics don't fit a single bucket: accessibility (users with color blindness or hearing loss), archivability (when data must be archived or deleted), authentication, authorization, legal, privacy (hiding transactions even from internal DBAs), security (encrypt data in the database? encrypt internal network traffic?), and usability (how much training users need to reach their goals).

④ Definitions are a battlefield: ISO, composites and "Italy-ility"

The same word means different things to different teams. The book offers three layers of defense. First, lean on the ISO (International Organization for Standardization) quality model, which breaks characteristics into finer parts by function — e.g. performance efficiency = time behavior + resource utilization + capacity; reliability = maturity + availability + fault tolerance + recoverability; maintainability = modularity + reusability + analyzability + modifiability + testability. ISO's "functional suitability" (completeness, correctness, appropriateness) the book explicitly says is not an architecture characteristic — it is the very motivation for building the software.

Second, beware composite characteristics: words like "agility" or "reliability" bundle several smaller characteristics and must be decomposed before they can be discussed or measured (Chapter 6 goes further). Third, organizations invent their own. The book's anecdote: after one client suffered a serious communications outage involving Italy, it demanded that every architecture support "Italy-ility" — in practice a specific mix of availability, recoverability and resilience. The lesson: names can be anything, but the definition must be shared inside the team, ideally in a single ubiquitous glossary.

⑤ Trade-offs and the "least worst" architecture

Why can a system support only a few? Two reasons. Each characteristic needs design effort and perhaps structural support. More fundamentally, characteristics are interconnected. The book's example: improving security almost certainly hurts performance — more on-the-fly encryption and more indirection for hiding secrets all cost time. Its metaphor is the helicopter: pilots struggle to learn because each hand and each foot works a control, and changing one affects the others, so they must be coordinated together.

Hence the chapter's most quoted line: never shoot for the best architecture, but rather the least worst architecture. Too many characteristics produce generic solutions that try to solve every business problem; such designs become unwieldy and rarely work. The companion advice: since choosing right the first time is hard, make the architecture as iterative and easy to change as possible — when change is cheap, a wrong first choice costs less.

Why it matters / in real systems

"State priorities explicitly and accept sacrifices between characteristics" isn't an idealization from the book; some of the industry's best-known systems did exactly that.

Industry evidence · claims from public sources (paraphrased)
  • Amazon · Dynamo paper (SOSP 2007): for scenarios like the shopping cart, it explicitly ranks "always writeable" high availability above strong consistency, leaving conflicts to be resolved by the application at read time; and it argues for setting latency SLAs at a high percentile such as p99.9 rather than the average. A textbook case of picking the critical characteristic, reshaping structure around it, and accepting the sacrifice of another. In reviews and interviews: asked "why this architecture?", a strong answer doesn't list technologies — it starts with "the three characteristics that matter most here are X, Y and Z, and we sacrificed W for them." Chapter 5 covers how to find those three in the requirements.

Pitfalls and debates

  • "Nonfunctional" ≠ "secondary": the authors drop the term precisely because it implies unimportance, while skeleton-level failures are usually harder to fix than feature bugs.
  • More is not better: every characteristic adds complexity and may conflict with others. Listing a dozen as "all important" is the same as having no priorities.
  • Implicit characteristics are the easiest to miss: absent from the requirements doesn't mean unneeded. Security and availability are present by default; domain-specific ones (like trading latency) require asking people who know the business.
  • Not every characteristic changes the skeleton: criterion two is a sieve — don't promote every design habit into an architecture characteristic.
  • Align the vocabulary: scalability vs. elasticity and performance vs. responsiveness are routinely blurred; teams should keep their own glossary, with ISO definitions as an anchor.
  • The list shows its age: the book came out in 2020, and characteristics discussed heavily since — cloud cost, observability — carry little weight in it; the list declares itself incomplete and should be extended for your business.

Key takeaways

① Architecture characteristics are the success criteria for how a system stays standing — peers of the domain requirements that say what it does, and they decide the skeleton.

② All three criteria are required: nondomain design consideration, influences structure, critical to success.

③ Payments: a third-party processor doesn't qualify; in-house processing that must be isolated into its own module does.

④ Explicit ones are written in requirements; implicit ones aren't — availability, reliability, security, plus domain-specific ones like trading latency.

⑤ Three families: operational (availability, performance, recoverability, scalability…), structural (maintainability, extensibility, portability…), cross-cutting (security, privacy, legal, accessibility…).

⑥ The list is intentionally incomplete; decompose composites; keep one shared team definition, anchored on ISO.

⑦ Characteristics constrain each other — security drags performance — like a helicopter's controls.

⑧ Pick only the few critical characteristics, aim for "least worst" not "best," and keep the architecture easy to iterate so wrong choices are cheap.

⑨ Interview line: "what matters most is X, Y, Z, and we sacrificed W for it" beats listing technologies.
