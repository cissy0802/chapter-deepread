Better Together or Better Apart?

A Philosophy of Software Design · Ch 9 · Better Together or Better Apart? · Ousterhout · 2nd ed 2021

中文 →

What is this chapter about?

Programmers make one small decision all day long: should these two pieces of functionality live together, or apart? Many people have a default answer: "split everything finely; the shorter each piece, the better." This chapter says: not necessarily. Splitting has a cost of its own, and sometimes the right move is to combine.

An analogy

Think of a kitchen. Keep the knife next to the cutting board and chopping is easy. Sort strictly by type instead (knives in the east cupboard, boards in the west) and it looks tidy, but every time you chop you walk to two places. On the other hand, putting dish soap and flour in the same jar is combining things that should stay apart. The test of a tidy kitchen is not how finely it is sorted, but how smoothly it works.

A strange thing first

Someone takes a few dozen lines of code and splits them into eight pieces of two or three lines each. Every piece looks "clean." Then a reader has to jump between eight places to work out what the code actually does. Each piece is easy to read; together they are hard to follow.

How it works, intuitively

  • Splitting charges a toll. Each new piece is one more name to remember and one more hand-off. Things that belonged side by side get separated, and a reader may not even know where the other half lives.
  • Four situations call for combining: both pieces rely on the same inside knowledge; they always show up together; they are really about the same thing; you can't understand one without reading the other.
  • One situation calls for splitting: keep the "anyone can use this" part apart from the "written for one special case" part. Picture a general "undo button" that only records each step and rolls them back in order; how each step is actually undone is described by each feature itself.
  • Long is not a crime. If a long piece of code reads smoothly from top to bottom, it is fine. The signal to split is that the piece you pull out can be understood on its own.

What it gives you / how to choose

When you're torn between together and apart, ask: which way leaves the reader with less to keep in mind? One honest cost: this is a judgment call that takes experience. There is no simple "split after so many lines" rule to lean on.

One line to remember

Whether to split depends on how easy the whole is to understand, not on how short each piece is.

In one sentence

You might assume finer splits and shorter functions mean better design. Ousterhout says splitting has costs of its own, and the deciding factor is not line count but the complexity of the whole system: choose the structure with the best information hiding, the fewest dependencies, and the deepest interfaces.

Glossary

  • Module: a unit of code with an interface and an implementation: a class, a method, a library, or a subsystem. This chapter's advice applies to both classes and methods.
  • Method / function: a named, callable block of code, the smallest kind of module.
  • Interface: everything a user must know to use a module well, including signatures and informal rules such as when it fails.
  • Deep / shallow module: from Chapter 4. Deep means a simple interface hiding a lot of functionality. Shallow means the interface is nearly as complex as the implementation, so the layer saves the caller almost nothing.
  • Information leakage: from Chapter 5. The same design knowledge, such as a data format, appears in several modules, so changing it means changing all of them.
  • Dependency: when one piece of code can't be understood or changed without looking at another.
  • Buffering: when reading a file, fetching a large chunk into memory at once so later small reads come from memory instead of the disk.
  • Undo: the editor feature that rolls back recent operations one step at a time.
  • goto: a C statement that jumps straight to a labeled spot inside the function.

The problem this chapter tackles

A popular intuition says smaller modules are always better and splitting is free tidying. The author says that's wrong: splitting adds complexity, and the costs are easy to overlook. He lists four:

  • More components: each new class or method adds a name and an interface to remember. The more components, the harder they are to keep track of.
  • Glue code: separate pieces often need extra code to manage them, such as passing data back and forth between two objects.
  • Related code gets separated: logic that should be read together ends up in two places. Readers struggle to see both at once, and may not even know the other half exists.
  • Possible duplication: shared logic that both sides need tends to get written twice.

He also gives four signals for combining. The two pieces share information, such as a format or a piece of knowledge both rely on. They are always used together, in both directions: using A means using B, and using B means using A. They overlap conceptually, so a single higher-level concept covers both. And you can't understand one without reading the other. Ignore this and a codebase drifts into a pile of shallow modules: each one short and "clean," the combination something nobody can follow at a glance.

The core ideas, explained

1. Combine when information is shared

The author returns to the HTTP server example from Chapter 5. Students wrote "read the request from the network" and "parse the request" as two methods. But reading has to understand the request format to know when the request ends, and parsing needs the same format. The same knowledge in two places is information leakage. Merging them into one method keeps the format knowledge in one place and shortens the interface.

2. Combine when it simplifies the interface

When two modules each do half of a job, callers often have to put the halves together by hand. Once merged, the module can do it automatically. The author's example is Java I/O. To read a file efficiently you create a FileInputStream, then wrap it in a BufferedInputStream. Almost everyone needs buffering, but everyone has to remember the extra wrapper, and forgetting it silently slows the program down. Fold buffering into the file stream and turn it on by default, and that knowledge disappears from every caller. Merging can also remove the part of an interface that only exists to pass information between two modules.

3. Combine to eliminate duplication

When the same code pattern keeps appearing, find a way to keep just one copy. The usual way is to extract a method. The author's caveat: it only pays off if calling the method is simpler than the code it replaces. If the original snippet is two or three lines and the new method needs a long list of parameters, nothing is saved. Another way is to restructure so the code runs in only one place. The book's example is C code that uses goto to jump to a single cleanup section at the end of a function, instead of repeating resource-release code in every error branch.

4. Separate general-purpose and special-purpose code

This is the chapter's one rule that clearly says split: when a general mechanism and special-purpose code share a module, pull them apart. Ideally a lower layer provides the general mechanism and a higher layer holds code written for specific cases, and the general module knows nothing about the special-purpose code. The author names this red flag Special-General Mixture.

His long example is undo in an editor. Students often put undo logic inside the text class. That mixed "keeping history," which has nothing to do with text, into the text class, and other changing state such as the selection and cursor still couldn't be undone. The author's fix is two layers. A general History module only stores a list of "actions" and supports fences that group actions together, so one undo rolls back to the previous fence. If a find-and-replace changes ten places, for example, one undo reverts all ten together. Each special-purpose module (text, selection, cursor) defines how its own actions are undone and redone. The History module never knows what an action contains, so it can serve any future feature.

5. Two counterexamples: splits in the wrong place

The author also shows cases where separating was the wrong call. The first is the cursor and the selection. Some student projects put both in one object, reasoning that the cursor always sits at one end of the selection. The author argues this didn't simplify the interface. Instead it forced special cases like "a cursor with no selection" everywhere, and two separate objects were clearer. Sharing a little information is not enough to justify combining; what matters is whether the merged interface actually gets simpler. The second is a separate logging class: a class that only logged network errors, where each method was called from exactly one place and did one small thing. It hid nothing and just sent readers back and forth between the error site and the logger. Putting the logging back where the error happens is better.

6. Splitting and joining methods: long is not a crime

This is the chapter's most-quoted section. The author holds that a long method is not a problem in itself. A method of a hundred-plus lines made of a few independent blocks that run in sequence is easy to read top to bottom. Splitting it just makes the reader jump around. Depth matters more than length: make methods deep first, then check whether they are short enough to read easily.

There are two ways to split a method. The good way is to extract a subtask: the child method has a clear job and makes sense on its own, so a reader knows what it does without reading its body, and the parent becomes easier to read. A common bad way is to cut a method into a first half and a second half that callers must call in order. Both new methods tend to be shallow, with interfaces almost as complex as their implementations, and callers must know the calling order. There is one test: after the split, can each method be understood without reading the others? If not, the author calls it a red flag: Conjoined Methods, two pieces that look separate but can only be understood together, meaning they should be one. In the other direction, joining methods can remove shallow interfaces, remove duplication, or pull scattered state back into one place.

7. New in the 2nd edition: disagreeing with Clean Code

The second edition adds a section answering Robert Martin's Clean Code. Martin argues that "the first rule of functions is that they should be small; the second rule is that they should be smaller than that," and the functions in his book are often just a few lines long. Ousterhout's reply: this advice puts the focus on length and encourages lots of shallow methods, each with an interface nearly as complex as its body, and readers end up jumping between them. He agrees that shorter methods are usually easier to read, but length is always a means; depth and being understandable on its own are the goal.

Why it matters / real systems

This chapter is one of the most-used rulers in code review. "This function is 300 lines, split it" and "these little methods make me jump around, merge them" both come up. The chapter doesn't hand you a position; it gives you questions you can test against real code: can the extracted piece be understood on its own? What does it hide? The same holds at the architecture level. Split one service into two that share a data format, always deploy together, and can't function without each other, and you've just built conjoined methods with network calls and release coordination added on top.

Evidence and sources · publicly verifiable

  • The Ousterhout–Martin public debate (2024–2025). The two authors left a long written discussion on GitHub. Ousterhout points out that Clean Code's PrimeGenerator example is split into 8 very small methods that are entangled and must be read together, and argues they should be combined. Martin keeps his preference for decomposition: if he has to err, he'd rather over-split, because extra methods can be inlined later. When both rewrote the code, one of Martin's intermediate versions split a loop into two and ran 3–4x slower; the loops were later merged back. Source: GitHub · johnousterhout/aposd-vs-clean-code
  • Linux kernel: goto to centralize cleanup. The kernel coding style has a section on "centralized exiting of functions": use goto when a function exits from several places and must do common cleanup, because it reduces nesting and prevents errors from failing to update individual exit points. The same guide takes a similar view on length: a function's maximum length is inversely proportional to its complexity and indentation level, and a long but simple case statement is fine. Source: Linux kernel · Documentation/process/coding-style.rst
  • Java I/O: the cautionary tale of leaving buffering to the caller. The author uses it in both Chapter 4 and this chapter: nearly everyone needs buffering, yet callers must add the wrapper by hand. Many later standard libraries make buffering the default for file I/O. Source: Ousterhout, A Philosophy of Software Design, Chapters 4 and 9

Pitfalls and debates

  • "Shorter functions are always better." Short is a common side effect of readable code, not the goal. Split a method that reads straight through into eight pieces and the reader has to assemble a puzzle from eight places, which is a heavier load.
  • "Ousterhout is against splitting." No. He explicitly supports extracting self-contained subtasks and insists on separating general from special-purpose code. What he opposes is splitting just to cut length.
  • "A little shared information means combine." The cursor-and-selection example shows the final test is whether the merged interface really gets simpler, not whether the two overlap.
  • "Extracting a method always removes duplication." If calling the new method is as complex as the lines it replaced, you've only moved the duplication into the parameter list.
  • The debate is still open. Martin's side holds that small methods with good names serve as documentation and over-splitting costs less than it seems. Ousterhout's side holds that entangled small methods carry a real cognitive cost. Each concedes some of the other's points in the debate, yet they don't agree. That alone shows the test has to be applied to specific code.

Takeaways

① Whether to combine or split depends on the complexity of the whole system, not on line counts or class counts.

② Splitting has four costs: more components, glue code, related logic pulled apart, and duplication.

③ Four signals for combining: shared information, always used together, conceptual overlap, one can't be read without the other.

④ Combine when it simplifies the interface, e.g. building buffering into the file stream by default.

⑤ Removing duplication is worth it, but only if the extracted method is simpler than the snippet it replaces.

⑥ The one clear case for splitting: separate general mechanisms from special-purpose code, as with undo split into History plus each module's own actions.

⑦ Long methods are not a crime. Splitting well means extracting a self-contained subtask; splitting badly means cutting a method into halves that must be called in order.

⑧ Red flags: Conjoined Methods (can't read one without the other), Special-General Mixture, Repetition.

⑨ The 2nd edition explicitly disputes Clean Code's "smaller is better," and the authors' 2024–2025 public debate put the disagreement on the record.
