What is this chapter about?

An app you use stops loading one night; by morning it works again. What fixed it was not only servers but a group of people woken by their phones. Who gets woken first, who makes the call, and how news travels decide whether you waited five minutes or five hours.

Earlier chapters described chaos engineering as breaking machines on purpose to see whether the system copes. This chapter says: the team itself is a system too, and it can — and should — be carefully shaken as well.

An analogy: fire drills

A school runs a fire drill every term. The useful drills are not just "ring the bell, everyone walks out". They add a twist: "stairwell A is blocked today", "the teacher who usually leads this class is off sick".

Add a twist and problems surface: nobody knows where the back stairs are; the substitute doesn't know the meeting point. Finding that out in a drill is far cheaper than finding it out in a real fire.

Why the old way was hard

Many companies keep two or three copies of every machine yet never notice that people come in a single copy: only Sam understands a certain system, and when Sam is on holiday nobody can fix it.

The other old habit is changing how the organisation works all at once: from tomorrow there's a new on-call arrangement for everyone, no pilot, no plan for going back if it's worse. Nobody would ship new software to every machine that way.

How it works: take the drill idea to people

  • First say what "normal" looks like. For example: when something big breaks, someone steps up to lead within ten minutes and customers get told.
  • Then add a twist that really happens. The person who knows the most "isn't here this time"; the usual chat tool "is down this time".
  • Start small. Try it with one group first, stop whenever it goes wrong, and never put real customers at risk.
  • Treat rule changes as experiments too. Want a new on-call arrangement? Let one group try it for a few weeks, watch whether things get better or worse, and switch back if not.

What it buys you / how to choose

Knowledge that lived only in one person's head gets dragged out, written down and taught to others. When something breaks, the team no longer depends on one or two heroes; whoever is around can pick it up.

One honest cost: the subject is people, so getting it wrong damages trust. Springing surprises on people, or using a drill to judge who did badly, teaches everyone to stop speaking honestly.

Remember it in one line

A team is a system: a person can be a single point of failure, communication has delays, and rules have bugs. Probe them the way you run a drill — small, stoppable — and find the weak spots before a real incident does.

In one line

You think chaos engineering is about machines; in fact an organisation is itself a complex distributed system — people are nodes, communication is the network, process is the protocol. The four principles of chaos engineering — define steady state, form a hypothesis, vary real-world events, minimise blast radius — carry over unchanged to safely probe, and even safely change, an organisation.

Glossary

  • Sociotechnical system: people, teams, process and software/hardware treated as one system whose successes and failures come from both together.
  • Game Day: a scheduled failure exercise where a team causes or simulates faults under controlled conditions to practise detection and response.
  • Incident Command System (ICS): an emergency-management method from US firefighting that divides work by role, not rank: who commands, who acts, who communicates outward.
  • Incident Commander (IC): the person who coordinates and decides during an incident, and in principle does not do the fixing.
  • On-call: a rotation responsible for answering alerts and handling failures outside working hours.
  • Steady state: the observable level of key metrics when things are "normal"; experiments use it to tell whether something went wrong.
  • Blast radius: how far the damage could spread if an experiment goes wrong.
  • Adaptive capacity: the ability of a system, including its people, to adjust and keep functioning when something unexpected happens.
  • Weak signal: a scattered, ambiguous, easy-to-dismiss sign of trouble before an incident.

Where it sits

Chapter 10, the second chapter of Part III "Human Factors", written by Andy Fleener, who leads platform operations at SportsEngine, a SaaS company for youth and amateur sports. It follows Ch9's design sessions for surfacing blind spots in people's heads and precedes Ch11, People in the Loop. This chapter turns people from the observers of an experiment into its subject.

The problem this chapter tackles

A typical scene: a SaaS platform with multi-zone redundancy, where any database can fail over automatically. Yet in a major 3 a.m. outage, what actually slows recovery is human: the on-call engineer isn't sure whether to wake the database owner; three teams investigate in three chat channels; support finds out twenty minutes later. No single point of failure in the technology; single points everywhere in the organisation.

The usual approach has two blind spots. It shakes machines but not people: failure drills test automatic failover but never what happens when the person who knows most is absent. It changes organisations without experiments: new on-call rules or a reorg usually flip for everyone overnight, with no canary and no rollback switch — exactly the kind of release chaos engineering argues against on the technical side.

So the question is: can the methods of chaos engineering be applied to people and organisations, exposing organisational weak spots ahead of time and making organisational change as testable and reversible as a code change?

The core ideas, explained

1. Put the "socio" back in sociotechnical

The starting point is an often-ignored fact: the system in an incident is never just software. An alert fires, and everything after that — who sees it, how they judge it, whom they call, when they escalate — is human behaviour. An organisation is a system of systems: each team has its own goals, tools and habits, and teams are coupled by communication.

Seen this way, every familiar technical idea has a counterpart: nodes are people and roles, the network is communication channels, protocols are processes, a single point of failure is the one person who "is the only one who knows", and the round trips of conversation are the organisation's network latency.

2. Adaptive capacity: success and failure are one coin

The chapter takes the "new view" of resilience engineering: on most days nothing breaks not because there are no disturbances but because people keep adjusting, usually unseen — someone restarts a stuck job in passing, someone notices an odd metric and asks about it. Success and failure come from the same adaptive behaviour with different outcomes. (This is what Hollnagel calls Safety-II: studying why things usually go right, not only why they go wrong.)

So the engineering goal is not only "eliminate error" but grow adaptive capacity: more people who can spot weak signals and take over unfamiliar situations. Chaos experiments serve this by manufacturing the unexpected under control and seeing where adaptive capacity holds and where it snaps.

3. Carrying the four principles over to people

  • Steady state: organisations have an observable "normal" too — how fast someone takes the IC role in a major incident, how fast the first external update goes out, how many people get pulled in.
  • Hypothesis: "Without the person who knows this system best, the team can still locate the problem within the agreed time" — falsifiable, and written down beforehand.
  • Vary real-world events: inject what genuinely happens — a key person on holiday or unreachable, the main chat tool down, a new hire's first on-call shift.
  • Minimise blast radius: start in a drill, start with one team, stop at any time; participants know, and results improve process rather than grade individuals.

4. Three case studies from SportsEngine

The chapter grounds these principles in three cases from the author's platform operations team and the wider product development organisation. Each leaves one criterion worth taking away:

  • Gaming Your Game Days: a game day should test not only whether machines heal but people — who commands, how information flows between teams, whether the runbook matches what people actually do. Criterion: if a game day ends with only technical findings and nothing about roles or communication, it exercised half the system.
  • Connecting the Dots: weak signals before an incident are usually scattered across different people, each harmless on its own. Criterion: ask "who saw this signal, and whom would they tell?" If the answer is "nobody would say anything", that link in the organisation is broken — the same defect as an alert nobody subscribes to.
  • Changing a Basic Assumption Safely: every organisation runs on premises everyone assumes and nobody has tested. Changing one is like shipping a risky release: write the hypothesis and success metrics, pilot small, watch steady state, keep a way back. Criterion: any organisational change should be able to answer "what is the blast radius, and how do we roll back?"

5. Communication is latency; leadership is emergent

In an incident, communication is the organisation's network latency: the same finding repeated in three channels and an IC unsure which message is newest amount to packet loss plus retransmission. ICS-style fixed roles (command, operations, external communication) and a single incident channel are, in effect, a protocol for that network.

The other observation is that leadership is an emergent property of the system, not a job title. Who coordinates an incident should follow from role and training, not seniority — so IC should be a rotating, trainable role, and experiments can test specifically "when the usual IC is away, can someone else step in?"

Key trade-offs and choices

Table one: the same principle, applied to machines versus people.

DimensionTechnical chaosHumanistic chaos
What you injectKill processes, add latency, cut the networkKey person absent, tool unavailable, role rotation
Steady-state metricSuccess rate, p99 latencyTime to IC in place, time to first external update
RepeatabilityHigh: same injection, roughly same resultLow: people learn, the second run differs
Main riskUsers affectedTrust damaged, seen as performance review
ConsentNotify affected teamsParticipants informed; results never grade people

People learn and remember being "set up", so the blast radius of an organisational experiment must be measured in trust, not only in user impact.

Table two: choosing among organisational disturbances.

ApproachFits whenCost
Tabletop exerciseJust starting; process not yet written downCheap, but doesn't test real reactions
Game day with human twistsTesting roles, communication, handoffsHours per session, many people
Process change piloted in one teamNew on-call, escalation rules, reorgsLong cycle (weeks); metrics noisy
Rotating IC in real incidentsMature training and backup existReal users online; a senior backstop is required
  • The process lives in a few people's heads: write it out in a tabletop first, then run a game day.
  • The team has an obvious hero: run a game day with the hero absent; it exposes knowledge single points fastest.
  • You need to change a rule everyone assumes: don't flip it overnight; pilot it like a canary release, measure, keep a way back.

Why it matters / in real systems

ICS is itself a successful precedent for designing people as a system: it emerged from multi-agency firefighting during the 1970s California wildfires and was later widely borrowed for software incident response. This chapter goes one step further — don't just adopt the protocol; use experiments to check whether it actually works in your organisation.

Asked in an interview or design review "does your system have single points of failure?", answering with database replication is one level. Adding "we also check for people single points: game days remove the main owner, and IC is a rotating, trained role" shows you understand reliability as a property of a sociotechnical system.

Industry evidence

  • PagerDuty: the commander is chosen by role, not rank, and keeps hands off. Its public incident response docs say the IC will "become the highest ranking individual on any major incident call, regardless of their day-to-day rank", and stress "You should not be performing any actions or remediations, checking graphs, or investigating logs" — leadership-by-role in practice. PagerDuty · Incident Response Docs · Incident Commander training page (github.com/PagerDuty/incident-response-docs, verified during this run)
  • The same docs treat people as components to be exercised. IC training runs through shadowing, reverse shadowing and "Participate in Failure Friday" — first as scribe, then as IC; the graduation test is that "an IC puts themselves on the schedules". Same source
  • A definition of Safety-II. The resilience engineering notes maintained by Lorin Hochstein, formerly of Netflix's chaos engineering team, summarise Safety-II as "the system’s ability to succeed under varying conditions" and note "performance is always variable" — the theory behind this chapter's "success and failure share a source". Pitfalls and debates

  • Turning "shaking people" into an ambush. Pulling someone without warning to watch the team flounder may expose problems short-term, but destroys psychological safety — next time nobody speaks honestly. That is precisely what "humanistic" is about.
  • Grading people on the results. A drill tests process and organisation, not individuals; once results feed performance reviews, people perform for the drill.
  • Assuming organisational experiments are repeatable. People learn, so the same drill is distorted the second time; keep varying the scenario rather than rerunning one script.
  • Adopting only ICS vocabulary. An IC role that is never exercised and that only one person can fill is redundancy on paper.
  • Metrics drowned in noise. Organisational changes have long cycles and few samples; a few weeks' change in "time to IC" is hard to attribute. Be honest about uncertainty.
  • Limits. The chapter rests mainly on one company's practice, with little cross-organisation quantitative evidence; for small teams, dedicated human experiments may cost more than they return. But it pushes the boundary of reliability from machines to organisations, setting up Ch11 and Ch18.

Key takeaways

① Thesis: an organisation is a complex distributed system; the four principles of chaos engineering apply unchanged to probing and changing it.

② The pain: plenty of technical redundancy, yet people remain single points; organisational change often flips overnight with no rollback.

③ Mapping: people = nodes, communication = network (latency), process = protocol, "only one person knows" = single point of failure.

④ Resilience view: success and failure come from the same adaptive behaviour (Safety-II); the goal is adaptive capacity and spotting weak signals.

⑤ Principles carried over: organisational steady state (time to IC, time to external update), falsifiable hypotheses, real twists (key person absent), small blast radius plus consent.

⑥ Three cases: game days must exercise people; connect scattered weak signals; change basic assumptions as reversibly as a canary release.

⑦ Communication is organisational latency; leadership is emergent, so IC is a rotating, trainable role.

⑧ Pitfalls: ambushes damage trust, grading people, assuming repeatability, adopting the vocabulary without drills.
