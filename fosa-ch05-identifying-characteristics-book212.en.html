Fundamentals of Software Architecture · Ch 5 · Mark Richards & Neal Ford · 2020

中文 →

What is this chapter about?

The previous chapter said a system has more than features: it also has to hold up — survive a crowd, stay up, stay easy to change. This chapter asks the next question: where do you find those requirements, and how many do you keep? The catch is that bosses and customers never use those words. They say "we must ship before the competition" or "users have to be happy".

An analogy: the interpreter

You're renovating a home and the owner mentions "my elderly parents live with us". They never said "non-slip tiles, grab bars, no door thresholds", but a good designer hears it instantly. An architect is that kind of interpreter, turning business language into engineering requirements. When the business says "we're in a hurry", that really means three things: change fast, test fast, release fast. Hear only one and you miss the other two.

A strange little fact first

A university has a thousand students and opens class registration for ten hours. On average that's fewer than two students a minute — a tiny server would do. But anyone who went to college knows: everyone waits until the last few minutes. What the system really has to survive is the stampede right before the deadline. No document says so; only someone who knows the territory "hears" it.

Three sources, one sieve

The book says these requirements hide in three places: the business's worries (losing to rivals, losing users), what's written in the requirements ("maybe millions of users one day"), and the common sense everyone in the field takes for granted (like that procrastination). Don't keep everything you dig up: the longer the list, the more the system becomes a Swiss Army knife that does everything badly. The book's advice is simple — ask the business to pick just the three that matter most, in no particular order.

What it gives you / how to choose

One more judgment call: some needs don't have to touch the skeleton at all — a clever trick in the code is enough. If every franchise store wants its own menu, you can build "slots" into the whole system, or just leave a swappable step inside the program. Both work; they just cost very different amounts. The honest price: all of this runs on conversation and judgment, not a formula, so different people will dig up different lists.

One line to remember

The business won't say "-ility": the architect has to translate their worries, add the field's common sense, then ruthlessly keep only three.

In one sentence

You'd think architecture characteristics arrive as a list from whoever writes requirements — in fact nobody writes that list: Chapter 5 of FoSA shows how the architect translates characteristics out of three sources — domain concerns, requirement text, and implicit domain knowledge — then shrinks the list hard: stakeholders pick the top three, unranked, and the test "could design handle this instead?" decides whether a candidate earns a place in the architecture at all.

Glossary

  • Architecture characteristics: from the previous chapter — requirements that aren't business features, shape the system's structure, and are critical to success, such as scalability or availability.
  • Stakeholders: anyone who has demands on the system or is affected by it: executives, product, business, operations, users.
  • Scalability / elasticity: keeping up as the user base grows over time / absorbing a sudden short burst of traffic and then scaling back.
  • Time to market: how long it takes for an idea to actually reach users.
  • Deploy: putting a new version of the software into production so users get it.
  • Microkernel architecture: a core system plus many pluggable plug-ins; covered in Chapter 12.
  • Design pattern: a standard code-level recipe for a recurring problem, e.g. Template Method.
  • Kata: borrowed from martial arts "forms" — a practice exercise built around a given architecture scenario.

Where it sits

Part I, "Foundations", Chapter 5 — the second of four chapters on characteristics: Chapter 4 defines them, this one covers how to find them and how many to keep, Chapter 6 measures and governs them, Chapter 7 scopes them. Real-world counterpart: the "non-functional requirements" slide in a design review, and the first five minutes of a system design interview.

What problem the chapter tackles

Chapter 4 built a dictionary, but nobody shows up holding it. The business says "we're acquiring other companies", "we need to beat competitors to market", "customer satisfaction must go up"; the requirements document lists features and a few numbers; and the requirements that matter most are often not written down at all. Architects and stakeholders speak different languages.

What happens if you don't solve it? Two ways to crash. One is omission: read "1,000 students, 10 hours" literally, design for average load, and watch the system fall over right before the deadline — a skeleton-level mistake that's very expensive to fix later. The other is greed: fearing omissions, you keep everything, list a dozen "all important" characteristics, and end up with a generic architecture that tries to solve every problem — complex, expensive, good at nothing. The chapter offers a method of translation plus convergence.

Core ideas, explained

① Three sources: where characteristics hide

The book groups the sources into three, each harder to mine than the last:

Domain concerns are the goals and worries stakeholders voice; requirement text carries explicit numbers such as expected users; implicit domain knowledge is what no document says but every insider assumes. The book stresses that architects must collaborate with stakeholders in their language, rather than hand them an "-ility" questionnaire.

② Translating business talk into characteristics

The chapter's most practical artifact is a table translating common domain concerns into characteristics (Table 1 below). Mergers and acquisitions mean integrating with other companies' systems, hence interoperability, scalability, adaptability, extensibility; time to market becomes agility, testability, deployability; tight time and budget become simplicity and feasibility.

The key insight: one business phrase usually maps to a set of characteristics, and grabbing only one backfires. The book's example is exactly time to market — architects often hear only "agility", make the system flexible, yet never make it easy to test and deploy: changes are quick, but tests run all day and releases wait a week, so delivery is still slow. Agility itself is a composite that must be broken into concrete parts like testability and deployability.

③ Reading requirements: the distribution behind the number

Explicit numbers still need interpreting. The book's university example: 1,000 students, registration open for 10 hours. Read literally, that's an average load; a domain insider knows students procrastinate until the last moment, so the system must be designed for the crush near the deadline.

Do the arithmetic (illustrative): 1,000 students spread over 10 hours is under 2 per minute; if they all crowd into the last 10 minutes, it's about 100 per minute — roughly 60× higher. A "small total, sharp peak" shape calls for elasticity, not just scalability — the distinction from Chapter 4: one handles long-term growth, the other sudden bursts.

④ Converging: don't rank, pick three

Mining usually yields a dozen candidates. The book's advice: keep the list as short as possible — every supported characteristic adds design complexity, and greed produces the generic monster from Chapter 4. How to converge? The authors admit that asking stakeholders to rank the whole list almost never reaches agreement; everyone thinks their item comes first. What works better: ask stakeholders to pick the three most important from the final list, in any order. Three is few enough to actually drive structural trade-offs, and not ranking sidesteps the endless argument.

⑤ Practice: the Silicon Sandwiches kata

The book practices with architecture katas, devised by Ted Neward: each exercise has a description, users, requirements and additional context; small teams design under a time limit and then critique each other. This chapter's kata is a national sandwich chain going online: users are "thousands, perhaps one day millions"; requirements include telling customers when to pick up their order plus directions (via an external mapping service with traffic data), delivery, mobile access, national and local daily specials, and payment online, in person or on delivery; context: shops are franchised with different owners, the parent company plans to expand overseas soon, and it wants to hire inexpensive labor.

Translated line by line: users heading toward millions → scalability; orders bunched at mealtimes → elasticity; local specials and independently owned franchises → customizability; payment involves money but goes to a third party, so ordinary security hygiene suffices with no special structure; availability and reliability are implicit characteristics present whether written or not. Other items read more like design concerns: mobile access and inexpensive labor call for an easy-to-learn interface, which lands mostly in UI design rather than the skeleton.

⑥ Architecture or design: two ways to customize

This is the chapter's meatiest passage. Is customizability an architecture characteristic? The book offers two routes: a microkernel architecture that turns customization into plug-ins — then it truly shapes the skeleton; or the Template Method design pattern in code, where a parent class fixes the workflow and each store's subclass overrides a few steps — the skeleton doesn't change at all.

The deciding test goes back to Chapter 4's second criterion: if design can handle it, it needn't be promoted to an architecture characteristic. Both routes are "right"; they differ in cost and flexibility. Here the book delivers a famous line: there are no wrong answers in architecture, only expensive ones.

Why it matters / in real systems

"Work backward from business concerns and keep only a few" isn't just the book's methodology — large systems set their goals exactly this way.

Industry evidence · claims from public sources (paraphrased)
  • Amazon · the Dynamo paper (SOSP 2007): the paper starts from a business concern — customers must be able to view and add items to their shopping cart even if disks are failing, network routes are flapping, or data centers are being destroyed by tornados — translates it into an "always writeable" availability requirement, and lets that decide the structure of the whole store. It's the full chain: domain concern → characteristic → structure.DeCandia et al., "Dynamo: Amazon's Highly Available Key-value Store", SOSP 2007
  • Google · Site Reliability Engineering, Ch. 4 "Service Level Objectives" (O'Reilly 2016): advises starting from what users actually care about rather than what is easy to measure, keeping objectives as few as will do the job, and not picking targets based on current performance. Pitfalls and debates

  • Don't wait for a list: stakeholders won't say "-ility"; if the architect doesn't translate, all they get is a feature list.
  • One business phrase ≠ one characteristic: hearing time to market as agility alone misses testability and deployability.
  • Don't design for the average: totals in requirements need domain knowledge to reveal their distribution; spiky shapes need elasticity.
  • Scalability ≠ elasticity: long-term growth vs. sudden bursts, met by different structural means.
  • Not every candidate enters the architecture: if a design pattern solves it, don't pay skeleton cost for it.
  • The ranking fight is a trap: top three, unranked, is what lets the decision actually land.
  • Limits of the method: it rests on conversation and judgment, so different architects reach different lists; the book offers no quantitative selection rule. The translation table also shows its age — later common concerns such as cloud cost and observability aren't in it and must be added per business.

Key takeaways

① Nobody hands you the characteristics; mine them from domain concerns, requirement text and implicit domain knowledge.

② The architect is an interpreter: M&A → interoperability, scalability; time to market → agility, testability, deployability; tight budget → simplicity, feasibility.

③ One business phrase often maps to a set; grabbing just one backfires.

④ Read the distribution behind the number: 1,000 students in 10 hours really means a deadline stampede — elasticity.

⑤ Shorter is better; have stakeholders pick the top three, unranked, to skip the priority fight.

⑥ Architecture katas: description, users, requirements, context — design under time pressure, then critique.

⑦ Don't promote what design can solve: customizability via microkernel or via Template Method.

⑧ No wrong answers, only expensive ones.

⑨ Interview line: "I translated the requirements into these three characteristics and pay structural cost only for them."
