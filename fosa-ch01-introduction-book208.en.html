Fundamentals of Software Architecture · Ch 1 · Mark Richards & Neal Ford · 2020

中文 →

What is this chapter about?

Every app you use — food delivery, online banking, video streaming — has an invisible master plan behind it: how the system is split into parts, how the parts work together, and which choices become very hard to undo once made. The person who draws that plan is the software architect. Chapter 1 tackles two questions that sound simple but nobody answers neatly: what is architecture, and what does an architect actually do all day?

An odd fact: the people in the job can't define it

Doctors and lawyers have agreed-upon job definitions; software architecture does not. The authors' reason is down to earth: the field changes too fast. The approach everyone praised fifteen years ago was forced by expensive servers and expensive software licenses; once cloud and open source made those cheap, the "best" approach flipped. So any talk about architecture has to start with "under what conditions?"

An analogy: planning a building

Think of a system as a building. Architecture covers four things: the building type (a house or an apartment block — the system's overall shape); the hard targets it must hit (survive a strong earthquake, hold so many people — like "how many users can it take, can it recover on its own"); the iron rules ("never remove a load-bearing wall"); and the recommendations ("prefer green materials", which can bend case by case). Saying "it's an apartment block" tells you only a quarter of the story.

An architect is more than "the person who draws boxes"

The book lists eight things expected of an architect: make the key calls, regularly check the health of the whole system, keep up with new technology, make sure people don't quietly break agreed rules, know a broad range of tools, understand the business, work well with people, and handle office politics. The last three don't sound technical, yet they often decide whether a good plan ever ships — your design will be challenged by developers, managers and other teams, and persuading people is part of the job.

What it gives you: two "laws"

First: everything in architecture is a trade-off. Faster usually means more money or more complexity; splitting things finer means more coordination. If you think you've found an option with only upsides, you most likely haven't spotted the downside yet. Second: why you did something matters more than how. Whoever inherits the system years later can see what it looks like, but not why it was chosen — lose the reason, and they're left guessing, maybe even tearing out a key design as "unnecessary". One honest caveat: this is a thinking framework; it won't pick the right option for your specific project.

One line to remember

Architecture has no textbook answer, only trade-offs under specific conditions; the architect's job is to see them, make them, and write down why.

In One Sentence

You think architecture means "we picked microservices over a monolith" — that's only a quarter of it. FoSA Chapter 1 breaks software architecture into four dimensions — structure, architecture characteristics, architecture decisions, design principles — breaks the architect's job into eight expectations, and lays down two laws that run through the whole book: everything in software architecture is a trade-off; why is more important than how.

Glossary

  • Software architecture: the system-wide decisions that shape everything and are hard to change later, such as how modules are split and how they talk.
  • Architecture style: a recurring overall structural template, e.g. layered, microservices, event-driven.
  • Monolith: the whole application packaged and deployed as one unit; the opposite of many independently deployable services.
  • Microservices: a system split into many small services, each deployed on its own and usually owning its own data.
  • "-ilities": quality attributes whose names end in -ility, such as availability, scalability, elasticity (scaling automatically with load).
  • DevOps: practices that make development and operations collaborate, so the people writing code also own deployment, monitoring and release.
  • Agile / Waterfall: two families of development process — short iterations that adjust as they go, versus designing everything up front and executing in phases.

Where It Sits

The book's opening chapter, standing on its own outside any Part. It defines "architecture", sets out the architect's responsibilities, and states the book's two laws; then Part I (Ch2–8) covers architectural thinking, modularity and architecture characteristics, Part II (Ch9–18) takes apart architecture styles one by one, and Part III covers decisions, risk and soft skills. Real-world counterpart: every technology selection review and architecture review meeting.

The Problem This Chapter Tackles

"Architect" is a senior title on many companies' career ladders, yet few people can say how it differs from "senior developer". The lack of consensus has concrete costs: some think architecture is drawing box diagrams, others think it's picking frameworks; the team ends up debating "should we use Kubernetes?" while nobody asks "how much concurrency must we handle, how much downtime can we tolerate?" The authors' diagnosis: the role lacks a definition not because it's vague, but because its ecosystem gets reshuffled every few years — containers, cloud and DevOps arrive, and yesterday's best practice becomes today's anti-pattern. So the chapter doesn't chase a timeless definition; it offers a framework you can decompose and check.

Core Ideas, In Depth

Four dimensions: architecture = structure + characteristics + decisions + principles

The book's definition isn't one sentence but four things stacked together:

  • Structure: which architecture style is used — microservices, layered, microkernel… the layer people talk about most.
  • Architecture characteristics: the success criteria of the system, the pile of -ilities: availability, reliability, testability, scalability, security, elasticity, deployability… They are orthogonal to functionality — features say what to do; characteristics say under which conditions it must still be done well.
  • Architecture decisions: hard rules the system must follow. The book's example: in a layered architecture, "only the business and services layers may access the database; the presentation layer may not." Exceptions go through a formal approval (e.g. an architecture review board).
  • Design principles: guidelines, not rules. Example: "development teams should prefer asynchronous messaging between services to increase performance" — developers may choose otherwise in a specific case.

Why split it this finely? Because an architecture description that only names the structure is incomplete: two microservice systems, one chasing extreme elasticity and one chasing strict auditability, will have entirely different decisions and principles. Writing all four down gives a review something to check against.

Eight expectations: the architect's job list

Titles and duties vary by company, so the authors list eight expectations that hold anywhere:

  1. Make architecture decisions: the point is to guide, not to specify technology. The book's line: deciding "use a reactive-based framework for the front end" is the architect's call; which library is left to the team — unless a specific technology directly affects an architecture characteristic.
  2. Continually analyze the architecture: assess whether an architecture designed three years ago is still healthy for today's business and technology (what the authors call architecture vitality), and watch for structural decay — coupling creeping up, boundaries bypassed again and again.
  3. Keep current with latest trends: architecture decisions are long-lived and hard to reverse; miss the trend and you bet wrong.
  4. Ensure compliance with decisions: e.g. in a layered architecture with closed layers, developers skip layers to move faster — the architect needs a way to keep checking.
  5. Diverse exposure and experience: knowing many technologies, frameworks and platforms matters more than going deep on one (the next chapter develops "breadth over depth").
  6. Business domain knowledge: without it you can't understand stakeholders, nor pick the characteristics that truly matter.
  7. Interpersonal skills: leading, facilitating, explaining. The book quotes Gerald Weinberg: "No matter what they tell you, it's always a people problem."
  8. Understand and navigate politics: almost every architecture decision gets challenged by developers, other architects and business stakeholders; negotiation is the job, not an extra burden.

Where architecture intersects four neighbors

The authors stress that architecture is no longer an isolated "up-front design"; it meshes with four things: engineering practices (Agile lets architecture adjust with each iteration; the book introduces evolutionary architecture and fitness functions — automated checks that continuously measure whether an architecture characteristic still holds); operations / DevOps (operations used to be walled off or even outsourced, so architects had to build scaling and fault tolerance inside the architecture itself, making structures heavy; DevOps turned operations into a dependable partner, which is what made styles like microservices feasible); the development process; and data (code and schemas evolve together, and architecture decisions often get stuck on data).

The two laws

First Law: everything in software architecture is a trade-off. Corollary: if an architect thinks they've found something that isn't a trade-off, more likely they just haven't identified the trade-off yet. This takes "best practice" off its pedestal — the same choice is an asset in one context and a liability in another.

Second Law: why is more important than how. Someone new can read the code and see how the system works, but not why it was chosen or which alternatives were rejected. Lose the reasons and successors either don't dare touch anything or tear out key constraints as redundant. This sets up the architecture decision record (ADR — one page stating context, decision and consequences) in Ch19.

Key Trade-offs and Choices

Chapter 1 doesn't select technologies, but it gives criteria for who owns a call and how hard a rule should be — exactly where day-to-day arguments break out.

Why It Matters / In Real Systems

The two laws are now the common language of architecture reviews: the most useful question in the room isn't "is this design good?" but "what does it trade for what, and why is that worth it here?" The public accounts below are evidence, for and against, that everything is a trade-off and context decides the answer.

Industry evidence · claims the companies made publicly, not the book
  • Google, in the SRE book, explicitly rejects aiming for 100% reliability: cost rises steeply with each extra bit of reliability and it slows feature launches, so reliability should be "good enough" and traded against the pace of innovation — the First Law written into operating policy. Site Reliability Engineering, Ch3 Embracing Risk (Google, full text) ↗
  • Amazon's Dynamo paper states openly that, to stay "always writeable", it deliberately sacrifices consistency under certain failure scenarios and pushes conflict resolution to the application — a design document that spells out what was traded and why. Dynamo paper, SOSP 2007 ↗
  • Segment (counterexample) published a retrospective: after splitting into 140+ microservices, shared-library versions, testing and operations overwhelmed the team, and they merged back into a monolith — the trade-off they hadn't seen came due in full. A. Noonan, "Goodbye Microservices", Segment Engineering, 2018 ↗
  • Shopify, facing a giant Ruby monolith of about 2.8 million lines, didn't follow the microservices trend; it restructured into a modular monolith — the same problem, a different answer in its own context. K. Westerlind, "Deconstructing the Monolith", Shopify Engineering, 2019 ↗

Pitfalls and Debates

  • Equating architecture with architecture style: "we're microservices" answers one of four dimensions; asked "describe your architecture" in an interview, add the key characteristics and a few hard decisions and the answer instantly carries weight.
  • Using "everything is a trade-off" as an excuse not to commit: "it depends" is only the opening; it must be followed by "depends on what, which option we pick in our case, and what it costs".
  • Blurring decisions and principles: enforcing principles as hard rules stiffens teams; writing key decisions as "suggestions" won't stop decay.
  • Recording conclusions without reasons: breaks the Second Law; years later nobody dares to change things, or changes them wrongly.
  • Limitations: the eight expectations read like a job profile and lack measurable criteria; the four-dimension definition is one framework among others — the industry also uses shorter takes like "architecture is the important stuff that's hard to change". The 2025 second edition added another law: most architecture decisions aren't binary but sit somewhere on a spectrum between extremes.

Key Takeaways

① Software architecture has no standard definition because its ecosystem reshuffles every few years; any architecture discussion starts with context.

② Architecture = structure + architecture characteristics + architecture decisions + design principles; naming the style is only a quarter.

③ Architecture characteristics (-ilities) are orthogonal to features: features say what, characteristics say under which conditions it must still work well.

④ Decisions are hard rules (exceptions need approval), principles are guidance (may bend); separate them and every rule gets the right hardness.

⑤ Eight expectations: decide, keep analyzing, track trends, ensure compliance, broad exposure, business knowledge, people skills, politics — the last three decide whether plans ship.

⑥ Decisions should guide, not specify technology, unless a specific technology directly secures a key characteristic.

⑦ Architecture meshes with engineering practices, DevOps, process and data; fitness functions make characteristics continuously, automatically checkable.

⑧ First Law: everything is a trade-off — no downside found means you haven't looked hard enough. Second Law: why beats how, so keep the reasons (ADRs).
