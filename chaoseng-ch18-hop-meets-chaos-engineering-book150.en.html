What is this chapter about?

The previous chapters were about how to break systems on purpose. This one changes the angle: the most important part of any system, and the part most often wrongly blamed, is the people. When something goes down, our first instinct is "who slipped up?" This chapter borrows decades of experience from factories, power plants and other safety-critical industries to show that this is the wrong question.

A strange fact first

You would think that punishing the person who made the mistake, then retraining everyone, would mean fewer accidents. Industrial safety has found the opposite: the harder you punish, the more "sudden" your accidents become. People don't learn "don't make mistakes" — they learn "don't mention the small ones." The small problems get tucked away in a drawer until they pile up into one big problem nobody can hide.

Why the old way is hard

  • "Human error" is the cheapest conclusion. Find the person who pressed the wrong button and the investigation can close.
  • But almost anyone could have pressed that button. Working late at night, two buttons that look identical, a manual that doesn't match how the job is really done — that setting is what actually pushed them.
  • Punish the person and the setting stays the same. The next person steps into the same spot and falls into the same hole.

The core intuition

  • Treat mistakes like weather, not like a character flaw. People will make mistakes the way it will rain. The smart move is not to ban rain but to carry an umbrella and dig drains, so a mistake doesn't turn into a disaster.
  • After something goes wrong, ask "why did that seem like the right thing to do at the time?" instead of "who was wrong?" A doctor doesn't scold a patient for having a fever; they look for the cause.
  • Chaos engineering is the fire-drill version of this idea. Instead of waiting for trouble, you cause small, controlled failures and see whether the system and the people on call can catch them. When a drill uncovers a weakness, the person who found it gets thanked, not blamed.
  • How leaders react decides whether people keep telling the truth. If the first reply to bad news is "thank you for telling me," people keep bringing small problems forward.

What it gives you / how to choose

Run the same drill with the goal "see who messes up," and people get nervous and put on a show. Run it with the goal "see where the system can't cope," and they show you the real weak spots. So the chapter's advice: when you design failure drills, treat people as the ones holding the system together, not as suspects to catch. One honest cost: blameless does not mean nobody is responsible, and the approach depends on trust built up over a long time — a single round of "settling scores" afterwards can wipe it out overnight.

One line to remember

People making mistakes is normal; fix the setting that lets a mistake become a disaster — chaos engineering rehearses exactly that.

One line

You think an incident review ends when you find "the person who made the mistake" — in fact that is where it should start. This chapter brings the five principles of Human and Organizational Performance (HOP) from industrial safety into software — error is normal, blame fixes nothing, context drives behavior, learning and improving is vital, intentional response matters — and argues that chaos engineering is itself a way of putting those principles into practice: it causes failure deliberately and under control, studies how the system (people included) copes under stress, and changes the setting instead of the person.

Glossary

  • Human error: an action that differs from what was intended, such as a mistyped parameter or a skipped step. HOP treats it as an outcome, usually not a root cause.
  • Root cause analysis: the traditional search for "the one cause" after an incident; complex systems rarely have one, only several contributing factors.
  • Postmortem: a written review after an incident or experiment covering what happened, why, and what changes next.
  • Blameless: a review whose goal is not to punish individuals but to find the conditions that let an error happen and grow.
  • Defenses / controls: designs that stop an error from becoming an accident, such as confirmations, permission limits, automatic rollback, alerts.
  • Psychological safety: team members' belief that raising a mistake, bad news or a question will not get them punished or humiliated.
  • Game day: a scheduled session where a team injects failures under controlled conditions and practises the response for real; a common form of chaos engineering.

What problem this chapter solves

Picture 3 a.m.: an on-call engineer follows the runbook to remove some servers, types one extra character, and takes out 200 machines instead of 2. The service is down for hours. A traditional review concludes quickly — "human error; more training, more approvals." Case closed. But the command that allows removing 200 machines at once, the rota that leaves one person alone at night, and the runbook that no longer matches real practice are all still there.

Industrial safety has watched the same ending play out again and again in chemical plants, utilities and factories: attribute the accident to an individual, close with discipline, and the next person in the same setting makes the same mistake. Worse, discipline stops the front line from reporting near misses and small problems, so the safety numbers management sees keep getting prettier — until a major accident shatters the illusion.

Software teams face the same problem, only faster: hundreds of changes a day, systems too complex for anyone to hold in their head. The chapter asks: how should we think about the people in a system so that incidents actually become less severe — and how does that change the way we design and review chaos experiments?

The core ideas, in depth

1. What HOP is: from fixing people to fixing systems

Human and Organizational Performance (HOP) is a philosophy that grew out of industrial safety, strongly influenced by safety thinkers such as James Reason, Sidney Dekker and Todd Conklin. It is not a toolkit but a shift in how you look at accidents:

  • Old view: the system is safe by design; accidents come from people who break rules or are careless. The fix is more rules, more training, harsher punishment.
  • New view: people are trying to do good work in a system full of time pressure, conflicting goals and incomplete information; errors are symptoms of deeper problems surfacing. The fix is to change the context and build the capacity to fail safely.

2. The five principles, one by one

① Error is normal. Even well-trained people make mistakes; it is a property of being human, not a matter of attitude. So the goal is not "zero errors" but a system that still fails safely when an error happens: limit the impact of any single action, catch errors early, make them quick to undo. Chaos engineering starts from exactly the same premise — failure will come, so verify in advance that the system can absorb it rather than hoping.

② Blame fixes nothing. Blame feels like resolution, but it only puts a different person at the edge of the same hole. The bigger cost is information: once admitting a mistake invites punishment, people hide near misses and the organization loses its cheapest early-warning signal. That is why the book insists on blameless reviews in several places.

③ Context drives behavior. People's choices are shaped by time pressure, tool design, incentives and process. To change behavior, change the context: rather than asking people to "be careful with parameters," make the tool refuse to remove more than 5% of capacity in one go. A useful pair of terms here: work-as-imagined, the way manuals and procedures describe the job; and work-as-done, the way the front line actually gets it done under real constraints. The gap between the two is where risk hides.

④ Learning and improving is vital. Organizations need to learn from the front line actively and continuously, not only after something breaks. One tool is the learning team: bring in the people who actually do the work and ask, without judgment, "how does this normally get done, where is it awkward, where does it rely on luck?"

⑤ Intentional response matters. The first sentence a manager says on hearing bad news decides whether anyone tells the truth next time. Responses should be deliberately designed: thank the reporter first, ask about context first, then talk about improvement.

3. HOP meets chaos engineering: how experiment design changes

The chapter's central argument is that chaos engineering and HOP point at the same thing — accept that failure is inevitable, and actively learn how the system fails before it does harm. For years HOP has studied systems "indirectly" on the industrial floor through interviews and learning teams; chaos engineering offers a way to observe directly, and under control, how a system (people included) responds to failure. Mapping the five principles onto experiments:

  • Put people inside the system boundary. A fault-injection hypothesis is not only "the service fails over automatically" but also "the on-call engineer understands the alert and takes the right action within 15 minutes." A game day tests people and tooling together.
  • Experiments look for gaps, not culprits. If someone strays from the runbook during a drill, the right question is "why didn't the runbook work at that moment?" — which often exposes the gap between work-as-imagined and work-as-done.
  • Make the context realistic. Drills run only in daytime with senior engineers in the room tell you nothing about one person alone on call at 3 a.m.; but raise realism step by step with the blast radius, not with a surprise attack on day one.
  • Review blamelessly. Weaknesses an experiment exposes must count as gains, not faults — or next time people will pick "safe" scenarios and go through the motions.
  • Leadership's response is part of the experiment. How the organization reacts after a big gap is found decides whether chaos engineering can last.

Key trade-offs and choices

The central trade-off from a HOP point of view: once an incident or experiment exposes a problem, do you spend your improvement effort on people or on context?

Four typical responses after something goes wrong

ResponseHow fastWhat actually changesCost / side effects
Discipline the personInstant "case closed"Almost nothing; context unchangedSuppresses reporting; near misses vanish from reports; same incident recurs
Retrain + add rulesFastA little; relies on people remembering under pressureProcedures thicken; work-as-done drifts further from the manual

Rule of thumb: if an improvement only works when people never make mistakes, it is not an improvement. Prefer measures that still work when someone errs, and use chaos experiments to prove that they really do.

Why it matters / real systems

Blameless postmortems are now standard in SRE teams, but many teams copied the template without the HOP logic behind it: their action items still say "more training" or "operators must be careful." The chapter's test: every action item should change the context, not ask people to be more careful. When an interview or architecture review asks "this outage was human error — how do you prevent it?", the strong answer points the same way: limit the blast radius of any single action, add undo and rate limits, make errors visible, and verify all of it with drills.

Industry evidence

  • Google: blamelessness written into SRE practice. Chapter 15 of Site Reliability Engineering, "Postmortem Culture: Learning from Failure," says a truly blameless postmortem must focus on identifying the contributing causes of an incident without indicting any individual or team, and that "you can't 'fix' people, but you can fix systems and processes" to better support people making the right choices. Source (text): Google, Site Reliability Engineering, Ch 15, O'Reilly 2016
  • Etsy: blameless postmortems and a just culture. On Etsy's engineering blog, John Allspaw argued that only when engineers can give a detailed account of what they did and why, without fear of punishment, can an organization truly understand how an incident happened; punishment just makes people stop giving those details. Source (text): John Allspaw, "Blameless PostMortems and a Just Culture," Etsy Code as Craft, 2012
  • Amazon: the S3 outage review targeted the tool, not the person (2017). The February 2017 S3 disruption in us-east-1 lasted about four hours; it began when an authorized engineer, following an established playbook, entered one input of a command incorrectly and removed far more servers than intended. AWS's public summary contains no disciplinary narrative; instead it announced changes to the tool: remove capacity more slowly, and add safeguards that prevent taking any subsystem below its minimum required capacity. A textbook "context drives behavior" response. Source (text): AWS, "Summary of the Amazon S3 Service Disruption in the Northern Virginia (US-EAST-1) Region," 2017

Pitfalls and debates

  • "Blameless = nobody is accountable." Blameless targets punishment, not accountability. People still explain what they did and help with the fix; what is removed is being punished for telling the truth. Malice or reckless disregard is another matter — that distinction is exactly what a just culture draws.
  • "Human error was the root cause." Writing it down as the root cause means stopping the investigation exactly where it should go deeper. Complex systems usually have several contributing factors.
  • "Drills are an exam for on-call engineers." Once drill results feed into performance reviews, people choose safe scenarios and hide real practice, and the experiments lose their point.
  • "One more manual approval makes it safe." Approvers usually have less context than the person doing the work; more approvals become a formality. Put defenses in the tooling wherever possible.
  • The move from factory to software has limits. HOP's experience comes from physical-harm settings with a rhythm of weeks and months; software changes happen in minutes, so interview-heavy methods like learning teams need to be made lighter. The chapter is also mostly principles and ideas with little quantitative evidence, and how to measure HOP's effect remains an open discussion in safety circles.

Key takeaways

① Human error is a symptom, not a root cause; a report that stops at "who got it wrong" stops at the tip of the iceberg.

② The five HOP principles: error is normal, blame fixes nothing, context drives behavior, learning and improving is vital, intentional response matters.

③ The goal is not zero errors but a system that still fails safely when people err.

④ Blame suppresses reporting and costs the organization its cheapest early warning; the learning loop surfaces weak spots earlier.

⑤ Watch the gap between work-as-imagined and work-as-done — that is where risk hides.

⑥ Chaos engineering is HOP made experimental: put on-call people into the steady-state hypothesis, and hunt for gaps, not culprits.

⑦ Rule of thumb: a measure that works only if people never err is not an improvement; change tools and context first.

⑧ Evidence: Google SRE blameless postmortems, Etsy's just culture, AWS's 2017 S3 review fixing the tool rather than punishing the person.

⑨ Blameless is not the same as unaccountable; a leader's first reaction to bad news decides whether the practice lasts.
