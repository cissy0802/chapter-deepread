What is this chapter about?

The previous three chapters were all about how to push failure into production. This one asks the question everybody steps around: production is full of real people.

You are not shaking a pile of machines. You are shaking hundreds of millions of people, some of whom are submitting a job application or talking to a recruiter right now. LinkedIn's answer is not "then don't do it in production." It is: make "who gets affected" a field you fill in.

An analogy: how a new drug is tested

No drug goes straight from the lab to everyone. First a handful of people, checked for safety; then dozens, then hundreds. And every phase is written down in advance with a stopping rule — "if this happens, halt immediately" — plus someone whose only job is watching that line.

Running failure experiments on real people follows the same three rules: start with a tiny group, widen one rung at a time, and define the automatic stop before you start. Only one thing differs: in software, "stopping the dose" takes seconds, so you can afford to be a little bolder than medicine.

Why the old way is hard

The default move is to kill a random machine. The problem with it isn't that it's risky — it's that you don't get to choose who suffers.

Who happens to be on that machine depends on how traffic was being split at that instant. It might be all idle browsing, or it might include the one person pressing "Submit application." "Only one request in a thousand" sounds small, but that one in a thousand isn't a number — it's a specific person's specific action.

And there is a sneakier layer. If the system habitually sends the same person to the same machine, then "one request in a thousand fails" really means "one person in a thousand has a broken session end to end." One person failing a hundred times and a hundred people failing once each are the same number on a dashboard and two different things in real life — the first one uninstalls the app.

The core intuition: three things

  • Attach the failure to one request. Instead of killing a machine, hand a single request a note: "when you reach that service, pretend it's broken." The note travels downstream with that request only. Nobody else notices anything.
  • Shake yourself first. Once you can target a single request, the cheapest subject is the engineer: open your own page, make one downstream "break," and see whether the page still works. The blast radius is one consenting person, at nearly zero cost.
  • If it hurts, stop by itself. Once real users are involved, "are we hurting anyone?" can't depend on a human staring at a screen. Pick a few metrics people actually feel (say, the share of messages that get sent), and pull the experiment the moment one drifts — the guardrail has to be faster than the harm.

What it buys you / how to choose

The question changes from "should we take this risk?" to "how much of it?" Most defects surface at the shake-yourself rung and never touch a real user; when you genuinely need real traffic, you climb one rung at a time and can always pull back.

One honest cost: a per-request failure is simulated — it only makes that one request believe the downstream is broken. A real outage lands on every caller at once, and that kind of global congestion only shows up at scale.

Remember one thing

Measure the blast radius of production chaos in people: attach the failure to a single request and let it propagate, shake yourself first and then widen cohort by cohort, and write the automatic stop condition before you begin — an experiment with no automatic abort has no business running on real people.

In one sentence

You think "minimize the blast radius" means making the experiment smaller — kill fewer machines, route less traffic. This chapter changes the unit: blast radius is measured in people, not machines. LinkedIn has hundreds of millions of members, and one of those sessions may be an interview invitation; so the real defect in "kill a machine" is not that it is risky but that you have no say in who gets hurt. The engineering answer is to push injection granularity down to a single request: the failure instruction propagates down the call graph with the request (LinkedOut), turning the affected set from "whoever happened to be routed there" into an explicit input of the experiment — then trade information up a ladder (your own session → employees → a targeted cohort → cluster level), with every rung wired to an abort that runs faster than the harm.

Glossary

  • Member — LinkedIn's word for a user. The starting point of every constraint here: the cost of an experiment ultimately lands on one specific person's one specific action.
  • Blast radius — the worst-case reach of an experiment. This chapter insists on counting it in people and situations, not machines or traffic percentages.
  • Request-level failure injection — the failure applies only to the request that carries the marker; every other request in flight proceeds normally.
  • Call graph — the fan-out of backend calls behind one user request; a single page often expands into dozens of downstreams.
  • Context propagation — carrying a small piece of data (here: "what failure to inject") along the call graph with the request. LinkedIn's implementation is called Invocation Context (IC).
  • Rest.li — LinkedIn's open-source REST framework; the injection point is implemented as one of its request filters.
  • LiX — LinkedIn's A/B experimentation and ramp platform, used to target an experiment at a chosen population and ramp by percentage.
  • Graceful degradation — when a downstream breaks, the page loses a module but the main function still works, instead of erroring out entirely.
  • Automatic abort — stopping the experiment the instant a predefined metric crosses its line, with no human in the path.
  • Sticky routing — the same user's requests always land on the same host or shard. It converts "1% of requests affected" into "1% of people affected throughout."

The problem this chapter attacks

Start with the constraint. Around the time this book was written, LinkedIn's publicly stated membership was in the 600–700 million range, and what makes the product unusual is how unevenly weighted a single session is: in the same minute, somebody is idly scrolling the feed and somebody else is pressing "Submit application" or answering a recruiter. A hiccup for the first costs nothing; one failure for the second may genuinely change something.

Now look at the default move. Killing 1 instance out of 1,000 affects roughly 0.1% of requests. Counted in machines, that is "small." Counted in people, two more questions are owed: who are those 0.1%, and what were they doing? Killing an instance does not answer either — the answer is decided by whatever the load balancer was doing that second.

Worse is the amplification sticky routing performs. If routing hashes on member, "1% of requests" is really "100% of the requests of 1% of members." One person failing a hundred times and a hundred people failing once each are the same number on an availability dashboard, and one is tolerable while the other is an incident.

What happens if you don't solve it? Two degenerations: you never dare — the tool and the permissions exist but nobody presses the button, so chaos stays in a staging environment that has neither real load nor real dependencies; or you force it and get shut down — one experiment becomes a real incident and chaos is unmentionable for six months. The restraint in this chapter exists precisely to keep the long-term license to experiment.

So the question is: how do you turn "who gets affected" from a bet into a parameter?

The core content, properly

1. Blast radius is measured in people, not machines

The one thing to take away is a change of units: stop asking "how many hosts, what percentage," and ask how many people, which people, what were they doing, for how long, and is it reversible.

The last three get dropped most often. For how long decides whether a person hits one retry or five minutes of blank screen. Reversible or not splits "a module is missing from the page" from "the application submission was lost, the message half-sent" — and no percentage is small enough to justify experimenting on the second kind. That is also why shaking a read path and shaking a write path are not the same class of risk here.

Once the units change, experiment design points somewhere else: the goal is not to shrink the scale but to make the affected set something you can write down. Killing instances cannot do that — its affected set is only knowable at runtime.

2. The mechanism: the failure instruction travels with the request

LinkedIn's framework is LinkedOut, and one sentence places it: the injection point is a request filter in the Rest.li stack, and whether this hop should fail is decided by context handed down with the request (the Invocation Context).

It injects three modes: error (throw an exception, mimicking an unavailable resource), delay (add latency before forwarding) and timeout (expire after a set period). Triggering comes in two forms: target a population through LiX, or put the instruction in a cookie — which makes the granularity as fine as "this one page load of mine."

Why this is not easy: a page fans out to dozens of downstreams, and the failure must land only on "this member's this request on its hop into Service X" — not leaking to other members, not leaking to Service X's other callers. That requires the framework to carry the context intact on every hop. Flip it around and you have the implicit cost: paths that never joined the propagation scheme cannot be injected (third-party SDKs, offline and asynchronous jobs, consumers behind a message queue).

What you buy is an order-of-magnitude shift in blast radius: from "every in-flight request of every member on that host for the length of the restart window" down to "one person's one click."

3. The ramp ladder: shake yourself, then a small crowd

Once injection is precise to one request, the cheapest subject is the engineer. LinkedOut ships a browser extension for exactly that: an engineer flips a switch in their own session, makes one downstream on the page "break," and looks at whether the page still works. Blast radius: one consenting person.

One rung up is automation: walk every downstream of a page, break each in turn, and check for graceful degradation. These experiments can run fully automatically and often because the only account affected is the one that launched them — their marginal harm is zero, so they can be used as tests.

The whole point of the ladder is to squeeze the most defects out of the cheapest rung: missing timeouts, missing fallbacks, a whole page going blank when one module dies — all of that surfaces on rung one, with no member involved. What actually justifies a ramp is only the class of problem that needs real traffic to be visible.

And the honest ceiling: some failures only exist at scale — connection-pool saturation, retry storms, mass cache expiry, a downstream hammered by every caller at once. Single-request injection cannot see them; those need a ramp or a cluster-level experiment.

4. Automatic abort: the guardrail must outrun the harm

Once real members are in scope, "are we hurting anyone?" cannot hang on a human watching a screen. Three points:

  • Pick metrics members can feel: message send success, page render success, application submit success — not CPU and memory. "CPU is fine while users are failing" is exactly the blind spot this chapter fears.
  • The verdict must be visible within the rung's own time scale. If harm takes ten minutes to show in a metric, the rung must run a full ten minutes before you can judge — and harm accumulates throughout. The conclusion is not "tolerate ten minutes," it is find a faster metric, or shrink this rung's population.
  • The abort must fire by itself, in seconds. The injection switch has to be killable without a deploy or a restart; a human approval workflow is not an abort mechanism.

A criterion you can take straight to a review: an experiment with no automatic abort condition should not run on real people. That is not a moral posture, it is cost arithmetic — the latency of "human notices plus human responds," multiplied by the ramped population, is the harm you prepay for the experiment.

Concretely:

  • Just starting, afraid to press the button: don't start by killing machines. Get injection down to request level, then give engineers a "shake me" entry point — zero harm, and it catches most of the missing timeouts and missing fallbacks.
  • Request-level ability in place, ready to ramp: build the automatic abort first, then ramp — never the other order. Pick the percentage not by what "looks small" but by how fast the harm becomes visible in a metric.
  • Systems with sticky routing: target by member rather than hitting requests at random, and cap how long one member can be hit — otherwise "1% of requests" becomes "1% of people, fully broken."
  • Money and irreversible writes: shake read paths and retryable writes only; for irreversible writes, take Ch8's Capital One route — rehearse outside production, gather the evidence, then talk about production.

Why it matters / in real systems

Three sentences that can change a design review. One: blast radius is measured in people, not machines. Two: "only 1% of traffic" means "1% of people, continuously" under sticky routing. Three: an experiment with no automatic abort condition has no business running on real people.

Asked in an interview how you do chaos engineering in production: "we kill random machines at night" is one level; "injection is request-level, experiments default to the initiator's own session, ramps go through a targeted cohort wired to an automatic abort on member-visible metrics" is another — the second shows you know the hard part is who gets affected, not whether you can inject.

Pitfalls and disputes

  • Counting blast radius in machines or traffic percent. The same 0.1% landing on feed scrollers and landing on people submitting applications are two different events.
  • Assuming "random" means "fair." Random injection plus sticky routing means a small group absorbs all of the harm.
  • Ramping first, guardrails later. Automatic abort is not an optimization; it is the precondition for the ramp.
  • Treating "shake yourself" as the whole program. It is cheap and productive, but structurally blind to pool saturation and retry storms, which only appear at scale.
  • Treating ethics as a PR problem. This chapter treats it as a design constraint with concrete criteria: the affected set must be enumerable, the harm reversible, and every experiment explainable to a non-engineer.
  • Dispute: request-level injection has limited fidelity. It injects "this one call failed," not "the service is actually down"; a real outage hits every caller at once and triggers retry storms and pool saturation. So it is the everyday, high-frequency rung and does not replace cluster-level experiments — the two divide labour rather than substitute for each other.
  • Period limits and what came after. LinkedOut rests on in-house Rest.li, LiX and IC, which is a high bar to build; today the same mechanism is largely something you install — service meshes such as Istio natively support HTTP-level abort and delay fault injection, OpenTelemetry baggage provides cross-service context propagation, and feature-flag platforms provide targeted ramps. What has not changed is the unit of measure: ask who gets affected first.

Takeaways

1. The thesis: blast radius is measured in people, not machines. The defect in "kill one machine" is not the risk but that you have no say in who gets hurt.

2. Change the units: not how many hosts or percentage points, but how many people, which ones, doing what, for how long, and is it reversible — the last two are the ones routinely dropped.

3. The mechanism: the failure instruction propagates down the call graph. LinkedOut = a Rest.li request filter plus Invocation Context, three modes error / delay / timeout, triggered by LiX targeting or by a cookie precise to one page load.

4. Its engineering value reduces to one line: it turns the affected set from "whoever happened to be routed there" into an explicit input of the experiment.

5. The ladder: your own session (1 person) → employees (10²–10³) → targeted member cohort (10⁴+) → cluster level (10⁶+). Most defects die on the first rung, which is what makes production work defensible.

6. Sticky routing amplifies "1% of requests affected" into "1% of people affected throughout" — one number on the dashboard, two different realities. Target by member and cap the exposure window.

7. Three rules for automatic abort: member-visible business metrics, harm visible within the rung's time scale, firing automatically within seconds. Criterion: no automatic abort condition, no running on real people.

8. The honest ceiling: request-level injection is blind to global effects (pool saturation, retry storms); it divides labour with cluster-level experiments rather than replacing them. And what restraint really buys is the standing permission to keep experimenting at all.
