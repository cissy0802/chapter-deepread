Introduction and Overview: the Seven-Step ML Design Framework

Machine Learning System Design Interview · Ch 1 · Ali Aminian & Alex Xu · 2023

中文 →

What is this chapter about?

When you scroll a short-video app, it somehow keeps queuing up the next clip you want. Your inbox quietly keeps spam out. Behind both sits a system that learns: nobody wrote the rules one by one — it worked them out from a mountain of examples. This chapter doesn't teach you to build one particular system of that kind. It hands you an order of operations: what to think about first, and where people fall over.

A strange fact first

Most people assume the hard part is "picking a clever model." Inside a real company, the model is usually a small piece of the whole thing. Wrapped around it: where the data comes from, how it gets cleaned and stored, how it reaches the model in time, and how anyone notices when the thing quietly gets dumber. Engineers spend most of their hours on that ring, not on the model.

Why the old world was hard

Ordinary software is "you set the rules": spend 200, save 30, written into the code, and when it's wrong you can trace it line by line. A learning system is "it finds the pattern in the examples itself": you hand it a million past records and it distills a way of judging that nobody can read back. Which leads to something genuinely awkward — when it goes bad, nothing throws an error. No server is down, the API still returns, the recommendations just get worse and worse. The usual debugging instincts stop working here.

What the seven steps actually do

The chapter splits building such a system into seven steps, each answering a very concrete question:

  • 1. Ask what's really wanted. "Get people more engaged" is not something a machine can chase. It has to become a scorable question first — say, "guess whether this person will finish this video."
  • 2. Turn it into a judgment call. What goes in, what comes out — pin both down word by word. Same request, different definitions, and you've built two entirely different systems.
  • 3. Find the rice, then wash it. Where the data comes from, how to fill the gaps, how to turn messy raw records into something the model can eat. This is the slow part.
  • 4. Build the dumbest version first. "Just show whatever is trending" — not to ship, but as a ruler: every fancier model has to beat the ruler before it counts.
  • 5. Hold a closed-door exam on records it has never seen. 6. Then try it on a slice of real people — because doing well behind closed doors doesn't mean it helps anyone, and that gap is the most famous trap in the field.
  • 7. Shipping is the beginning. Someone has to keep watching: the world shifts, tastes shift, and it slowly falls behind.

The crucial bit: these seven steps aren't a line, they're a loop. What step seven catches sends you back to step three to redo the data and retrain.

What this costs

It's slow: just pinning down the requirement and standing up the data pipeline often takes several times longer than training the model — and almost none of that work is visible from outside.

Remember one thing

Building a system that learns is never mainly about picking the model. It's about translating a fuzzy business wish into a scorable question, getting the data where it needs to be, and then proving on real people that it actually helps — and after launch it degrades on its own, so someone has to keep watching.

In one sentence

The framing chapter for the whole book: designing a system with a model in it splits into seven steps — clarify requirements, frame it as an ML task, prepare data, develop the model, evaluate, deploy and serve, monitor. The core insight is that the model is a small piece of the system: what decides success sits on either side of it — upstream, "how does a business objective become an ML objective"; downstream, "how do you prove online that it helps, and how do you notice when it rots."

Glossary

  • Machine learning: instead of hard-coding rules, let a program fit a mapping from many labelled examples; the fitted parameters are the model.
  • Supervised learning: training data carries the right answer (a label). Categorical output is classification, continuous output is regression.
  • Feature: an input signal fed to the model, e.g. "videos watched in the last 7 days"; turning raw data into features is feature engineering.
  • Embedding: representing a discrete object (user, video, word) as a vector of floats, so that "similar" becomes "close in vector space."
  • Offline evaluation: scoring the model on held-out historical data — how accurate the model itself is.
  • Online evaluation: putting the model on real traffic and watching business metrics (click-through, watch time, orders) move or not.
  • A/B test: split users randomly into two groups on the old and new systems, and compare business metrics statistically.
  • Data distribution shift: live data drifts away from the distribution the model was trained on, so the model quietly degrades.

Where it sits

This is the only chapter of Part I in Aminian & Xu's Machine Learning System Design Interview (2023), and the template every one of the ten case chapters (Ch 2–11) follows. It inherits the four-step interview method of Xu's earlier two volumes but changes the terrain: those books deal with deterministic systems (what you write is what you read), this one with statistical systems (the output is a probability; correctness only holds in aggregate). In the wild it maps to recommendation, search ranking, ads, risk scoring and content moderation.

The problem this chapter solves

ML design questions are even more open-ended than pure system design questions, and there is one dominant failure mode: opening with the model — "I'd use a Transformer two-tower retriever." That may be technically fine while skipping everything that actually decides the outcome: what the business objective is, how positives and negatives are defined, where the features come from, whether an offline win moves anything online, and how you'd know three months later that it still works.

The deeper pain: ML systems fail silently. A broken service throws 5xx, times out, pages someone. A drifting ranker does none of that — latency normal, error rate zero, recommendation quality sliding for weeks until it shows up in a business review. Skip this and you build a system that runs beautifully while quietly losing money. The seven-step framework exists to nail down everything that lives outside the model.

The core content, explained

Step 1 · Clarify requirements: narrow an open question into a designable one

In "design a video recommendation system," nearly every word is undefined. Ask about: the business objective (watch time or new-user retention? the optima differ), which features the system supports (can users say "not interested"?), data (which sources, is anything labelled, can behaviour serve as implicit labels), constraints and scale (compute budget, how many users, how much content, requests per day), and performance requirements (inference latency ceiling, freshness).

The output isn't a design, it's a list that pins the boundaries down. One order of magnitude changes everything: a candidate pool of 10 thousand versus 1 billion decides whether you need approximate nearest-neighbour retrieval; a 500 ms versus 30 ms budget decides how big the model can be.

Step 2 · Frame it as an ML task: settle this and everything downstream is settled

The most valuable step in the chapter and the one most often skipped. Three parts.

(a) Define the ML objective. A business objective is human language; an ML objective must be a quantity a model can optimise. The book puts the two side by side: a video platform's "increase user engagement" → "maximise time users spend watching"; harmful content detection's "make the platform safer" → "accurately predict whether a piece of content is harmful"; friend recommendation's "help users grow their network faster" → "maximise the number of connections formed." The sloppier the translation, the further the system drifts — translate "engagement" bluntly into "maximise click-through" and the model learns to promote clickbait.

(b) Specify input and output. Same requirement, different definitions, two different systems. The book's cleanest example: harmful content detection can be one multi-label model (one piece of content in, probabilities for violence / nudity / hate speech out) or one binary model per category. The former shares representations and saves compute, but adding a category means retraining everything; the latter lets each category iterate and tune its own threshold, at the cost of multiplying training and serving by the number of categories.

(c) Choose the ML category. Supervised / unsupervised / reinforcement; within supervised, classification versus regression. The most commonly misjudged family is ranking: recommendation, search and feeds look like classification, but what you actually need is a sortable score, so they are usually framed as learning to rank or representation learning — encode users and items as embeddings and use vector similarity as the score, which is what lets a billion-item candidate pool be pre-filtered by approximate nearest-neighbour search.

Step 3 · Data preparation: where the work actually is

Two halves. Data engineering: sources (user-generated, system-generated, third-party), storage choices, ETL pipelines, and data types — structured (numerical, categorical) versus unstructured (text, images, audio, video). Feature engineering: turning raw data into something a model can consume, with four routine moves —

  • Missing values: delete or impute (mean / median / default). Imputation introduces bias, deletion loses information; no free lunch.
  • Feature scaling: normalisation (squash into [0,1]), standardisation (zero mean, unit variance), log transform (for long-tailed features like "follower count" spanning several orders of magnitude).
  • Discretisation / bucketing: cut continuous values into ranges (age bands), trading a little information for robustness to outliers.
  • Categorical encoding: integer encoding (risks implying a false order), one-hot (dimensionality explodes with many categories), or learned embeddings — the standard answer for high-cardinality fields like "video ID," compressing millions of IDs into tens of dimensions.

The hard part isn't the algorithm, it's train/serve consistency: a feature computed in SQL offline and by different code online only needs to disagree slightly (misaligned time window, different missing-value fill) for online quality to drop inexplicably — training-serving skew.

Step 4 · Model development: baseline first, complexity later

Model selection weighs more than accuracy: training data volume, number of features, training speed, hyperparameter count, compute needs, interpretability. The book's path is plain — start from a simple baseline: get the whole chain working with heuristics or logistic regression, then swap in more complex models, each compared against the baseline. The reason: a dumb model that has a working data pipeline, evaluation and release path beats a better model that only exists in a notebook.

Model training deals with constructing the dataset (defining features and labels, sampling, train/validation/test splits, handling class imbalance — in CTR settings positives are often a fraction of a percent, addressed by resampling or loss weighting), choosing a loss function, training from scratch versus fine-tuning a pretrained model, and at scale distributed training (data parallelism: one model copy per device, each on a different batch; model parallelism: the model itself split across devices).

Step 5 · Evaluation: offline gains don't imply online gains

Offline evaluation scores held-out history, with metrics by task type: classification uses precision / recall, F1, ROC-AUC and PR-AUC; regression uses MSE / MAE / RMSE; ranking uses precision@k, MRR, nDCG (@k meaning "only the top k results" — nobody scrolls to the 50th); generation uses BLEU / ROUGE / FID.

Online evaluation measures something else entirely: business metrics — click-through, watch time, conversion, retention, revenue. There is no stable correspondence between the two, which is the counter-intuitive lesson of the chapter: offline AUC climbs two points, you ship it, and the business metric doesn't budge or goes backwards. Usually because the offline data carries the incumbent system's exposure bias (you only have feedback on what was already recommended; anything never shown has no label), or because the ML objective was never aligned with the business objective.

Steps 6–7 · Deploy, serve, monitor: launch is the start

Deployment settles three things: where it runs (cloud or on-device), how to shrink it (distillation: a small model learns the large one's outputs; pruning: drop low-contribution parameters; quantisation: 32-bit floats down to 8-bit integers), and how to roll it out (shadow, A/B, canary, interleaving, bandits — see the next section), plus whether the prediction pipeline is batch or online.

Monitoring answers one question: why does a model degrade in production? The leading cause is data distribution shift — behaviour changed, the content ecosystem changed, an upstream source quietly redefined a field. So you watch two families: operational metrics (latency, throughput, utilisation, uptime) and ML-specific metrics (drift in input feature and output prediction distributions, accuracy over time where ground truth flows back, and which model version is actually live).

Key trade-offs

Table 1 · Cloud deployment vs on-device deployment

Table 3 · How to test a new model in production

MethodHow it worksWhat you get / what it costs
ShadowBoth models take the same traffic; the new model's output never reaches users, it's only logged and comparedZero user risk; costs double inference, and yields no business metrics (nobody reacts to its output)
A/B testRandomly split users between old and new, compare business metricsThe only method giving a causal answer; needs enough samples for significance — a small change may need one to two weeks
CanarySend 1% of traffic to the new model, widen if nothing breaksContained blast radius, fast rollback; too noisy at small traffic to conclude anything
InterleavingMix both rankings into one list and see which source the user clicksFor ranking, an order of magnitude fewer samples than A/B (between-user variance is cancelled); only applies to ranking
BanditShift traffic share between versions based on live performanceEarns while it tests, wasting less traffic on bad versions; statistical inference is harder

All three tables run on one theme: every choice trades "more accurate" for "slower, costlier, or harder to change." What an interview actually tests is not how many options you can name, but whether you can justify one against a stated scale and latency budget.

Why it matters / real systems

The framework isn't only for interviews. It maps onto what the industry calls MLOps: feature platforms, experimentation platforms, model registries and versioning, drift monitoring. Pull apart the internal ML platforms at Google, Meta, Netflix or Airbnb and you get roughly these seven steps, engineered.

In an interview it gives you pacing: of 45 minutes, spend 5 clarifying, 5 translating the objective and pinning input/output, 20 on data, features and model choice, and the last 10 on evaluation and post-launch monitoring. Skipping step 2 and going straight to the model is the most common way to lose these interviews.

Industry evidence · claims these companies made publicly, not the book's
  • Google argued that in real-world ML systems the ML code is only a tiny fraction, with configuration, data collection, feature extraction, serving infrastructure and monitoring making up the bulk — direct support for this chapter's "the model is one small box." D. Sculley et al., "Hidden Technical Debt in Machine Learning Systems," NeurIPS 2015
  • Netflix publicly explained that only part of the million-dollar Netflix Prize winning solution was put into production; the full ensemble was not shipped because the engineering effort did not justify the measured gains — an offline win is not an online system. Netflix Tech Blog, "Netflix Recommendations: Beyond the 5 Stars," 2012
  • Booking.com, reviewing 150 models it had put into production, reported that gains in offline model performance do not reliably correlate with online business value — the hardest evidence for the "offline up, online flat" trap. Bernardi et al., "150 Successful Machine Learning Models: 6 Lessons Learned at Booking.com," KDD 2019
  • Google's Rules of Machine Learning opens with "don't be afraid to launch a product without machine learning": ship with heuristics first and get the data pipeline running — the same advice as this chapter's "baseline first." M. Zinkevich, Google

Pitfalls and open questions

  • Using the business objective as the ML objective: translate "more engagement" into "maximise clicks" and the model learns clickbait; this mismatch never shows up in offline metrics, only in business data months later.
  • Treating offline metrics as the bar for shipping: a higher offline score only says the model fits history better, and history carries the incumbent system's exposure bias. Without an online experiment, nothing is verified.
  • Ignoring training-serving skew: inconsistent feature definitions between offline and online is the most mundane and most frequent cause of "good offline, bad online," and it's hard to find from the model side.
  • Reading "monitoring" as latency and error rates: ML failure is silent, so operational metrics alone amount to no monitoring at all.
  • Period limits: written in 2023, the ten case chapters live in a discriminative-model world; generative systems (no ground-truth evaluation, cost per token, failures that are fluent rather than loud) need a different framework — the subject of the next book in the series. The seven steps are also narrated linearly, while real projects loop between steps 3, 4 and 5 many times.

Takeaways

1. The book's framing chapter: ML system design = seven steps — clarify requirements, frame as an ML task, prepare data, develop the model, evaluate, deploy and serve, monitor.

2. Core claim: model code is a small piece of the system; data, features, serving, experimentation and monitoring are where the work is.

3. Step 2 matters most: translate the business objective into an optimisable ML objective and pin input/output down — same requirement, different definitions, two different systems; ranking problems are usually framed as learning to rank or representation learning.

4. Feature engineering has four moves: missing values, scaling, discretisation, categorical encoding (learned embeddings for high cardinality); the engineering risk is train/serve consistency.

5. Build a baseline before anything complex; training must handle class imbalance, loss choice, fine-tuning and distributed training.

6. Evaluation has two layers that routinely disagree: offline gains don't imply online gains — exposure bias and objective mismatch are the usual culprits.

7. Rollout methods each cost something: shadow is risk-free but yields no business metrics, A/B gives causality but slowly, canary contains blast radius, interleaving saves an order of magnitude of samples for ranking.

8. ML systems degrade silently: drift is the leading cause, so monitoring must cover both operational metrics and input/output distributions — the seven steps are a loop, not a line.
