Architecture Decisions: The Reasoning Is Worth More Than the Verdict

Fundamentals of Software Architecture · Ch 19 · Mark Richards & Neal Ford · 2020

中文 →

What is this chapter about?

You buy an older house and find a pillar in the middle of the living room. You want it gone. But nobody knows why it's there: is it holding up the floor above, or was it a previous owner's decorative whim? With no one to ask, you have two options: live with it, or knock it down and hope. Software teams hit this every day: why is the system built this way? The original reasons are long forgotten. This chapter is about keeping the "why" around.

A strange thing first: the same argument, again and again

Most teams have sat through this meeting: something settled last month gets argued all over again this month, because the new people don't know why it was decided and the old hands can't quite remember. The book names it "Groundhog Day," after the film where the hero wakes up to the same day over and over.

Why the old way is hard

Decision makers tend to fall into three holes. First, afraid of being wrong, they never commit, and everyone waits. Second, once they do commit, they explain the "what" but not the "why", so the argument keeps coming back. Third, the reasons do get written, but scattered across inboxes and chat threads, where newcomers can't find them or don't even know a decision exists.

How it works

The fix is humble: for every big decision, write a decision card, like a builder's job log. The card says five things: what the decision is called; whether it's a draft, in force, or replaced by a newer one; what situation we faced and which options we had; which one we picked and why; and what good and bad things follow from it.

All cards live in one place everyone can browse, not in somebody's email. When a decision changes, you don't tear up the old card; you write a new one and mark the old one "replaced by card number such-and-such," so the whole story stays readable.

As for when to decide: not too early, when you don't know enough yet, and not so late that everyone is waiting on you. The best moment is the last point before waiting starts to cost something, when you know the most.

What you get, and how to choose

A newcomer reads the card and knows why the pillar is there, and whether it's safe to remove. Old questions stop being reopened; arguments become "has the situation changed since then?" The price: cards take time to write, and cards nobody maintains go stale and start to mislead.

One line to remember

Architecture decisions = decide on time, write down why, keep it findable. One decision card remembers, for the whole team, why things are the way they are.

The one-liner

You think an architect's output is the decision. The book says the decision itself is the cheapest part; the justification is what's valuable. Chapter 19 of FoSA breaks good decision making into three things: decide at the last responsible moment, give both technical and business justification, and record it in one searchable place, an Architecture Decision Record (ADR). It uses three chained anti-patterns to show what happens when any one is missing.

Glossary

  • Architecture decision: a decision that shapes the system's structure or key qualities and is expensive to change, such as synchronous calls versus messaging between services.
  • Anti-pattern: a common practice that looks reasonable but reliably produces bad outcomes.
  • Architecture characteristics: the non-domain qualities a system must deliver, such as availability, performance, scalability.
  • ADR (Architecture Decision Record): a one-to-two-page document capturing a single decision, its context, its justification, and its consequences.
  • Fitness function: an automated check that continuously verifies the system still respects an architectural rule (Chapter 6).
  • Publish/subscribe (pub/sub): a sender posts a message to a topic and every subscriber gets its own copy; the sender doesn't need to know who is listening.
  • Architecture review board (ARB): a group of senior people who approve major or cross-team architecture decisions.
  • Wiki: a shared online documentation site anyone on the team can search and edit.

What problem this chapter tackles

The book opens by calling decision making one of an architect's core duties. The hard part isn't only choosing well; it is the whole lifecycle: when to commit, how to win people over, and how to make sure someone a year later still knows why.

What goes wrong without it is concrete. A 30-person team makes dozens of architecture-level decisions in a year; after some turnover, most of the reasoning lives only in the heads and inboxes of people who have left. A newcomer facing a design that "looks dumb" can only accept it blindly or overturn it blindly, and overturning it may walk straight back into the trap the original decision was built to avoid. Michael Nygard, who proposed ADRs, put this dilemma most sharply, and the chapter is built around it.

The core ideas, in depth

① Three anti-patterns, one chain

The book frames decision failures as a progression: overcome the first and you tend to fall into the second; overcome that and the third awaits.

Covering Your Assets: the architect fears being wrong, so keeps postponing and "researching a bit more." Two remedies. First, decide at the last responsible moment: wait until there is enough information to justify and validate the decision, but not so long that development teams are held up or you slide into analysis paralysis. Second, collaborate closely with development teams: implementation details often break a decision, developers see it first, and the architect must be able to adjust quickly.

Groundhog Day: the decision was made, but nobody knows why, so it keeps getting relitigated. The root cause is incomplete justification. The book requires both technical and business justification, with business value usually falling into four buckets: cost, time to market, user satisfaction, and strategic positioning. It also offers a reverse test: if you can't name any business value for a decision, maybe it shouldn't be made.

Email-Driven Architecture: decisions and reasons live in email and chat, where people lose them, forget them, or never learn the decision was made. The remedy: don't put the decision in the body of an email. Put it in a single system of record (such as a wiki) and let the email say only "I've made a decision that affects you; see this link." And notify only the people actually affected, not everyone.

② What counts as "architecturally significant"

Not every decision deserves an ADR. The book adopts Nygard's definition: an architecturally significant decision is one that affects any of these five things:

  • Structure: the architecture patterns or styles in use, such as letting microservices share data.
  • Nonfunctional characteristics: performance, scalability, and other architecture characteristics.
  • Dependencies: coupling points between components or services.
  • Interfaces: how services and components are accessed, including gateways, contracts, and versioning.
  • Construction techniques: platforms, frameworks, tools, even processes.

The practical test: expensive to reverse, or reaching across teams? Write it down. Picking a logging library, which you can swap easily? Don't.

③ What an ADR looks like: five sections plus two

Nygard proposed ADRs in a 2011 blog post with five sections; the book adds Compliance and Notes.

  • Title: a sequential number plus a short, unambiguous phrase, e.g. "42. Use of Asynchronous Messaging Between Order and Payment Services."
  • Status: Proposed, Accepted, Superseded; the book also adds Request for Comments (RFC) with an explicit deadline for gathering input. When superseding, the old ADR is kept and marked "Superseded by 68," while 68 says "Supersedes 42," forming a traceable history. Status can also encode approval rules: the book suggests agreeing in advance which decisions an architect can approve alone and which, because of cost, cross-team impact, or security, go to a higher authority.
  • Context: what situation forces the decision, with the alternatives considered. As a side effect, it documents the architecture itself.
  • Decision: written in an affirmative, commanding voice, "We will use..." rather than "I think ... might be better." The emphasis is on why; the book's Second Law of Software Architecture is exactly this: why is more important than how.
  • Consequences: good and bad alike, which forces the author to think the trade-off through.
  • Compliance: how the decision will be measured and governed, by manual review or as an automated fitness function (for example, a test library like ArchUnit asserting "the business layer must not call the persistence layer directly").
  • Notes: metadata such as original author, approval date, approver, superseded date, and last modifier.

The book's example comes from the auction platform: after the bid-capture service receives a bid, it must forward it to the service that streams bids and the service that tracks them; the options are point-to-point calls or asynchronous pub/sub. The ADR records the choice of pub/sub: the sender needs no response, and new subscribers can be added without touching the sender; the consequences section honestly states the cost, that the message queues must be clustered and highly available (paraphrased from the book's example).

④ Where they live and what else they do

The book leans toward storing ADRs in a repository or wiki separate from application code: some decisions span several applications, and some readers (managers, for instance) have no access to code repositories. Directories are organized by scope: a single application, common to all applications, integration, and enterprise-wide.

ADRs have two bonus uses. One is as architecture documentation: Context plus Decision already explain why the system looks the way it does. The other is as a test for standards: when a team wants to set a rule, write an ADR first. If you can't justify it, you may not need the standard.

A rough sense of scale: an ADR is usually one or two pages, about an hour of careful writing; a single Groundhog Day relitigation is often a whole meeting for five or six engineers. Put side by side, any decision that will be asked about a second time has already paid for its record.

Why it matters, in real systems

ADRs are among the easiest tools in this book to carry straight back to a team: cheap, lightweight, immediately useful. In interviews and reviews, "tell me about a key technical decision you made, and why" is almost guaranteed, and the ADR structure (context, options, decision, costs, how it's verified) is a ready-made skeleton for the answer.

To start on a team: create an adr/ directory in your architecture-docs repository, begin at number 1, and make the first record "We will use ADRs." From then on, review meetings discuss only entries in Proposed and update their status afterwards. For an interview, say it like this: "Make architecture decisions at the last responsible moment, give both technical and business justification, and record them as ADRs: title, status, context, decision, consequences, compliance, notes; superseded records are kept and linked both ways."

Pitfalls and debates

  • "An ADR is a design doc": an ADR captures one decision and centers on why; long design docs tend to bury the reasoning.
  • "Change the decision, edit the original": rewriting an old ADR erases history; write a new one and mark the supersession.
  • Technical justification only: "because it's faster" won't stop the next debate; adding cost or time-to-market reasons gets non-technical stakeholders on board.
  • Writing everything down: ADRs for every reversible trifle bury the important ones; record only architecturally significant decisions.
  • Leaving Compliance empty: a decision nobody verifies quietly erodes over iterations; automate it as a fitness function where you can.
  • The storage debate: the book leans toward a separate repo or wiki, while many in the industry (such as the radar above) prefer the code repo. A common compromise: application-level ADRs live with the code; cross-application and enterprise ADRs go in a separate repository.
  • Period limits: written in 2020, the book's approval flow reads as traditional. Many teams now run "proposed → accepted" through pull requests, with merging as the approval; the spirit is the same.

Key takeaways

① The decision is the cheap part; the reasoning is the valuable part. Why is more important than how.

② Three anti-patterns arrive in sequence: Covering Your Assets (won't decide) → Groundhog Day (no "why") → Email-Driven Architecture (written but unfindable).

③ Decide at the last responsible moment, and stay close to development teams so you can adjust.

④ Justify on both technical and business grounds; a decision with no business value deserves suspicion.

⑤ Architecturally significant = affects structure, nonfunctional characteristics, dependencies, interfaces, or construction techniques.

⑥ An ADR has seven sections: Title, Status, Context, Decision, Consequences, plus the book's Compliance and Notes.

⑦ Status flows; superseded records stay, linked both ways; status can also encode approval rules.

⑧ Email carries only a link; ADRs double as architecture documentation and as a test of whether a standard deserves to exist.
