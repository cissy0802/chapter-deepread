The Future of Data Engineering

What is this chapter about?

Every time you scroll a video feed or order takeout, a group of people has moved huge amounts of data from all over the place, cleaned it up, and delivered it to dashboards and recommendation algorithms. Those people are data engineers. In the book's final chapter, the authors put the specific tools down and ask: will this job still exist in ten years, and what will it look like? Their answer: the tools will keep changing, but the work of carrying data from where it's born to where it's used isn't going anywhere.

A strange fact first

More than a decade ago, a company that wanted to analyze "big data" first had to staff a small crew just to babysit a pile of machines: installing software, fixing breakdowns, tuning settings. Today a beginner can sign up for a cloud account and query hundreds of millions of rows that afternoon. The tools keep getting easier, yet data engineers haven't been put out of work; they're in more demand than ever.

Why the old way was hard

  • You had to be a mechanic before you could drive. Most of the effort went into keeping the system from falling over, not into making the data useful.
  • Every tool spoke its own language. Switch tools and you had to move and reformat all the data again.
  • The data was always "yesterday's." Everything was crunched once overnight, and results showed up the next morning.

The core intuition

  • From running your own generator to a wall socket. Factories once built their own power plants; then the grid reached every door and you just plugged in. Cloud services take the chore of babysitting machines off your hands.
  • Like a phone's operating system giving every app shared rules. Photos, contacts, and notifications are handled by the system, so apps can work with each other. Data tools are heading the same way: everyone stores data in the same formats, so switching tools doesn't mean moving it again.
  • From yesterday's newspaper to a live broadcast. Data is processed and shown to people as it happens, rather than saved up for a day and crunched at night.
  • Less grunt work, more stewardship. Once the machines look after themselves, the hard questions become: is this data trustworthy, who may use it, and do the numbers mean what we think?
  • Don't underestimate the spreadsheet. The most widely used data tool in the world is the office spreadsheet. Good new tools will let people handle huge cloud datasets as easily as a spreadsheet.

What it brings / how to choose

With the barrier lowered, small teams can do what only giant companies could afford before, and a data engineer's value moves from "can build the system" to "understands the business, takes good care of the data, and wires it into the product." One honest cost: these are the authors' predictions, not settled facts, and "live broadcast" processing is faster but also pricier and harder to debug; not every company needs it.

Remember it in one line

The tools will keep changing; the road that carries data from birth to use won't. Hand the grunt work to the cloud and spend people on the judgment calls that matter.

In one sentence

You'd think that as tools become push-button simple, data engineers get replaced. It's the opposite: what gets automated away is the chore of babysitting infrastructure, while the lifecycle itself (generation, storage, ingestion, transformation, serving, plus undercurrents like security, data management, and DataOps) doesn't go anywhere. The chapter is the authors' set of bets on the future: complexity keeps falling; cloud services grow into an interoperable "data operating system"; data engineering becomes more "enterprisey" and management-heavy; job boundaries get redrawn; and the field moves from the batch-centric modern data stack toward a streaming, application-fused live data stack.

Glossary

  • Data engineering lifecycle: the book's backbone. Data is generated in source systems, then stored, ingested, transformed, and finally served to analytics and machine learning.
  • Undercurrents: six concerns that cut across every stage: security, data management, DataOps, data architecture, orchestration, and software engineering.
  • Managed / serverless: the cloud vendor runs the machines; you just submit jobs or queries and pay for what you use.
  • Orchestration: scheduling a chain of data tasks by their dependencies, like Airflow's "run A, then B."
  • Open file formats: shared formats any engine can read and write, such as Parquet (columnar) and Avro (row-based, with a schema).
  • Metadata catalog: the registry of which tables exist, what their columns are, and where their files live, such as the Hive Metastore.
  • OLAP: analytical queries that scan many rows at once to aggregate them, as opposed to the row-by-row reads and writes of an app.
  • Stream processing: handling each event as it arrives, instead of collecting events into a batch and processing them on a schedule.

Where it sits

The book's last chapter, closing Part III right after Chapter 10, "Security and Privacy." The first ten chapters cover how things are done today; this one is the authors' forecast for the next few years built on those principles. In practice it maps to architecture and career questions like "what should our next data platform bet on?"

What problem this chapter tackles

Data tools turn over at a frightening pace. Around 2010 the star was the self-hosted Hadoop cluster; a few years later, Spark; a few years after that, the "modern data stack" assembled from Snowflake or BigQuery plus dbt and Fivetran; and then lakehouses and streaming databases. An engineer who only learns tools has to start over every three to five years. The chapter asks: amid all this churn, what changes, what doesn't, and where should people place their bets? Get it wrong and you lose both ways: you either cling to an outdated stack or chase every new buzzword, and never actually get good at reliably delivering data to the people who use it.

The core ideas, explained

1. The lifecycle isn't going away; the tools are

The authors' first claim is the foundation of the whole book: the lifecycle and its undercurrents are stable abstractions, and tools are just how a given era implements them. In the Hadoop days you still ingested, stored, transformed, and served; move to a cloud warehouse and not one stage disappears. What changes is who performs each step and with what. The authors even argue that data engineering will grow in importance: the more a company depends on data, the more it needs people who keep that chain healthy.

2. The decline of complexity and the rise of easy-to-use tools

Early big data meant buying your own servers, deploying Hadoop, tuning JVM settings, and replacing failed disks at 2 a.m. Only large companies could afford a team like that. Managed services and serverless warehouses absorb that layer of complexity: you pay per byte scanned or per unit of compute time and never think about the cluster, and ingestion comes as ready-made managed connectors. The result is that big data capabilities get democratized; a team of a few people can handle terabytes. The authors' inference: data engineers will move up from low-level operations and spend their time on architecture, data quality, and business value. The work doesn't disappear; it moves up a layer.

3. The cloud-scale data OS and improved interoperability

This is the chapter's most vivid analogy. An operating system gives applications standard services (a file system, process scheduling, device drivers) so each app doesn't have to build its own. The authors see cloud data services growing into the same kind of layer: object storage is the "file system," query engines and processing frameworks are the "applications," and what lets them cooperate is standardized interfaces: common object storage APIs, open file formats like Parquet and Avro, a new generation of metadata catalogs replacing older ones such as the Hive Metastore, and orchestrators that grow from "just run the jobs" into platforms aware of data assets, lineage, and quality. The better the interoperability, the cheaper it is to swap out an engine, and the less data has to be copied again for each new tool.

4. "Enterprisey" data engineering

Among engineers, "enterprisey" is usually an insult: tedious and bureaucratic. The authors flip it. The easier the underlying tools become, the more data management, DataOps, governance, and data quality, the stuff once dismissed as "too enterprise," become the core of a data engineer's job. Once the machines look after themselves, the remaining hard problems are: who owns this table, are the metric definitions consistent, will we notice when something breaks, and who is allowed to see it.

5. Titles and responsibilities will morph

The authors expect job boundaries to keep shifting, with more overlap among data engineering, analytics engineering, ML engineering, and software engineering. Software engineers will be expected to know more about data (streaming, data modeling), while data engineers move closer to the business and the application. Titles may change; the lifecycle work won't shrink.

6. Beyond the modern data stack, toward the live data stack

The modern data stack (MDS) was the dominant recipe around 2020: managed connectors batch-load data into a cloud warehouse, SQL transforms it in place (ELT), and BI dashboards sit on top. It's cheap and plug-and-play, but at heart it's batch: data often lags by hours to a day, and it mostly serves "humans reading reports." The authors' bet for the next stop is the live data stack: data arrives as streams, is transformed on the stream, lands in real-time analytical databases that answer in seconds, and directly powers application features such as recommendations, fraud checks, and dynamic pricing. With it comes the fusion of data and applications: apps aren't just sources of data, they consume analytical results in real time, and a tighter feedback loop forms between applications and ML, with models adjusting continuously to what users just did. The authors admit that streaming data modeling and tool maturity are still catching up.

7. Dark matter data and the spreadsheet

The authors point out a fact engineers tend to overlook: the most widely used data platform in the world is the spreadsheet. A huge share of real decisions runs on spreadsheets scattered everywhere, like dark matter in the universe: invisible, yet enormous in weight. They predict a new class of tools that keeps the spreadsheet's interactive feel while sitting on top of cloud analytical engines, so business users can work directly with massive datasets. The lesson for data engineers: don't serve only the people who write SQL.

Key trade-offs and choices

Table 2 · Once complexity falls, where to place your effort

ChoiceYou getCost / risk
Self-hosted open sourceControl, deep customization, no vendor lock-inHeavy ops burden; the team's energy goes to plumbing

The rules of thumb are simple: freshness requirements decide batch vs streaming. Go streaming only when the business must act on the data within minutes; otherwise batch is cheaper and easier to debug. Differentiation decides build vs buy: any layer that isn't your competitive edge, hand to the cloud.

Why it matters / in real systems

Asked in an interview "how would you build a data platform from scratch today?", this chapter gives you the skeleton: lay out the five lifecycle stages and the undercurrents first, then choose tools layer by layer; keep data in object storage with open formats and a unified catalog so you aren't locked into one engine; default to batch and open a streaming path only where there is a real need for seconds-level freshness (fraud, recommendations); and spend the people you save on data quality, metric definitions, and access control. The same goes for a career: learn tools, but hang them on the lifecycle map, so when the next generation arrives it's just a part swap.

Open-source / industry evidence

  • Interoperability is landing: many engines, one table. Apache Iceberg describes itself as "a high-performance format for huge analytic tables" that lets engines like Spark, Trino, Flink, Presto, Hive, and Impala "safely work with the same tables, at the same time." That is exactly the standard-interface layer of the "data OS." Source: apache/iceberg README, GitHub
  • A common language in memory. Apache Arrow calls itself "a universal columnar format and multi-language toolbox for fast data interchange and in-memory analytics," enabling data systems to efficiently store, process, and move data. Source: apache/arrow README, GitHub
  • Real-time analytical databases come from big tech. Apache Pinot describes itself as "a real-time distributed OLAP datastore, built to deliver scalable real-time analytics with low latency," ingesting both batch sources such as S3 and HDFS and streams such as Kafka; it "was built by engineers at LinkedIn and Uber." Source: apache/pinot README, GitHub

Pitfalls and debates

  • "Push-button tools will make data engineers obsolete." What disappears is the ops grind; lifecycle and data management work only grows.
  • "Real-time is always better than batch." Most reports don't need seconds-level freshness; streaming costs more and is harder to debug, and without a business action behind it, it's waste.
  • "Managed means no lock-in." You trade away ops for billing and migration risk; open formats are the hedge.
  • "The modern data stack is the end state." The authors are explicit that it is one phase; batch's ceiling is freshness.
  • The limits of prediction. This chapter is opinion rather than argument, written in 2022. Since then lakehouses and open table formats have indeed spread, bearing out the interoperability call; but the shifts brought by the large-language-model wave (unstructured data, vector search) are barely touched here, and the "live data stack" has not displaced batch across the board as forecast.

Key takeaways

① The lifecycle (generation, storage, ingestion, transformation, serving) and its six undercurrents aren't going away; tools are just one era's implementation.

② Complexity is falling: managed and serverless services absorb operations, big data gets democratized, and a small team can handle terabytes.

③ Cloud services are growing into a "data OS": object storage + open formats + metadata catalogs + smarter orchestration, with swappable engines.

④ "Enterprisey" isn't an insult: data management, DataOps, governance, and quality become the core of the job.

⑤ Job boundaries will shift; software engineers learn more about data, and data engineers move closer to the business and the app.

⑥ The modern data stack is batch and serves humans; the live data stack is streaming, powers the app directly, and closes a feedback loop with ML.

⑦ Decision rules: freshness needs decide batch vs streaming; differentiation decides build vs buy.

⑧ Spreadsheets are the biggest "dark matter" data platform; don't serve only SQL users.

⑨ These are forecasts: lakehouses and open formats came true, while the LLM-era shifts lie outside this chapter's view.
