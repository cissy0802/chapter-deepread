Personalized Headshots: a Face Is the One Condition You Cannot Write Into a Prompt

Generative AI System Design Interview · Ch 10 · Ali Aminian, Hao Sheng & Alex Xu · 2024

中文 →

What is this chapter about?

You upload a dozen phone selfies, pick a "professional headshot" style, wait a few minutes, and get back dozens of studio-lit portraits in a suit — with your face still being your face. This chapter is about how that pipeline is built.

A strange thing first

Last chapter's model was already very good at drawing. Tell it "a professional headshot of a woman in her thirties" and it produces something fast and beautiful — but it is a stranger. Add details: monolid eyes, a high nose bridge, a mole on the left cheek… by the twentieth adjective it is still somebody else.

Think about it and you already know why: could you describe your mother in words well enough that a stranger could pick her out of a crowd? The words simply do not exist. What makes a face recognizable lives in millimetre-scale geometry, and adjectives are a very blunt instrument.

So stop describing — show it instead

If it can't be said, teach it differently: put a handful of your photos in front of the model, let it take a short class, and give you a brand-new name that means only you. Afterwards you say "draw that name standing on a snowy mountain" and it knows who to draw.

There are two ways to run that class. One is to let it relearn everything: the memory sticks best, but every single user then needs their own full copy of the retrained "brain", several gigabytes each — and it easily overdoes it, getting so absorbed in you that afterwards everyone it draws looks like you.

The other is what nearly every product picks: leave the original skills completely untouched and pin a tiny badge beside them. A few dozen megabytes, clipped on when you want a picture and taken off afterwards — so one brain can serve thousands of people in turn.

An easy trap

Overdo the class and what it learned is not "your face" but those dozen photos memorized whole. Ask for you on a snowy mountain and you get the same white study wall as before, just with a more accurate face.

So "does it look like me" cannot be read alone — while it climbs, "does it do what I asked" is falling. What you want is where the two cross. It is also why a dozen photos from different places and different angles beat forty taken in front of the same wall.

And this chapter carries something extra

Earlier chapters generated fictional people; this one generates a real person. So a whole set of things the earlier chapters never needed has to be built into the flow: confirming on upload that the face really is yours, blocking what shouldn't be produced, leaving a traceable mark on the image, and — when someone deletes their account — destroying that badge along with the photos, because it amounts to a standing ability to recreate that person.

The cost is concrete too: every user needs their own training run, a per-head compute bill none of the earlier chapters had.

Remember this one line

Identity is the one condition you cannot talk your way into, so it has to be taught with samples; the winning way to teach is not "relearn everything" but "pin on a few-dozen-megabyte badge", because that lets one brain be shared by everyone and lets the memory be deleted cleanly; and "does it look like me" must always be read alongside "does it do what I asked", or you end up with a model that only recites its training photos.

In one line

Last chapter's condition was a sentence; this chapter's is a handful of photos — identity is the one condition that cannot be encoded into a prompt. The fix is to write it into the model from few samples: learn a single word vector (Textual Inversion), fine-tune the whole network with a built-in antidote (DreamBooth's prior preservation), or hang a low-rank bypass (LoRA). But the real systems problem sits outside the model: storing and hot-swapping one artifact per user, an evaluation where "looks like me" and "does what I asked" pull against each other, and likeness rights — the one chapter in the book where abuse prevention is not a bonus but the requirement itself.

Glossary

  • Base model: last chapter's general text-to-image model — the one set of weights every user shares.
  • Fine-tuning: continuing to train an already-trained model on a little new data, nudging it in some direction.
  • Few-shot: learning from single digits to a few dozen samples, as opposed to training from scratch.
  • Embedding: representing a word or a face as a string of numbers; similar things end up with nearby strings.
  • Adapter: a small block of trainable parameters hung beside a frozen main model, holding the extra memory for one task or one identity.
  • Rank: how many genuinely independent directions a matrix has; lower rank means fewer expressible variations and fewer parameters.
  • Overfitting: the model memorizing the training samples instead of the pattern behind them; it falls apart in a new setting.
  • Face embedding: the string of numbers a face-recognition model outputs (commonly 512 dimensions); different photos of the same person land close together.
  • Cosine similarity: a measure of how closely two such strings point the same way, from −1 to 1; closer to 1 means more alike.
  • Biometric data: bodily features that uniquely identify a natural person — a face is the classic case, and most jurisdictions put extra constraints on it.

What this chapter is solving

Pin the scenario down first: a user uploads 10–20 selfies, picks a style pack, and the system returns dozens of professional headshots within a few minutes — an inherently asynchronous product shape, not Ch9's second-scale synchronous generation.

Why isn't Ch9's machinery enough? Because 77 tokens cannot hold a face. Text conditioning has been discretized by language; it can only express things language has words for. What makes a face recognizable sits in millimetre-scale geometry — inter-eye distance, nasolabial folds, cheekbone projection — and a face-recognition model needs a 512-dimensional continuous vector to hold it. Language has no words along those dimensions.

What happens if you don't solve it? There is no "fluently wrong" escape hatch here, unlike last chapter: however polished the headshot, if it isn't you the product value is zero — users reject it at a glance.

Two more constraints all but single-handedly determine the technical choices below:

  • The cost shape changed. Ch9 was "one model serves everyone"; this chapter is "one set of weights per person". Stored as full models, a million users × several GB is petabyte-scale storage — and those weights cannot share GPU memory with anyone else at inference time. The arithmetic doesn't close.
  • The input is a real face. Is the uploader the person? Can the output be used to deceive? In this chapter compliance and abuse prevention are not non-functional requirements in an appendix; they are the requirement.

The core content, properly

1. The shared skeleton: make the samples themselves the condition

Since identity can't be described, it has to be taught with samples. The training objective never changes — it is still Ch8's denoising loss: add noise to your photo, have the model restore it. Exactly one thing varies: which parameters this round is allowed to change. That is the entire difference between the three mainstream routes: a single new slot in the token-embedding table, the whole denoising network, or a very narrow bypass beside the attention layers.

2. Textual Inversion: don't change the model, change the dictionary

The lightest route. The diffusion model and the text encoder are entirely frozen; you open one pseudo-word S* in the vocabulary and take gradients only on that single embedding vector — so that shown "a photo of S*", the model can restore your photo out of noise.

In plain terms: none of the model's skill moved, you only added an entry to its dictionary. From then on S* enters prompts like any noun: "S* in a spacesuit, oil painting".

The upside is seductive: the artifact is one vector, a few KB, entries compose freely, and it cannot possibly contaminate the base — you changed nothing.

The cost is equally plain: expressiveness is capped by what one vector can hold. Likeness is the lowest of the three, and precisely the identifying details — a mole, a hairline — tend to disappear. With so few trainable parameters the gradient signal is weak, so it actually trains slower than LoRA, often needing thousands of steps. It suits a style or an object — and what this chapter needs is a face.

3. DreamBooth: full fine-tuning, and the antidote it must carry

The other extreme. A prompt built from a rare identifier plus a class word (shaped like "a sks person") fine-tunes the entire denoising network, often the text encoder with it.

Why must the identifier be rare? Because you need a symbol the model has almost no prior for to hang this face on. Use "john" and you fight the john it already knows, ending up half you and half its imagined john. A meaningless short string like sks is an empty lot in the vocabulary.

But it has two unavoidable side effects:

  • Language drift. Train repeatedly on the class word "person" and the model's understanding of person as a whole gets dragged toward your dozen photos — after which everyone it draws looks a bit like you. You contaminated not a word but an entire category.
  • Overfitting to background and pose. A dozen selfies usually come from one room, one angle, one light; the model can't tell "which parts are this person" from "which parts are just that wall".

The antidote is the prior preservation loss: during training you also feed a batch of the model's own generated images of the same class ("a photo of a person" — implementations commonly use a couple of hundred), and require its output on that batch to stay essentially unchanged. In plain terms, you teach it "sks is you" while constantly reminding it what an ordinary person looks like — regularizing the model with its own prior knowledge.

It reveals a general rule: whenever you change a large model with a small amount of data, you must simultaneously arrange to preserve what it already knew, or you get a model that is stronger in a narrow domain and dumber everywhere else.

Likeness and controllability are the best of the three, but the artifact is a full copy of the weights (Stable Diffusion-family snapshots run 2–4 GB), one per user — a storage dead end that keeps it out of consumer products.

4. LoRA: compress the change itself to low rank, and the serving problem dissolves

The engineering landing point of the chapter. LoRA's observation: what fine-tuning wants to do — move the weight matrix W a little — does not need a full-rank move. So freeze W and wire a narrow channel alongside it:

output = W·x + B·A·x, where A is r×d, B is d×r, and the rank r is typically 4–32

What that formula is saying in plain terms: constrain "how much you may move" into a very narrow shape. Parameters drop from d×d to 2×d×r — about 1/64 when d=1024 and r=8. Only A and B train; not a byte of the original weights changes. It hangs on the attention layers' projection matrices — exactly where conditioning entered in Ch9. To change "how the model gets steered by its condition", those layers are the best value.

The artifact lands at a few MB to a bit over a hundred MB, two to three orders of magnitude below a full model. But storage is only the paper win; the real dividend is the serving shape: the base model stays resident in GPU memory, and at inference you stack the adapter on by user_id and peel it off afterwards, so one GPU can serve thousands of users in turn. Full fine-tuning cannot do this — each user occupies their own copy of GPU memory, and utilization is doomed by design. Being a standalone file, the adapter can also enter a user's deletion pipeline, which matters below.

Quality is close to DreamBooth, so the most common practice combines them: DreamBooth's recipe (rare identifier plus prior preservation), with the artifact emitted as a LoRA.

There is also a route with no training at all: train an identity encoder, pull an identity vector from an off-the-shelf face-recognition model, and inject it through a separate cross-attention path decoupled from text — the user arrives and you simply run inference, no training, seconds to an image, at the price of a personalization ceiling fixed once and for all by that encoder. The book was written in 2024, when per-user fine-tuning was still the mainline.

5. Evaluation: higher likeness is not necessarily better

Three yardsticks, pulling against each other:

  • Identity similarity: run the generated image and the user's originals through an off-the-shelf face-recognition model and take the cosine similarity of the face embeddings; the chapter's primary metric.
  • Prompt fidelity: Ch9's CLIP score — did it actually deliver the suit, the mountain, the lighting.
  • Quality and diversity: FID plus human review.

The chapter's most counter-intuitive point: a monotonically rising identity similarity is not good news. Push the step count and similarity does keep climbing — but the later gains come from the model starting to copy the training photos: ask for "on a snowy mountain" and you still get that white study wall, only with a more accurate face. Fidelity collapses, diversity implodes, and the primary metric glows green.

So plot two curves: training intensity (steps, learning rate, rank r) on the x-axis, identity similarity and prompt fidelity on the y-axis, and stop early where they cross. This is the same disease as FID and CLIP score moving against each other along w in Ch9 — the dial just moved from sampling time to training time. Add one yardstick unique to this chapter: "is it copying" — run near-duplicate detection between generated images and training photos, and a hit is hard proof of overfitting.

6. Turning it into a service: async training, hot-swapped adapters, clean deletion

Training is an async job, not a synchronous request. Training one user's LoRA on a single mainstream training card takes minutes (commonly five to twenty), far beyond any HTTP timeout. The shape is therefore fixed: upload → verify → enqueue → GPU worker → artifact to object storage → callback. That is why these products all say "we'll notify you" instead of spinning.

Inference shares the base and hot-swaps the adapter. The first image for a given user pulls tens of MB from object storage — hundreds of milliseconds in-region; an LRU cache keyed on activity makes the cold start effectively invisible.

Deletion is a hard requirement here. On account closure you must delete not just the original photos but the adapter itself — it is data derived from biometrics, and keeping it means keeping a standing ability to recreate that person. That yields one hard architectural constraint: never merge the adapter into shared weights. Once baked into the base it cannot be separated out or cleanly deleted; stacking at runtime and peeling off afterwards is the deletable shape.

7. Likeness rights and abuse prevention: the one requirement you cannot treat as a bonus

The same capability, pointed at someone else's photos, is a deepfake. So protection has to be designed into the flow, not bolted on as a filter afterwards:

  • Input side: confirm it's the person. A guided selfie (randomized on-the-spot actions) beats "upload an album" by a wide margin — the latter lets anyone model a colleague or an ex. This is not a UX detail; it decides whether your system can be used as a forgery tool.
  • Training side: block celebrities. Match uploads against a celebrity face set and refuse on a hit.
  • Output side: filtering is mandatory, and one notch stricter than Ch9. There the output was a fictional person; here every inappropriate image points at a specific, recognizable human being.
  • Provenance: invisible watermark plus content-credential metadata. But be honest about its limits — screenshots, recompression and cropping all weaken it. It is a chain of evidence, not a defence.
  • Compliance: faces are special-category data. Most jurisdictions classify face data used to uniquely identify a natural person as requiring separate, explicit consent; that consent cannot be buried in general terms, and the retention period must be stated.

One lever fits in no table and is the cheapest of all: how you collect the photos. A dozen selfies across different settings, angles and lighting clearly beat forty taken in front of one wall — what overfitting feeds on is exactly the shared background and pose. That lever lives in your onboarding copy and costs no compute at all.

Why it matters / in real systems

Asked in an interview how you'd do personalization, "fine-tune it" is a non-answer. This chapter gives three separately actionable layers: what changes (word vector / full model / low-rank bypass), where you train (once per user / one encoder once) and how you serve (dedicated GPU memory / shared base with hot-swap). Keeping those three apart already beats most answers.

LoRA's significance also long ago outgrew this chapter: it is today the default shape of all large-model customization — domain adaptation for language models, style packs for image models, the same "freeze the trunk, hang a low-rank bypass".

Pitfalls and disputes

  • Treating identity similarity as the only metric. The winner it crowns will always be the "copies the training photos" run. Read it alongside prompt fidelity, with near-duplicate detection as a backstop.
  • Thinking LoRA's value is saved storage. Storage is the paper win; the real dividends are GPU utilization from a shared base and the deletability of a standalone adapter file. Merge it into the base and both disappear at once.
  • Treating the rare identifier as an incantation. sks is just an empty lot in the vocabulary where the prior is weakest; a common first name fights the semantics already there.
  • Forgetting prior preservation. Without it, full fine-tuning drags the whole class word along — after which everyone it draws looks a bit like you. Not a hyperparameter issue; a mandatory part of the method.
  • Dating. The book was written in 2024, with per-user fine-tuning as the mainline. Since then encoder-based zero-shot personalization has spread widely through consumer products, compressing "wait a few minutes" into seconds at the price of likeness and personalization ceiling. The skeleton is unchanged: identity is always injected as a path independent of text.
  • The real dispute is not technical. This capability and its abuse are the same thing — there is no technical switch that only generates the uploader. The constraints can only come from the process: identity verification, retention and deletion, provenance, explicit consent. Answering this chapter as pure engineering is the biggest misreading of it.

Takeaways

① The core claim: identity is the one condition you cannot write into a prompt — 77 tokens cannot hold a face, whose recognizability needs a 512-dimensional continuous vector. It can only be taught with samples.

② The three routes differ in exactly one respect: which parameters this round may change — one new slot in the embedding table (Textual Inversion), the whole denoiser (DreamBooth), a low-rank bypass beside attention (LoRA). A fourth trains nothing: an identity encoder injecting a face vector at inference, seconds fast but ceilinged.

③ DreamBooth must carry a prior preservation loss: teach "sks is you" while reminding it with its own generated class images what an ordinary person looks like, or language drift makes everyone look like you.

④ LoRA is the product default: W·x + B·A·x, rank r of 4–32, artifact tens of MB. The real dividend is not storage but hot-swapping on a shared base (GPU utilization) and deletability (never merge it into the base).

⑤ The most counter-intuitive evaluation point: monotonically rising identity similarity is not good — the later gains are bought by copying the training photos. Plot two curves, stop early where they cross, and add near-duplicate detection.

⑥ Training duration dictates the serving shape: minutes means an async queue plus callback; inference shares the base and hot-swaps the adapter by user_id.

⑦ The cheapest lever is on the product side: ask users for photos across different settings, angles and lighting — a dozen of those beat forty from the same wall.

⑧ Abuse prevention is a requirement, not an appendix: liveness on upload, celebrity blocking at training, one-notch-stricter output filtering, watermarks as evidence only, and faces as special-category data requiring explicit consent.
