Leader Election

Database Internals · Ch 10 · Alex Petrov · 2019

中文 →

What is this chapter about?

If a team had to hold a full vote on every small decision, nothing would ever get done. So the group picks one person to call the shots — you bring things to them, and their word is final. Every time you transfer money, send a message or upload a photo, the few dozen machines behind it are doing exactly that: picking one machine to be in charge. Chapter 10 of Database Internals is about how that pick is made, and why it is far harder than it sounds.

A strange thing first: the boss gets replaced while alive

The most common reason for replacing the boss is not that the machine actually broke. It is that its disk got busy and it answered half a second late. Nobody hears it speak up, everyone assumes it is gone, and a new election starts — while the machine itself, blissfully unaware, is still working normally. Replacing the boss is usually triggered by a misunderstanding, not by a failure.

Why the old world was hard: two bosses beat no boss

With no boss, work stalls — but it fails loudly, and someone comes to fix it. The real disaster is two bosses at once: the old one never actually died, it just went unheard, and the new one has already taken office. Two people writing in the same ledger at the same time can bill your payment twice, and afterwards nobody can tell which ledger to believe — both are internally consistent. This is the accident the whole chapter is built to prevent.

The core ideas: a few ways to pick, and one more important seal

The chapter lays out several classic approaches, each a step past the last:

  • Seniority: number every machine; among those alive, the highest number is boss. Simplest possible rule, and rather dumb: the highest-numbered machine may be the flakiest one, and every time it wobbles the whole group must re-elect.
  • Name a successor in advance: the boss adds a line to its routine check-in — "if I go down, number three takes over." When it does go down, number three simply steps up, skipping the whole group re-election.
  • Only a few may stand: if everyone can run, the shouting alone drowns the room. Nominate three to five candidates and let everyone else just pledge allegiance; the cost of arguing drops immediately.
  • Pass a note around the circle: everyone sits in a ring and passes a note; whoever is present adds their name and hands it on, and the dead are skipped. When the note returns to the start, the roster is complete.
  • Just allow two camps: a cut road inevitably splits the group in two, so accept it — each side picks its own boss and keeps working, and the two merge once the road reopens.

But the thing most worth remembering is none of the above. It is the seal: give each boss an official stamp engraved with their term number, and rule that documents count only if they bear the newest term. Now it no longer matters if the old boss wakes up still issuing orders — the stamp in their hand is last term's, so everything they stamp is void.

How to choose

The real trade-off is not "which picking method is cleverest." It is two things: do not keep swapping (every swap stops everyone while the handover happens), and once you swap, the old boss must truly lose power. The first comes from "don't move unless something actually broke"; the second comes from that term-numbered seal.

The honest cost: no picking method can guarantee there is only ever one boss — the best any of them promises is "eventually only one is left, after a messy stretch." The safety net was never in the picking. It is in the stamping.

One sentence to remember

Picking a boss is easy; voiding the previous one is the hard part. So stop expecting the election itself to be both fast and correct — stamp every term with a number and make everyone honour only the newest one. Then even when you get it wrong and briefly have two bosses, the ledger stays intact.

In one sentence

You think leader election is about "picking the fittest machine." What this chapter is really about is that election itself cannot guarantee a single leader at any instant. Electing a leader is an instance of consensus, and every algorithm engineers actually use is weaker and cheaper than consensus — it only promises to converge on one leader. Which pushes the real safety boundary somewhere else entirely: into stability (don't re-elect unless something truly broke) and epoch numbers (make the previous leader's writes fail).

Glossary

  • Leader / coordinator: the node the group picks to order operations, and whose word everyone else follows.
  • Network partition: the network splits in two; each side talks to itself but not the other. Both sides are alive, each believing the other is dead.
  • Split brain: two nodes in one cluster each believe they are the leader and each accept writes, so the data diverges with no way to decide automatically which history is correct.
  • Epoch / term: a monotonically increasing integer, bumped on every leader change, which turns "who is the current leader" into a comparable fact.
  • Lease: a time-limited grant. The leader must keep renewing it; if it stops, the lease simply expires — nobody has to declare it dead.
  • Quorum: the minimum votes needed to decide, usually a majority. 2f+1 nodes tolerate f failures, and a majority can only ever exist in one partition — that is the root defence against two leaders.

Where it sits

Third chapter of Part II, "Distributed Systems." It follows Chapter 9 on failure detection — the trigger for an election is "we've decided the old leader is dead," so the detector's false-positive rate is your re-election rate — and sets up Chapter 11 on replication (a leader is what makes single-point ordering possible) and Chapter 14 on consensus, where Raft and Multi-Paxos fold election into the algorithm itself. Real-world counterparts: the ZooKeeper leader, etcd's Raft leader, Kafka's controller and per-partition leaders, Kubernetes controllers racing for a Lease, and automated MySQL failover.

What the chapter is trying to solve

First, why insist on a leader at all? Because "have n nodes negotiate every operation" and "have one node assign the order" differ by an order of magnitude in cost: in a leaderless design every write runs a round of voting and waits for a majority — two or three round trips even inside one datacenter — whereas with a leader the client gets an ordered result in a single round trip. Ordering, uniqueness and conflict resolution all degrade from "things to negotiate" into "look it up in the leader's log."

What happens if you don't solve it? The two directions fail very asymmetrically.

No leader: it fails loudly. A three-node strongly consistent cluster whose leader dies and cannot be replaced is simply not writable — not slow, fully stalled. That election gap is the most visible line on your availability bill: etcd's default election timeout is 1 second, so from "the leader is gone" to "the new leader can work" takes at least a few hundred milliseconds to a couple of seconds, during which every write queues or errors out.

Two leaders: it fails silently. The old leader was merely misjudged — one long GC pause, one bout of cross-rack jitter — and has no idea it was deposed, so it keeps accepting writes while the new leader does too. Two internally consistent histories, with no automatic way to decide which is right: that is not slow recovery, it is permanent data corruption, usually ending in a human reconciling ledgers by hand.

So the real question in this chapter is not "which algorithm elects fastest." It is: given that the signal triggering an election is itself unreliable (Chapter 9 proved "slow" and "dead" are indistinguishable), how can an election mechanism that will misfire avoid corrupting data?

The core content, properly

1. Electing a leader is consensus — and nobody actually does it that way

Strictly speaking, "everyone agrees X is the leader" is a consensus decision: a set of nodes agreeing on a value in a way that cannot later be overturned. Consensus costs something: at least two rounds of messages, a majority, and durable state. Almost every classic algorithm in this chapter deliberately dodges that cost — no majority, just local rules like "whoever has the biggest id" — which means they only promise eventual convergence on one leader, never that two never coexist along the way. Hold onto that and the rest becomes easy to judge: these algorithms are not competing on correctness, but on how many messages, how fast the handover, and how bad the mess can get.

2. Bully: the minimal id-only solution, and its three blind spots

Bully is where every variant starts: each node has a globally comparable unique id (its rank), and the highest-ranked live node is leader. Whoever notices the leader is gone sends ELECTION to every node ranked above it; any reply means someone bigger is alive, so it drops out; silence means it broadcasts "I am the leader."

Why design it this way? Because it needs no shared state and no durability — ids you can compare and messages that arrive are enough. The price is three blind spots:

  • Rank, not fitness: the highest-ranked node may be the slowest box, the furthest from clients, the one with the stalest data — it wins anyway. The rule contains no notion of "who is better suited."
  • Instability: if that highest-ranked machine is intermittently sick, it grabs leadership back on every recovery and then dies again, trapping the cluster in perpetual re-election — effectively long-term downtime, a livelock.
  • Noise: worst case every node starts an election, giving O(n²) messages. Fine at twenty nodes; at two hundred a single false positive can kick off tens of thousands of messages.

3. Two ways to save a round: successor lists and candidate sets

Next-In-Line Failover: the leader piggybacks a priority-ordered list of alternates on its heartbeats. When it dies, the first name simply takes over and the entire election round disappears — handover time shrinks from "one election" to "one timeout." The cost is blunt: the list was written while the leader was alive and may be stale, so you must be able to walk down it, falling back to a full election only when it runs out.

Candidate / Ordinary: only candidates may become leader; ordinary nodes just notice trouble and notify a candidate. Messages drop from O(n²) across everyone to O(k²) among candidates — with 5 candidates in a 200-node cluster, an election costs two orders of magnitude less. The cost is that the candidate set becomes a new single point: lose all 5 and the remaining 195 healthy machines cannot elect anyone.

4. Ring and Invitation: one pretends partitions don't exist, one admits they do

Ring: nodes form a logical ring where each knows only its successor. The election message travels around it, appending each live node's id as it passes; by the time it returns to the origin the roster is complete, and the highest id on it becomes leader. The appeal is linear message cost and needing only "know your successor" worth of topology.

Invitation is the one genuine change of mind in the chapter. The others all strain to guarantee a single global leader; this one simply admits that multiple groups may exist. Each process starts as leader of its own group of one, processes invite each other in, groups merge, and one of the original group leaders carries on. A network partition stops being an anomaly: both sides form groups, keep working, and merge once connectivity returns.

The cost is that the hard part moves from election to merging — two groups each hold an independently written history, so who arbitrates the conflict? Precisely what CRDTs and eventual consistency handle in Chapter 11. The trade-off in one line: either sacrifice availability to keep a single leader, or sacrifice the single leader to keep availability and settle the bill at merge time.

5. Stability is the real thread: re-election is not free

When the book judges each algorithm, it keeps returning to one question: does it cause frequent re-elections? That is an accounting question. Every re-election means stopping writes, waiting for the new leader to catch its log up, and waiting for clients to reconnect and retry — a few hundred milliseconds to several seconds each time. A detector that misfires once a minute leaves the cluster permanently half-dead. Hence the first engineering rule: if the incumbent is still working, never re-elect (a stable leader).

This is also why Bully's blind spot is lethal: its rule is that the highest rank must lead, so that flaky top-ranked machine forces a re-election on every recovery. Real systems invert it: the incumbent keeps the job as long as it can renew.

The other damping trick is randomised timeouts. If every node times out at the same instant and campaigns at once, the votes split, nobody reaches a majority, and the whole thing repeats — a livelock. Add a little jitter to each node's waiting period and "who goes first" separates naturally.

6. The real safety boundary is not inside the election

None of the algorithms above can rule out this scene: the old leader only appeared dead (a five-second full GC is plenty), the new leader has taken office, and the old one wakes up still believing it leads, and writes. If election cannot prevent it, defend elsewhere — give every leadership term a monotonically increasing epoch, carry it on every write down to the storage layer, and have storage accept only the highest epoch it has seen.

This step swaps an impossible requirement — guarantee two leaders never coexist — for an achievable one: allow two leaders, but let only one change data. The old leader's write comes back rejected, which is how it learns it was deposed and steps down. Election provides liveness; the epoch provides safety. The book's full treatment of epochs waits for Chapter 14 — Paxos ballot numbers and Raft terms are doing exactly this job.

One rule you can take away directly: every irreversible action must carry the epoch to its executor — shifting traffic, deleting data, charging a card. An election only tells you "I am probably the leader"; the epoch is what says "I am demonstrably the current one."

Why it matters / real systems

In any architecture review or interview question about high availability, the second cut lands here (the first is failure detection). Upgrade "if the leader dies we elect a new one" into "incumbent renewal first, randomised timeouts to avoid split votes, candidates must have current logs, and the executor fences on epoch" and the answer is in a different class.

Naming real systems: ZooKeeper uses Zab, relying on transaction ids so the new leader's history is no older than anyone's; etcd uses Raft with a 100ms default heartbeat and a 1000ms election timeout; Kafka's controller was once elected via ZooKeeper and since KRaft uses its own Raft implementation, with partition leadership additionally requiring the new leader to be in the ISR (in-sync replica set); Kubernetes runs multiple controller replicas that race for a Lease object.

Pitfalls and disputes

  • Thinking "we elected a leader" means "there is one leader." The single most important line in the chapter: election only promises eventual convergence, so uniqueness does not come from the election, it comes from the epoch.
  • Shrinking the election timeout to go faster. What you buy is "one disk hiccup triggers a re-election," and re-elections stop writes, so you end up less available. etcd's guidance runs the other way: anchor the timeout to round-trip time, stretching to tens of seconds across regions.
  • Electing on id alone, ignoring data freshness. This is the widest gap between the chapter's classics and modern practice: Bully and Ring compare only static ranks, so they can crown a node whose log is behind — and the moment it accepts writes, that missing stretch of history is gone for good. Raft explicitly forbids candidates whose logs are not current; Zab does the same with transaction ids.
  • Two nodes for high availability. With 2 nodes a majority is 2, so losing either leaves no majority; allow "1 vote counts" instead and a partition gives each side a leader. Majority-based defence against two leaders needs at least 3.
  • Period limits. These algorithms mostly date from the 1980s, and the 2019 book presents them as a lineage of ideas — but industry has narrowed to two camps: election built into consensus (Raft / Zab) and external leases (ZooKeeper / etcd / Kubernetes Lease). Bully and Ring are worth knowing mainly to see why they are not enough; the book also leaves election under Byzantine faults untouched.
  • The dispute: do you even want a leader? Dynamo-style leaderless designs have none of this chapter's problems — no leader to elect, hence no two leaders. The price is moving conflict resolution onto the read and write paths (vector clocks, read repair, CRDTs): the complexity does not vanish, it just gets paid elsewhere.

Takeaways

1. The core claim: electing a leader is consensus (swapping "n nodes negotiate every operation" for "one node orders them"), yet every algorithm engineers use is weaker and cheaper — promising only eventual convergence on one leader, never that two never coexist.

2. The two failure modes are wildly asymmetric: no leader is a loud stall (recoverable); two leaders are a silent fork (usually not automatically recoverable).

3. Bully: highest live rank leads, no shared state needed; three blind spots — ignores fitness, a flapping top rank livelocks, and O(n²) messages.

4. Two cheap optimisations: successor lists (handover costs one timeout) and candidate sets (messages drop to O(k²), at the price of a new single point).

5. Ring collects the roster around a lap — linear messages, but the ring needs upkeep; Invitation simply allows coexisting groups and merges later, trading the single leader for availability and settling up at merge time.

6. Stability is the through-line: re-election costs stopped writes, log catch-up and reconnects, hundreds of milliseconds to seconds each. The rule is never re-elect while the incumbent can renew, plus randomised timeouts to avoid split votes.

7. The safety boundary is outside the election: give each term a monotonic epoch and fence at the storage layer, converting "there must never be two leaders" into "there may be two, but only one can write."

8. Real systems: etcd and Kafka KRaft use Raft (100ms heartbeat, 1s election timeout), ZooKeeper uses Zab, Kubernetes races for a Lease and says outright that it does not fence; in practice re-elections are triggered by slow disks and CPU contention far more often than by crashes.
