What is this chapter about?

You type a line into a chat box and something writes back like an assistant. This chapter is about the steps between a machine that has read most of the written world and that assistant — and those steps have almost nothing to do with making it know more.

A strange thing first

The freshly built machine already knows a great deal, but it does not answer you. Ask it "how do I soft-boil an egg" and it may well continue: "how do I soft-boil an egg? how do I fry one? how do I…" — because all it ever learned was to continue: see half a sentence, guess the rest. And on the internet, a question is very often followed by another question. It isn't unable to answer; nobody ever told it that being asked means answering.

Why the old way was hard

So write rules? The trouble is that "a good answer" cannot be written down as rules: lead with the conclusion or not, how long, ask a clarifying question or not, when to refuse outright — the answer changes with every request. It is the kind of thing you recognise instantly but cannot specify.

How it is actually done

Step one: have people demonstrate. Pay people to write good answers to a batch of questions by hand, and let the machine imitate them — like a new hire reading a folder of examples. The catch is plain: demonstrations are slow and expensive to write, and tens of thousands of them still cannot cover everything people ask.

Step two: have people judge. Let the machine write several versions of the same answer, and ask a person only which one is better. Picking is ten times faster than writing, and two people agree far more often about "which is better" than about "what score does it deserve". Feed those picks to a second machine and you get a scorer: hand it an answer, it hands back a number.

Step three: practise against the scorer. The machine writes, the scorer grades, the machine shifts a little towards higher scores, over and over. People no longer sit in the loop — their judgment already lives inside the scorer.

One more thing matters: a leash. It is not allowed to drift far from what it was. Without the leash it learns to please the scorer rather than do the job — discovering, say, that longer answers score higher, and padding every reply from then on.

Talking well isn't enough: memory and outside help

It can only look at one small stack of paper at a time, so a long conversation stops fitting: either the earlier turns get boiled down into a summary, or the important things you once said get written into a notebook and looked up when needed. It also can't do big arithmetic and doesn't know today's news — so it has to learn one more habit: when the job is beyond it, ask for a tool. The system looks something up or computes it, hands the result back, and the machine carries on.

One honest cost: the same training that makes it obedient also makes it eager to agree — saying "you're right" where it should say "your premise is wrong". Flattery is the shadow of that leash. You cannot remove it, only manage it.

One line to remember

What it has read decides what it knows; these later steps decide how it is willing to talk to you — and since "well said" has no right answer, the only way in is people picking, again and again, between two versions.

In one sentence

You think the hard part of ChatGPT is making the model smarter; in fact the knowledge is largely fixed by the end of pretraining. What this chapter actually solves is an objective mismatch — pretraining teaches "predict the next token", while the product needs "follow the instruction, answer the way people prefer, refuse when it must". The answer is three stages (pretraining → supervised fine-tuning → preference alignment), and the engineering intuition behind it fits in one line: when "good" cannot be written as a loss function, turn human pairwise comparisons into a scorer you can optimise against.

Glossary

  • Base model: a model trained only to predict the next token over huge amounts of text, with no instruction tuning yet.
  • Token: the unit a model reads and writes; context length and billing are both counted in tokens.
  • RLHF: reinforcement learning from human feedback — train a reward model on human preferences, then push the model toward higher reward.
  • Reward model (RM): takes a prompt plus a reply and returns one scalar: roughly "how much would a person like this".
  • Preference data: several replies to the same prompt, ranked by people — data of the form "A beats B".
  • PPO: a common RL algorithm that only allows small policy updates per step, which keeps training stable.
  • KL penalty: a measure of how far the new model's output distribution has drifted from a reference model; added to the objective, it discourages drift.
  • Reward hacking: the model learns to please the reward model instead of doing the job — padding length, piling on boilerplate.
  • Context window: the total tokens a model can see at once; system prompt, history and retrieved text all compete for it.
  • KV cache: cached intermediate vectors so each new token doesn't recompute the whole history; saves compute, costs GPU memory.
  • Tool / function calling: the model emits a structured call request that the system executes, feeding the result back into the context.
  • Prompt injection: instructions hidden inside external content (a web page, a document) that trick the model into obeying them.
  • Alignment tax: capability the model loses on other tasks as a side effect of alignment.

What the chapter is solving

Pretraining has exactly one objective: given what came before, predict the next token — so what it learns is what internet text tends to look like next, and on the internet a question is often followed by another question, an ad, a table of contents. The characteristic failure of a base model is therefore not "can't", it is answering beside the point, or continuing the list of questions.

The second difficulty is that "good" cannot be defined. Translation has a reference; code has tests. A good resignation letter has infinitely many forms, and its quality depends on length, tone, whether to ask a clarifying question, whether it oversteps — none of which reduces to a rule or a single labelled answer.

The third is that safety becomes a system requirement for the first time: a fluent model states harmful content just as fluently. The failure mode is being wrong fluently, not throwing an error.

What happens if you skip all this? You get a knowledgeable model nobody can use: unstable formatting, instruction-following by luck, and one sentence away from saying what it shouldn't. The whole chapter is about turning "can talk" into "gets things done and stays inside the lines".

The core, explained

1. Three stages, three different purchases

Pretraining buys knowledge and language: unlabelled text at the trillion-token scale, and the overwhelming majority of the compute. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) buys format and intent: human-written "instruction + ideal reply" demonstrations drag the output distribution onto the "answering" side. It is cheap and fast, and its ceiling is obvious — demonstrations must be written one at a time, and they can only say "this is right", never "that one is worse".

Preference alignment buys ranking: people write nothing, they only pick the better of several replies the model produced itself. Throughput is an order of magnitude higher, and the signal is exactly the one SFT cannot give. The design in one line: write demonstrations while you can, and switch to picking when you can't.

2. The reward model: compressing an undefinable "good" into one number

The recipe: sample several replies per prompt (commonly 4–9), have people rank them, and train a scoring model on those pairs with a blunt objective — the chosen reply must score above the rejected one.

Why comparisons rather than scores? Because absolute scores are not comparable across people: the same reply gets a 3 from a strict annotator and a 5 from a lenient one, and the noise swamps the signal, while agreement on "is A or B better" is far higher. This is the one piece of methodology worth taking away: when no absolute standard exists, step back and ask only for relative judgments.

The cost lives in the same place: the reward model is only a finite fit to human preference, and its own biases — toward length, toward pretty formatting, toward a confident tone — are amplified faithfully by the next step.

3. PPO, and the leash

The RL loop is: the policy model generates → the reward model scores → the weights move a small step toward higher scores. PPO's "small step" matters, but the KL penalty in the objective matters more: it measures how far the new output distribution has drifted from the frozen SFT reference, and charges for the distance.

Without it, the model attacks the reward model rather than pleasing people: if long answers score higher it pads endlessly, if boilerplate scores higher every sentence becomes boilerplate — while other capabilities quietly degrade, a loss known as the alignment tax. The KL coefficient is therefore the knob that matters most here: too small and it drifts, too large and it barely learns.

A sense of cost: in Microsoft's published DeepSpeed-Chat numbers, a 13B model finishes all three stages on 8 A100s in about 13.6 hours, of which PPO alone takes 10.8. The bulk of alignment compute is not reward-model training, it is that sample–score–update loop — which is also the heaviest to engineer: policy, reference, reward and value models all sit in GPU memory at once.

4. Memory in a conversation: the context window is a hard constraint

The naive approach re-assembles the whole history into the prompt every turn. The problem isn't forgetting, it is arithmetic: 500 tokens a turn and 20 turns is 10k tokens; attention cost grows with the square of length (double the length, roughly quadruple that part of the cost) while KV-cache memory grows linearly and every concurrent session holds its own copy — what you hit first is usually not the context limit but the concurrency your GPU memory allows.

So real systems split "memory" into layers: the system prompt (fixed, cacheable), recent turns verbatim (the last k, preserving tone and reference), compressed history (a rolling summary of everything earlier), plus an optional long-term memory: stable user preferences written to external storage and retrieved on demand.

Two traps that get overlooked: summarisation is lossy and irreversible — what it drops cannot be asked for later; and information sitting in the middle of a long context is more likely to be ignored, so put what matters first or last.

5. Tool calling: outsourcing what it cannot do

Three things a model is structurally bad at: events after its training cutoff, exact computation, and private data it has never seen. One answer covers all three — have it emit a structured call request, let the system execute it, feed the result back into the context, and let generation continue.

Three things to watch in production. Latency: every tool round-trip is one full forward pass plus the tool's RTT, so two or three of them take you from a few hundred milliseconds to several seconds; cap the number of rounds and fire independent calls in parallel. Reliability: models call the wrong tool and invent arguments, so validate arguments against a schema and have retries and a fallback script. Trust boundary: once tool output is in the context it sits next to the user's instructions — this is exactly where prompt injection comes in, so external content must be labelled by origin and never granted instruction authority.

6. Safety and evaluation: signing off with no right answer

Safety is three layers stacked: refusal behaviour trained in with safety preference data, an input-side classifier that blocks clearly disallowed requests, and an output-side pass on the way out. Streaming makes the third layer hard — the text is already on the screen and retracting it is expensive, so you either validate in delayed chunks or accept the "emit then withdraw" experience.

Evaluation has to be replaced too: perplexity barely correlates with user satisfaction. The workable combination is human pairwise comparison (blind A/B, ranked by win rate or Elo) + LLM-as-judge (the same pairwise verdicts at scale — cheap and repeatable, but with position bias and a length preference that has to be corrected, e.g. by swapping order) + red teaming + online signals (thumbs-up rate, regeneration rate, human-handoff rate).

And one product-level trade-off you cannot dodge: over-refusal. Raise the safety threshold and violations fall, but the share of legitimate requests that get refused rises — the most typical "hurts at both ends" curve in alignment.

Why it matters / in real systems

This skeleton reaches well past chatbots: every product whose quality only a human can judge looks like this — copy generation, coding assistants, support QA, AI review. Fix format with demonstrations, fix preference with pairwise comparisons, scale human judgment with a stand-in scorer, then watch like a hawk for the day the stand-in gets over-optimised.

The most valuable sentence in an interview: translate "the model answers badly" into "what went wrong with our preference signal". Saying "this bad case isn't missing knowledge, it's a reward model that prefers long answers, so I'd add a batch of 'short and correct beats long and correct' pairs" carries far more weight than naming architectures.

Pitfalls and open arguments

  • Thinking RLHF teaches knowledge. Knowledge is set during pretraining; alignment changes behaviour and expression, adding almost no facts. What it doesn't know, alignment can't fix.
  • Thinking SFT is enough. A demonstration can say "this is right" but never "that is worse" — and writing one costs roughly ten times what picking a winner costs.
  • Forgetting the KL constraint. Optimisation without a leash slides into reward hacking: answers get longer, boilerplate piles up, the metric climbs while the experience sinks.
  • Treating the reward model as truth. Over-optimising a fitted stand-in must diverge — Goodhart's law in textbook form: once a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.
  • Treating the context window as a disk. It is a workbench, wiped after the turn; cross-session memory must be stored and fetched explicitly, and users should be able to inspect and delete it.
  • Underrating retraction under streaming. By the time disallowed text is appearing character by character, blocking it is too late — the product's shape adds a hard constraint to safety design.
  • Signing off on perplexity or single-point scores. The former barely correlates with satisfaction; the latter isn't comparable across annotators.
  • Ignoring sycophancy and over-refusal as twins. Both come from the same source: the model learns "what people will like", and people like being agreed with while annotators reward cautious replies. They are not bugs, they are what this objective produces; only data mix and dedicated evals hold them down.
  • Period limits. The chapter takes the PPO three-stage pipeline as its spine. Around its publication, DPO-style methods that skip a separate reward model, and RLAIF where a model does the labelling, had already displaced much of it, and reasoning models put automatically verifiable rewards back into RL. The underlying constraint hasn't moved: open-ended generation has no single right answer, so quality can only be defined by comparison.

Takeaways

1. The chapter solves an objective mismatch: pretraining teaches "predict the next token"; the product needs instruction-following, preferred answers and refusal.

2. A base model's characteristic failure isn't inability, it's continuing the list of questions — it learned to continue, not to answer.

3. The three stages buy different things: knowledge, then format and intent, then "which version is better".

4. The methodology to keep: with no absolute standard, ask only for relative judgments — people agree far more on "A or B" than on "what score".

5. The reward model is a stand-in for people, and its biases get amplified faithfully by the step that follows.

6. The KL penalty is the leash on that stand-in: too loose and you get reward hacking, too tight and nothing is learned — the alignment tax lives in that tension.

7. Compute: pretraining dominates overall, but inside alignment the expensive part is PPO's sample–score–update loop.

8. Memory is not one technique but four layers: system prompt + last k turns verbatim + rolling summary + retrieval on demand.

9. Tool calling covers the training cutoff, exact computation and private data — at the price of pushing the trust boundary into the context, where prompt injection enters.

10. Sign-off means pairwise comparison + LLM judges + red teaming + online signals; the safety threshold and over-refusal are two ends of one curve.
