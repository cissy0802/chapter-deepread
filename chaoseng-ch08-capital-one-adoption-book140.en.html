What is this chapter about?

You tap your card for a coffee and, a second or two later, the till beeps. Behind that beep is a bank's entire system. This chapter asks: how does a bank dare to break its own systems on purpose, for practice?

A bank is not a video site. When a video stalls, people grumble. When a bank fails, someone can't pay, and the regulator wants to know why. So "randomly switch off machines in production" sounds, inside a bank, like "cause an incident on purpose." Capital One's answer was not "then we won't." It was a slower road where every step leaves a paper trail.

An analogy: learning to drive

Nobody puts a new driver on the motorway on day one. First the practice yard, then quiet streets, then the motorway — and at each stage an instructor signs off on what you practised and how it went.

A bank breaking its systems for practice follows the same order: get fluent in the test environment, write down the result of every drill, then use that record to earn the right to move closer to the real thing. The sign-off book is exactly what regulators and auditors want to see.

Why the old way was hard

The bank's old method was the surprise inspection: another team quietly broke something, unannounced, to see whether the people on call would notice and fix it. That measures real skill, but it has two flaws. The tested team feels like it's sitting an exam — fail and you get blamed, so nobody volunteers their weak spots. And it happened only a few times a year while the system changed every week, so each result went stale fast.

The core idea: three moves

  • From exam to experiment. No more outsiders ambushing you. The team that owns the system writes down its own guess first: "If this part breaks, customers shouldn't notice." Then it checks — and if the guess was wrong, it fixes the system.
  • Build the drill into the release line. Every new version, like a product leaving a factory, gets "broken a little" in the test environment automatically. If it can't cope, it doesn't ship.
  • Let the record write itself. Every drill automatically logs who ran it, when, what was broken and what happened. When auditors come, nobody scrambles to produce paperwork — the ledger was always there.

What it buys / how to choose

Drills go from "a big exam once a year" to "a check-up on every release," so problems are stopped before launch — and once enough records pile up, you have the standing to ask for small, careful drills in the real environment.

One honest cost: a test environment is never the real one — no real crowds, no real peaks — and some failures only appear for real, so practising in testing is the starting line, not the finish.

Remember it in one line

In a regulated industry, chaos runs on a ledger, not on nerve: make the drill a routine check on every release in the test environment, let each drill leave its own record, and trade those records, step by step, for permission to get closer to production.

In one sentence

You'd think heavy regulation means "no chaos engineering." In fact what regulators want — evidence that you can survive failure — is exactly what chaos experiments are best at producing. Capital One's path has three steps: from blind resiliency testing run by an outside team, to chaos experiments whose hypotheses the application team writes itself, to experiments embedded in the CI/CD pipeline so every release runs them and records them automatically. On that path, "start outside production" is not a compromise — it is how you earn permission to enter production.

Glossary

  • Regulated industry: banking, payments, insurance, healthcare — sectors under continuous government supervision. Major outages must be reported; changes must be traceable.
  • Compliance: demonstrating that your practices meet laws and internal policy. The emphasis is on being able to prove it, not just doing it.
  • Audit trail: a time-ordered record of actions — who, when, what changed, with what result. Auditors verify against it.
  • Non-production: dev, QA, staging and other environments with no real customers. Safe, but neither the load nor the data is real.
  • CI/CD pipeline: the automated chain of build, test and deploy steps that runs after code is committed.
  • Gate: a checkpoint in the pipeline; fail it and the build may not be promoted to the next environment.
  • Blind test: a drill where the tested team doesn't know when or what will be injected, so it measures real detection and response.
  • Steady-state hypothesis: a prior statement of what key metrics look like when the system is healthy; the experiment checks whether it still holds under injected failure (Ch3, principle one).
  • Blast radius: the worst-case extent an experiment can affect.
  • Game day: a scheduled day on which a team runs failure drills together and debriefs.

The problem this chapter solves

Start with the constraints. A bank outage is not just a UX problem: a card authorization has to come back within a second or two, and a failure means someone standing at a till unable to pay. Major disruptions must be reported to regulators, and every production change goes through change management with an approval record. When the book was written Capital One was moving its business wholesale onto the public cloud — its dependencies suddenly became more numerous and newer, and experience alone could no longer say what happens when a given component breaks.

Here Ch3's principle three, "run experiments in production," collides directly with policy: in an environment where every change needs approval, deliberately injecting failure looks like causing an incident on purpose.

What happens if you don't solve it? Two failure modes. One is paper drills: a disaster recovery failover once or twice a year, a checklist ticked, proof that "we have a plan" but not that "the plan works" — while the system changes weekly and each conclusion quietly expires. The other is going ahead without a mandate: one self-inflicted incident, and chaos engineering becomes a forbidden phrase at that company.

So the question is: under the constraint that every step must be explainable to outsiders, how do you get chaos engineering started and walk it, step by step, towards production?

The core, explained

1. Blind testing: first it tests people and process

Capital One's starting point wasn't chaos engineering but blind resiliency testing: a separate team injects failure into an application, and the tested team doesn't know the time or the form in advance. It tests three things — do the alerts fire, does on-call pick up, does the runbook actually work.

It has real value: in an announced drill everyone is already sitting in front of a screen, so you can't measure real detection latency; a blind test can. But its structural flaws are just as clear:

  • It's an exam, not an experiment. Results arrive as "you failed," and the tested team's instinct is to defend itself rather than expose problems.
  • It's one-off. It depends on a central team organizing by hand, so frequency stays low; the system changes and the result expires.
  • Knowledge doesn't stick. The hypothesis belongs to the examiner; the tested team doesn't come away understanding its own system better.

2. The turn to chaos: from being examined to experimenting

The pivot is who states the hypothesis. Chaos experiments are written by the application team itself: "When the cache tier is unavailable, the success rate of transaction lookups stays within its steady-state range — it just gets slower." Then they verify it in a controlled environment; if the hypothesis is refuted, that's a defect found before launch.

That step changes three things: ownership returns to the people who know the system best; experiments are repeatable rather than one-off ambushes; and every experiment naturally carries a four-part record — hypothesis, injection, observation, conclusion. That last point is exactly what a regulated industry needs most: structured evidence, not a report written after the fact.

3. Chaos in CI/CD: evidence as a pipeline by-product

The chapter's most actionable step: make chaos experiments a pipeline stage. After a new version deploys to a non-production environment, the pipeline reads a chaos configuration checked in alongside the code (which environment, what to inject, for how long, what counts as a pass), injects the failure automatically and checks the steady-state hypothesis; if it holds, the build is promoted; if not, it's stopped and sent back to development.

This fixes both of blind testing's flaws. Frequency: from a few times a year to every release — the system changes, and the verification changes with it. Evidence: a pipeline already records who ran each step, when, on which version, with what result; write the experiment result into the same record and the auditors' material needs no separate report.

The hidden cost should be said out loud: experiments on a gate must be stable and repeatable. Put a flaky experiment on the gate and within weeks the team will learn to route around it — which is worse than nothing, because the record still says "verified."

4. What to watch for when designing experiments

The chapter lists specific cautions for designing experiments in a regulated environment. Condensed into four rules:

  • Observability before injection. If you can't see the steady-state metrics clearly, don't start — the experiment's verdict can be neither judged nor evidenced.
  • Tell everyone who might be startled. The operations center, on-call and the security team all need to know — in a bank, an unannounced anomaly can be treated as a real incident or even a security event, waking a whole response chain.
  • Write the abort and the rollback first. Every experiment must be stoppable immediately without a release, and the rollback steps rehearsed beforehand.
  • Grow the blast radius slowly; steer clear of money and customer data. Start with retryable, recoverable paths; failure modes that could corrupt funds or customer data are out of scope for early experiments.

5. Tooling, team and evangelism: surviving the first quarter

Tooling. Build or buy? The chapter gives criteria, not an answer: does it cover your platforms and failures at several layers (infrastructure, network, application), can teams self-serve, does it have guardrails and a one-click abort, does it plug into the pipeline, and can it produce records an auditor will accept. In a regulated industry that last one is a hard requirement.

Team. At the start, a central team builds the platform, sets standards and accompanies the first few experiments; at scale, ownership of experiments must return to application teams — the central team can't do them all and doesn't know the systems as well. The central team's end state is platform and coach, not executor.

Evangelism. Technology decides whether it can be done; culture decides whether anyone does it. Describe the defects experiments find as "incidents stopped before launch," let people try it hands-on at game days, and share wins across the company again and again. The biggest obstacle to chaos engineering in a bank is not tooling but the instinctive fear carried by the words "break things on purpose."

Concrete choices:

  • Just starting, policy not yet on side: don't open with production. Pick a service with many dependencies that has just moved to the cloud, run a few hypothesis-driven experiments outside production and settle the record format — it's your bargaining chip for every later conversation.
  • A few dozen experiment records banked: put the most stable ones on a pipeline gate, moving from "when someone remembers" to "on every release."
  • Ready for production: take the records to risk and audit; start with read-only paths and retryable writes, with auto-abort; paths that affect the correctness of money come last.

Why it matters / in real systems

Three sentences that can change a review outcome. One: the regulator is not chaos engineering's opponent but a buyer of its evidence. Two: the value of starting outside production is not safety but banking permission. Three: put experiments in the pipeline and the evidence writes itself.

Asked in an interview "how do you do chaos engineering in a regulated industry?": "we can't touch production, so we just do DR drills" is one level. "Start with hypothesis-driven experiments outside production, put the stable ones on a CI/CD gate with results flowing into the audit record, then trade those records for small-blast-radius production experiments" is another — it shows you know the hard part here is permission, not technology.

Pitfalls and debates

  • Treating non-production as the destination. Staging lacks real load, real dependencies and real data distributions; failures like connection-pool saturation and retry storms are invisible there. Non-production is where you bank evidence, not the whole of verification.
  • Chaos for the auditors. When "one record per version" becomes a KPI, teams pick experiments that always pass — the record looks great and nothing was tested. The check: has the gated experiment stopped anything in the past quarter?
  • Carrying blind-test culture into chaos. If results still arrive as "you failed," teams will hide problems. The output of an experiment is knowledge, not a grade.
  • Forgetting security and the operations center. In a financial institution, an unannounced anomaly can trigger a real incident or even a security response, costing far more than the experiment itself.
  • Flaky gate experiments. An experiment that passes and fails at random gets bypassed quickly, while the record still says "verified."
  • Limits of its time, and what came after. The chapter was written before regulators asked specifically for resilience testing, so chaos engineering had to win over policy on its own. The wind has since shifted: the UK PRA / FCA operational-resilience rules require firms to set "impact tolerances" for important business services and test against severe but plausible scenarios; the EU's DORA, in force from 2025, requires financial entities to run a digital operational resilience testing programme. On tooling, the major clouds now offer managed fault-injection services. What stays the same is this chapter's path: evidence first, scope later.

Key takeaways

1. Core claim: regulators want evidence that you can survive failure, and chaos experiments naturally produce it — regulation isn't a reason not to do chaos, it's a reason to take a different road.

2. Constraint: bank outages must be reported and changes approved, so "experiments in production" looks like causing an incident on purpose; principle three moves to the last step.

3. Starting point, blind testing: an outside team ambushes and measures real detection and response — but it's an exam, one-off, and the knowledge doesn't stick.

4. The pivot: the application team writes its own hypothesis; experiments are repeatable and naturally carry a structured hypothesis–injection–observation–verdict record.

5. The key step: make experiments a CI/CD gate with config checked in alongside the code; frequency goes from a few times a year to every release, and evidence becomes a by-product of the pipeline.

6. Cautions: observability before injection, warn operations and security in advance, write abort and rollback first, grow blast radius slowly and avoid money and customer data.

7. Organization: a central team builds the platform and coaches, ending as platform + coach while experiments belong to app teams; evangelism frames defects as "incidents stopped."

8. Honest limits: non-production can't see scale failures and is not the destination; gate experiments must be stable, or a "verified" record is more dangerous than none.
