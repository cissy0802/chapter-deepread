What is this chapter about?

Plenty of online services hand you a usage cap: the free tier converts five files a day, that forum won't let you post twenty comments in a minute. This chapter is about the little part behind that cap — the rate limiter: a doorman who enforces "at most so many entries per person per minute" and turns away anyone over the line.

Something odd first

You probably assume the door is there to keep bad actors out. Most of what it turns away is well-meaning. More precisely: a well-meaning program with a bug — some customer's software has a loop that forgot to stop and is firing thousands of requests a second. They don't know, they meant no harm, and the effect is identical to an attack. The limiter's day job is to stop one person's slip from becoming everyone's outage.

Why the old world was hard

With no door, everything runs first-come-first-served: whoever shouts loudest gets served. That sounds fair and is brutal in practice — one runaway program can eat a service's entire capacity in seconds, and thousands of perfectly well-behaved users get locked out together. There's a blunter cost too: many services call somebody else's metered API behind the scenes, billed per call, and a loop that won't stop can burn a month's budget overnight.

The mechanism: four ways to guard a door

1. The candy bowl (token bucket). Put a bowl by the door. Two candies drop in every second; the bowl holds at most 40. To get in, take one; empty bowl, no entry. The nice part: if things were quiet for a while the bowl fills up, and then 40 people can walk in at once — it accommodates a short rush, yet over any long stretch the average is still two per second.

2. The queue (leaky bucket). One line at the door. However many people pile up outside, exactly two per second are let in — never more. The pace inside is perfectly even; the price is that once the line is full, latecomers can't even join it and are simply turned away.

3. Reset on the hour (fixed window). The doorman holds a counter that zeroes every minute; five per minute and no more. Simplest possible thing, with one hole: if five people squeeze in at 0:55 and five more at 1:05, then across those few seconds ten actually got in — twice the rule.

4. Look back one minute (sliding window). Change "this minute" to "the minute counting backwards from now," and the hole closes. The cost is that the doorman must remember when each person walked in, and with a crowd that ledger gets thick and expensive. The compromise is to keep no ledger at all and just carry over a fraction of last minute's headcount by overlap — not exactly right, but far cheaper.

How to choose

Want to leave users room to be occasionally hasty? Candy bowl. Need the pace downstream to be perfectly even? The queue. Just want a crude barrier up fast? Reset-on-the-hour takes half an hour to write.

One honest cost: the door itself can break — and when it does, do you let everyone in or shut everyone out? There's no standard answer, but every team shipping a rate limiter has to settle it in advance.

Remember this

A rate limiter is a doorman, and what it mostly guards against isn't attackers — it's one person's slip taking everyone down. Four postures: candy bowl (absorbs bursts), queue (even pace), reset-on-the-hour (cheap but leaks at the boundary), look-back-a-minute (accurate but expensive). Which one you pick comes down to whether you can tolerate bursts.

In one line

You think a rate limiter is a wall against attackers. It is really a contract for allocating capacity — the first question it answers isn't "how do I keep bad actors out" but "when there isn't enough to go around, who starves first." The value of this chapter isn't the five algorithms either; algorithms are the easy part. The hard parts are three: how much to allow, which layer to enforce it at, and how to make dozens of stateless limiters agree on one counter.

Glossary

  • Rate limiting / throttling: capping how many requests are allowed per unit of time, rejecting or deferring the rest.
  • QPS (queries per second): the usual unit for traffic volume.
  • API gateway: the single layer every external request passes through before reaching services; it usually also does authentication, SSL termination and IP allowlisting.
  • Middleware: code sitting between clients and business services that handles every request uniformly.
  • Redis: an in-memory key-value store with sub-millisecond reads and writes; used here as the counter because it's fast and natively supports "expire at time T."
  • Atomic operation: an action that either happens entirely or not at all, with nothing slipping in halfway. Redis's INCR (increment and return the new value) is atomic.
  • Race condition: two operations concurrently read-modify-write the same data; the later write clobbers the earlier one and an update vanishes.
  • HTTP 429: the status code 429 Too Many Requests, how a server says "you've been limited."
  • DoS (denial of service): exhausting a service's resources with volume so legitimate users can't get through.
  • Burst: a short stretch of traffic far above the average, like the first seconds of a flash sale.
  • Sticky session: a load balancer pinning one client's requests to one server.

What this chapter is solving

The book opens with three reasons. Stopping resource starvation — without limits, a handful of callers can consume a service's entire capacity and everyone else goes dark. Cost — many requests fan out to metered third-party APIs (credit checks, payments, health records), and a limiter is the first cap on that bill. Preventing server overload — keeping bots and misbehaving users from crowding out real traffic. Twitter caps tweets at 300 per 3 hours; Google Docs APIs default to 300 read requests per user per 60 seconds. Those numbers aren't arbitrary — they're the published price of "how much shared resource one person may hold."

What happens without it? Do the arithmetic: an API sits steadily at 5,000 QPS; one customer's retry logic is wrong — resend immediately on failure, no backoff — and within seconds their errors compound into 50,000 QPS hitting you. Database connection pools fill, and the other 99% of well-behaved customers all get 503s together. What flattened the system wasn't an attack; it was a loop with no backoff.

The conditions the book lists in "establish design scope" are worth memorizing verbatim, because they drive every trade-off later: accurately limit excess requests; low latency (it sits on every request's critical path); low memory footprint; distributed (many limiter nodes sharing one quota); clear feedback to throttled users; and high fault tolerance — when the limiter itself has problems, it must not take the whole system down with it. That last one is the easiest to forget and the most dangerous.

The core, explained

First decide: which layer does the door go in

The book offers three positions and rejects the first outright. Client-side limiting is unreliable — client code can be modified, requests can be forged; handing the quota to the caller to police is not policing. Server-side limiting lives in business code with the richest context, but every service reimplements it. Middleware pulls the concern out into its own layer between clients and services — in a microservice architecture that position is usually the API gateway, which already does authentication, SSL termination and IP allowlisting, so folding limiting in is the natural move.

How to choose? The book's criteria are practical: check what your current stack supports and whether the algorithm you want can be implemented at that layer; if you're already on microservices with a gateway doing auth, put it in the gateway; if you intend to build your own, accept that building a rate-limiting service takes time — a third-party gateway is money traded for time.

Token bucket: the only one that welcomes bursts

A bucket holds up to b tokens; the system refills it at a fixed rate and stops once it's full. Each request takes one token — got one, you pass; none left, you're dropped. Two parameters: bucket size (the maximum burst) and refill rate (the long-run average).

The intuition is in one sentence: the tokens you've saved up are the burst you're allowed. A customer quiet for 20 seconds has a full bucket and can fire 40 requests in the next second — but stretched over time, their rate stays pinned to the refill rate. That's exactly the behaviour most public APIs want: being occasionally hasty is fine, being persistently fast is not.

How many buckets? The book lists three granularities, each with a use: one per API endpoint (posting is more precious than reading, so quotas differ); one per IP (for anonymous traffic); and one global bucket (the whole system tolerates at most 10,000 QPS — the last line of self-defence). Real systems stack all three.

Leaky bucket: a flat pace, at the cost of bursts

The leaky bucket is a FIFO queue: an arriving request is enqueued if there's room and dropped otherwise, while the other end pulls requests off at a constant rate. Two parameters: queue size and outflow rate.

The difference from a token bucket fits in a line: the token bucket governs "can you come in," the leaky bucket governs "the pace inside." A token bucket lets 40 rush in and be processed together; a leaky bucket, however hard it rains outside, still does two per second. So when the downstream has fixed throughput and can't take a shock — an ageing database, a third-party API billed by concurrency — the leaky bucket fits better.

The book states the cost plainly: a burst fills the queue with old requests, and if those can't be drained, newly arriving and possibly more valuable requests get rejected — the queue turns into a device that prioritises stale work, while the user on the other end sees a response that took eight seconds to come back.

Three counting windows: from cheapest to most exact

The fixed window counter is the obvious one: slice time into whole minutes (or hours), give each window a counter, increment per request, reject once the threshold is hit, reset at the next window. Memory-cheap, easy to reason about, easy to build.

Its flaw is at the boundary, and the book's example is crisp: with a limit of 5 per minute, if 5 requests land in 2:00:30–2:01:00 and 5 more in 2:01:00–2:01:30, then across the rolling minute 2:00:30–2:01:30 exactly 10 got through — twice the quota. An attacker only has to aim traffic at either side of the boundary to collect double reliably.

The sliding window log closes that hole: instead of bucketing by the clock it records a timestamp per request (typically in a Redis sorted set). On arrival it removes timestamps older than the window start, adds the current one, and passes if the remaining count is under the threshold. No rolling window can ever exceed the limit — this is the only truly exact algorithm. The cost is glaring: memory grows with request count, and the book specifically notes that a rejected request still leaves its timestamp behind. For an endpoint currently under abuse that property is dangerous: memory grows precisely when it least should.

The sliding window counter is the hybrid, and the usual engineering compromise: keep only the previous and current window counts and weight the previous one by overlap — current count + previous count × overlap. The book's example: limit 7 per minute, 7 requests last minute, 5 so far this minute, currently 30% into the window, so the weighted value is 5 + 7 × 70% = 9.7, floored to 9, which exceeds 7 — reject.

It's memory-cheap (two numbers per key) and smooths spikes, but it is an approximation: the formula assumes the previous window's requests were evenly distributed, and real traffic never is. How far off? Cloudflare's measured number is below.

Two hard bones in a distributed setting

Limiting on one machine is a giveaway; going multi-node changes everything. The two problems the book raises are where this chapter's real depth is.

Race conditions. The naive implementation is read-count, add-one, write-back. Two requests arrive nearly together: both read 3, both compute 4, both write 4 — two requests came in and the counter rose by one. Under load that loss recurs and the threshold means nothing. The remedies are in Table 3 below; here just note that locking is the most intuitive and the worst — the book says outright that it will significantly slow the system down, and the limiter sits on every request's critical path.

Synchronization. To scale horizontally the limiter must be stateless, but a client's requests get balanced across different limiter nodes, each keeping its own books, so the quota is over-issued. The book explicitly rejects the lazy fix of sticky sessions — "neither scalable nor flexible": lose a node and every client state pinned to it is gone; scale in or out and traffic reshuffles, scrambling quotas. The right answer is to move state into centralized storage (Redis) so all limiters read and write one counter.

Rules, responses and observability

Limiting rules are usually config files on disk, pulled periodically by workers into a cache the middleware reads (the book's sample format comes from Lyft's open-source rate-limiting component: one rule declaring "domain = auth, key = login, 5 per minute"). Changing a threshold then needs no code change and no restart.

Rejected requests get 429 Too Many Requests; where the business allows deferral, they can instead be queued for later. More important are the three response headers — the contract between limiter and caller: X-Ratelimit-Limit (what the quota is), X-Ratelimit-Remaining (how many are left), X-Ratelimit-Retry-After (how long until you may return). Without them a client can only discover its limit by hitting the wall; with them, a well-behaved client slows down on its own.

Monitoring watches two things: whether the rules are too strict (lots of legitimate requests killed), and whether the algorithm fails under bursts — if real users get mass-rejected during a flash sale, it's time to switch to a token bucket.

One more choice, mentioned at the end of the chapter and worth pulling out: hard vs soft limiting. Hard means the threshold can never be exceeded; soft allows a small overshoot for a short time. Why it matters / in real systems

The rate limiter is a component nearly every backend engineer eventually touches and few get right on the first try. Getting it right means three things at once: an algorithm that matches the shape of your traffic, state that counts correctly across machines, and a failure mode that doesn't take the system with it. Every public API you use has one — and the choices differ a lot between companies, which is precisely the point: there is no best algorithm, only a matching one.

Field evidence · these claims aren't the book's — the companies stated them publicly
  • Stripe described its limiting stack on its engineering blog: token bucket underneath, four limiters in series — a request rate limiter capping requests per second per identity, a concurrent request limiter capping in-flight requests, and two load shedders that drop lower-priority traffic when the fleet is strained — validating "token bucket is the default for public APIs" and adding what the book omits: one limiter isn't enough. Stripe, "Scaling your API with rate limiters" ↗
  • Cloudflare published that the algorithm it chose to serve millions of domains is exactly the sliding window counter, along with a measurement of how good the approximation is: across 400 million requests from 270,000 distinct sources, only 0.003% were wrongly allowed or wrongly limited — a hard number showing an approximate algorithm is good enough on real traffic. Cloudflare, "How we built rate limiting capable of scaling to millions of domains," 2017 ↗
  • Shopify's public docs state the Admin API uses a leaky bucket: the REST API allows a bucket of 40 requests per app per store, replenishing at 2 per second, returning 429 with a Retry-After header when exceeded — a verbatim instance of "the leaky bucket fits when you need a steady outflow." Shopify Dev Docs, "REST Admin API rate limits" ↗
  • Amazon's API Gateway docs say plainly that it "throttles requests using the token bucket algorithm, where a token counts for a request," with defaults of 10,000 requests per second steady-state and 5,000 of burst capacity per account per Region — "refill rate sets the long-run average, bucket size sets the burst ceiling" is legible right there in those two numbers. Pitfalls & debates

  • Treating limiting as a security boundary. It stops one runaway client; it does not stop a distributed attack from hundreds of thousands of IPs each sending a few requests — that's the job of a WAF and the network layer. The book itself lists iptables-style IP-level measures as "limiting at a different level," which is the tell: application-layer limiting is one link, not the chain.
  • Limiting by IP misses both ways. A few thousand people behind one corporate egress IP get punished collectively, while mobile IPs shift often enough that changing address resets the quota. If you can limit by user or API key, don't limit by IP — reserve IP for anonymous traffic where identity is unavailable.
  • The limiter becomes a single point. The book lists "high fault tolerance" as a requirement but doesn't answer the question a real system must: when the counter store dies, do you fail open or fail closed? The prevailing choice is fail open — better to lose protection briefly than to let an auxiliary component kill the main path — though billing and compliance quotas may go the other way. That's an addition from outside the book, not one of its conclusions.
  • The approximation isn't universal. The sliding window counter assumes an even distribution in the previous window. Cloudflare's 0.003% shows it's fine on real mixed traffic, but on a billing or compliance quota that must never be exceeded it isn't acceptable — there you take the sliding window log and pay the memory.
  • The retry storm after a 429. If rejected clients retry immediately, all their retries align on the same second and form a second wave. Retry-After is the floor; what's actually needed is exponential backoff plus jitter. The book covers backoff and client caching, but this second-order effect — retries synchronizing — deserves its own warning.
  • Period limits. Written in 2020, this chapter is about static thresholds: a human picks the number and writes it into config. Since then the trend has been pushing limiting down into service-mesh sidecars, and toward adaptive limiting — adjusting admission from the downstream's observed latency and queueing rather than a guessed number. The five algorithms remain the building blocks, but the answer to "who sets the threshold" is changing.

Key takeaways

1. In one line: a rate limiter isn't a wall against attackers, it's the contract deciding who starves first when capacity runs short; what it stops day to day is a runaway retry loop, not an adversary.

2. Three reasons: prevent DoS-style resource starvation, cap metered third-party calls, prevent server overload. The requirement people forget: when the limiter itself has problems, it must not take the system down.

3. Three positions: the client is untrustworthy, in-app has the richest context, middleware / API gateway is the default under microservices.

4. Token bucket: bucket size sets the burst ceiling, refill rate sets the long-run average; the only algorithm that natively welcomes bursts, and what Stripe and AWS API Gateway both use.

5. Leaky bucket: FIFO queue plus constant outflow — steady output, no burst tolerance, and a burst fills the queue with stale requests. Shopify uses it.

6. Fixed window is cheapest but leaks twice the quota across the boundary; the sliding window log is exact and costliest (rejected requests occupy memory too); the sliding window counter is the compromise, measured by Cloudflare at only 0.003% misjudgement.

7. Two distributed traps: races (read-modify-write loses counts — use atomic increments or a Lua script, not a lock) and synchronization (no sticky sessions; put state in centralized Redis).

8. Give the whole contract: 429 plus X-Ratelimit-Limit / -Remaining / -Retry-After; keep rules in config pulled by workers so thresholds change without a deploy. The most valuable sentence in an interview is why you picked this algorithm because the business does or doesn't tolerate bursts — not a recital of five names.
