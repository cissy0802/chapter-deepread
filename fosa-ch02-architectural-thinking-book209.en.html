Architectural Thinking: Seeing Problems Like an Architect

Fundamentals of Software Architecture · Ch 2 · Mark Richards & Neal Ford · 2020

中文 →

What is this chapter about?

A brilliant programmer gets promoted to architect, and in the first week often discovers that the habits that made them great have quietly stopped working. Chapter 2 is about that change of mindset — how an architect looks at problems differently from a developer. The book names four shifts: how to work with developers, whether knowledge should be "deep" or "wide," how to think about trade-offs, and whether to keep writing code at all.

An analogy: the chief architect and the building crew

The old way works like this: the chief architect draws the blueprints in an office, throws them over to the building site, and leaves. On site, the crew finds a problem with the foundation or swaps a material, but the news never travels back, and the drawings and the real building drift further apart. The authors argue that when software architecture "doesn't work," it is usually because of this one-way street. The better way: the architect lives on site, talks to the crew every day, and the drawings change along with reality.

Know wide, not just deep

Picture three drawers in your head: things you know, things you know you don't know, and things you've never even heard of. A developer earns a living from the first drawer, and deeper is better. An architect gets more value from the second: knowing "there are five ways to solve this" beats mastering just one, because otherwise everything looks like a nail to your one hammer. The book also warns about a type of person who got burned by one old incident and now drags it into every new design, long after it stopped being relevant.

Every choice has a price tag

The book's example is an auction site: when someone places a bid, three back-office teams need to hear about it. You could use a loudspeaker — shout once, whoever wants to listen does, and adding a new team later changes nothing on the sender's side. Or you could give each team its own mailbox — more work, but you can see how many letters pile up in each box, add staff only where it's slow, and outsiders can't peek. Both are right; which is better depends on what you care about most. The authors' famous line: architecture is the part you can't just look up online.

Should the architect still write code?

Yes — but not the piece everyone else is waiting on. Architects get pulled into meetings constantly; if the whole team depends on the core code only they are writing, the team stalls. Better: run small experiments to test ideas, fix bugs, pay down old shortcuts, build little tools that check things automatically, and read lots of other people's code. One honest cost: the further you drift from daily coding, the rustier your instincts get, and that balance is never settled for good.

One line to remember

An architect's mind = two-way talk with developers + wide knowledge + find the price tag on every choice + write code without becoming the bottleneck.

In one sentence

You think becoming an architect means "going one level deeper technically." It is the opposite: you trade some depth for breadth. Chapter 2 of FoSA breaks "architectural thinking" into four things — making architecture and design a two-way collaboration, judging with breadth rather than depth, analyzing every choice as a trade-off, and keeping hands-on coding without becoming a bottleneck — plus one that runs through the rest of the book: translating business needs into architecture characteristics.

Glossary

  • Architecture characteristics: the non-functional things a system must achieve — scalability, availability, security — one of the four dimensions from Chapter 1.
  • Component: a chunk of code with a clear responsibility; the basic box an architect draws when laying out structure.
  • Message queue: middleware for passing messages asynchronously; a message put in is taken out by exactly one consumer.
  • Topic / publish-subscribe: the other style; a message sent once is delivered to every subscriber.
  • Contract: the agreed data format between two services, e.g. which fields a message carries.
  • Technical debt: shortcuts taken to ship faster that cost extra time to "repay" later.
  • POC (proof of concept): small experimental code written to show an approach is viable.
  • Critical path: the chain of tasks where any delay delays the whole project.

Where it sits

The first chapter of Part I, "Foundations," right after Chapter 1's definition of architecture and its two laws. Chapter 1 says what an architect does; this chapter says how an architect thinks. Chapter 3 on modularity and Chapters 4–7 on architecture characteristics build on this mindset. Real-world mapping: the jump from senior developer to architect, and the "why didn't you pick the other one?" question in every design review.

What problem this chapter solves

Many architects are simply "the strongest developer," promoted. The trouble is that the habits that made them a great developer — drilling into one technology as deep as it goes, personally writing the hardest core code, hunting for the single best solution — become liabilities in the architect role. Drill too deep and you can't see alternatives; guard the core code and the whole team slows down; obsess over "the best" and you ignore what each option costs. At the other extreme, an architect who only draws diagrams in a meeting room and throws them one way to developers never hears what changed on the ground, and the architecture documents quickly turn into decoration. The chapter's question: when the same person switches from developer to architect, how should their thinking change?

Core ideas, explained

① Architecture vs. design: the one-way street is the root of failure

The traditional split is a pipeline: the architect analyzes the business, picks architecture characteristics, chooses a style, and draws the component structure; then it is handed to developers, who do class design, UI design, and code. The book's verdict is sharp: that one-way arrow in the middle is the reason architecture doesn't work. Decisions are distorted by the time they reach developers, and what developers discover — a component split wrongly, a library that can't cope — never makes it back to the architect.

The fix isn't abolishing roles but tearing down the wall: architects and developers sit on the same (virtual) team. The architect explains decisions and their reasons to developers, keeps collecting feedback from the code, and revises iteration by iteration. That matches the agile rhythm — architecture is no longer a one-off "big design up front."

② Breadth over depth: the knowledge pyramid

The book describes a person's knowledge as a three-layer pyramid. At the top is "stuff you know" (technologies you use fluently); in the middle, "stuff you know you don't know" (you've heard of it and know what it's for, but haven't used it in depth); at the bottom, the biggest layer, "stuff you don't know you don't know" — the blind spot, where the perfect solution may be sitting unseen.

In plain terms: the top layer needs constant upkeep — unused knowledge goes stale. A developer's value lies in that top-layer depth; an architect's lies in the breadth of the top two layers combined. The authors' point is that for an architect, knowing that five solutions exist for a problem is more useful than being an expert in one. So switching roles means deliberately giving up some depth and spending the time moving the bottom layer into the middle — reading widely and learning what's out there.

Two common imbalances: trying to stay an expert in everything, which fails and burns you out; and stale expertise, mistaking what you learned years ago for current knowledge. The book also names an anti-pattern, the Frozen Caveman: an architect who once lived through an incident where the connection to Italy was lost, and since then asks in every review, "but what if we lose Italy?" — frozen by one old wound and projecting an irrelevant risk onto every new system.

③ Analyzing trade-offs: architecture is what you can't Google

The authors' slogan: architecture is the stuff you can't Google; plus the jab that programmers know the benefits of everything and the trade-offs of nothing. Architectural thinking means asking of every option, "what did it trade for what?" The book's signature example is an auction system: a Bid Producer must send each bid to three downstream services — Bid Capture, Bid Tracking, and Bid Analytics. Topic or queues?

The topic's advantages are architectural extensibility and decoupling: the producer just publishes and has no idea who's listening; tomorrow's Bid History service simply subscribes, and the producer doesn't change a line. Queues' advantages are threefold. First, independent monitoring and auto-scaling: each queue's depth is visible on its own, so if Analytics falls behind you scale only Analytics, whereas on a topic everyone shares one stream and you can't see per-consumer backlog. Second, heterogeneous contracts: if Analytics needs extra fields, only its queue's message format changes, while a topic forces one shared contract on all. Third, security: a queue delivers only to its intended receiver, but any service that can connect to a topic can listen in on bid data. The flip side: with queues the producer must know every downstream, so adding a service means changing the producer. Neither is right; one simply fits better with the characteristic you care about most — Chapter 1's first law, demonstrated.

④ Understanding business drivers

The fourth piece: translating what the business says into architecture characteristics. When the business says "we cannot go down on Black Friday," you should hear availability and elasticity; "three new markets next quarter" means scalability and configurability. That requires domain knowledge and a working relationship with business stakeholders. This chapter only names the skill; Chapters 4–7 unpack identifying, measuring, and scoping characteristics.

⑤ Balancing architecture and hands-on coding: avoid the bottleneck trap

The book's position: architects should keep coding, or they lose touch with technical reality and with developers' respect. But they must avoid the Bottleneck Trap: the architect claims code on the critical path (typically the underlying framework code), keeps getting pulled into meetings, and the whole team waits. The fix is to hand critical-path and framework code to the development team and have the architect take a slice of business functionality (a service or a screen) instead. The book lists ways to stay hands-on without blocking anyone:

  • Do frequent POCs — say, comparing two caching solutions in your actual context. And write them to production quality: POC code tends to stay in the repo and become the reference others copy.
  • Take technical-debt or architecture stories, leaving functional stories to developers — both sides are happier.
  • Fix bugs, which also exposes weak spots in the code base and the architecture.
  • Automate with small tools — command-line tools and analyzers that save the team repetitive work, including turning architecture rules into checks that run automatically (expanded as "fitness functions" in Chapter 6).
  • Do frequent code reviews, staying in touch with the code and catching deviations from architecture decisions on the spot.

Why it matters / in real systems

"Topic vs. queues" is not a textbook puzzle: every team that adopts messaging middleware argues about it at least once. How real systems evolved supports the book's point — when people wanted both sides' benefits, they built a new trade-off point, not a trade-off-free one.

Industry evidence · in the primary sources' own words
  • Apache Kafka (born at LinkedIn) states plainly in its official docs that each model has a strength and a weakness: queuing lets you divide processing across consumer instances and scale, but "queues aren't multi-subscriber—once one process reads the data it's gone"; publish-subscribe broadcasts but "has no way of scaling processing since every message goes to every subscriber." Kafka's answer is the consumer group: within a group it divides work like a queue; across groups it broadcasts like pub/sub. Apache Kafka docs, Introduction (v0.10.2 source on GitHub) ↗
  • ArchUnit (open-sourced by TNG) turns "architects write small tools to guard decisions" into a library: it describes itself as automatically testing architecture and coding rules — package and class dependencies, layers, cycles — "using any plain Java unit testing framework." That is the book's "automate the checks instead of sitting on the critical path," productized. TNG/ArchUnit README, GitHub ↗

Note that Kafka's "have both" isn't free: parallelism within a group is capped by the number of partitions, so create too few and you can't scale — the cost just moved somewhere else.

Pitfalls and debates

  • "Breadth first" ≠ not technical: the authors want a few areas of real depth plus a wide field of "knowing what exists," not buzzwords everywhere. In an interview, when asked "why X," naming two or three alternatives and their costs is the evidence of breadth.
  • Turning trade-off analysis into a pros list: writing only the upsides of the chosen option is the most common review failure. Writing the costs in both columns, as in Table 1, is what counts as analysis.
  • Every team has a Frozen Caveman: lessons expire with their context. Old incidents deserve memory, but ask, "does today's system still have that premise?"
  • The coding balance is the hardest part: don't code and you lose touch; code the core and you become the bottleneck. The book gives direction, not a ratio.
  • Limits and later developments: the chapter's "architecture vs. design" covers collaboration but offers no test for where the boundary lies; the 2025 second edition redraws it as a spectrum from strategic to tactical, filling that gap. The auction example also reflects classic messaging middleware; log-based systems like Kafka have partly dissolved Table 1's either/or.

Key takeaways

① Architectural thinking = two-way architecture/design collaboration + breadth + trade-off analysis + business understanding + coding without bottlenecking.

② The one-way "blueprint handoff" is why architecture fails: architects and developers belong on one team, with reasons flowing down and findings flowing up.

③ The knowledge pyramid: what you know / know you don't know / don't know you don't know; an architect's value is the breadth of the top two layers.

④ Knowing five solutions to a problem beats mastering one; switching roles means deliberately giving up some depth.

⑤ Beware stale expertise and the Frozen Caveman — one old incident hijacking every new design.

⑥ Architecture is what you can't Google: ask of every option what it trades for what.

⑦ Topic = extensibility, decoupling; queues = independent monitoring and scaling, heterogeneous contracts, security. The right pick depends on the characteristic you care most about.

⑧ Architects should code, but off the critical path: POCs (production quality), bug fixes, tech debt, automated checks, reviews.
