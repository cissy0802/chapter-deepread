Choosing the Appropriate Architecture Style: No Best Style, Only the Least-Worst Mix

Fundamentals of Software Architecture · Ch 18 · Mark Richards & Neal Ford · 2020

中文 →

What is this chapter about?

The previous chapters were a tour of a car show: sedans, off-roaders, trucks, sports cars, each good at something. This chapter answers the last question: which one do I actually buy? In software terms: for a new system, say the online ordering app of a sandwich chain, or a live auction website, what overall "shape" should it take?

A strange thing first: the fashionable model changes every few years

Fifteen years ago the advice was "the whole company shares one set of parts." Then it was "split everything into a hundred small services." Now some people say "one big block is actually fine." Nobody was stupid. The roads changed: tools got cheaper, businesses changed, and each generation's scars taught the next one something. So the book says: don't chase the fashionable model, look at your own road.

Why choosing is hard

Every shape is a trade. The cheap one can't go fast; the one that survives huge crowds is expensive and complicated. None wins at everything. And the choice isn't only technical: it also depends on whether the team can drive it, what the budget is, and where the data already lives.

How the choice gets made

The book's method is like checking the road before picking the car, asking three questions in order.

First: is the whole system one kind of road? For the sandwich shop, everything from ordering to pickup has roughly the same needs, so one car is enough: build it as a single block. The auction site is different. The part where thousands of people watch and bid must be able to grow in seconds; the part where the auctioneer drops the hammer must never get it wrong. That's two kinds of road, so you split into several cars, each suited to its own.

Second: who keeps which ledger? A single block usually shares one ledger; once you split, you decide which ledger belongs to whom.

Third: how do the cars talk to each other? Call when you can, it's simple and direct. Leave a message only when the other side is swamped or doesn't need to answer right away.

What you get, and how to choose

The win: your choice has reasons you can explain to your boss and teammates, and write down for whoever comes next. The cost: this method doesn't produce one correct answer. Two careful architects can read the same requirements and pick different designs, both defensible.

One line to remember

Choosing an architecture = check the road, then pick the car: one kind of road, one car; several kinds, several cars; then decide who owns which ledger and how they talk. There's no best car, only the one that fits this road.

The one-liner

You'd think choosing an architecture style means picking "the best one" out of eight. The book is blunt: the answer is always "it depends." FoSA Chapter 18 folds the previous 17 chapters into one decision path: use architecture quanta to decide monolith or distributed, then decide where data lives, then decide whether services talk synchronously or asynchronously. The output isn't a style name; it's a set of three: a topology, architecture decision records, and fitness functions.

Glossary

  • Architecture style: the overall organizing shape of a system, such as layered, microkernel, event-driven, or microservices.
  • Architecture characteristics: qualities a system must have that aren't specific business features, like scalability, availability, or performance: the "-ilities."
  • Architecture quantum: the smallest independently deployable unit that carries its own set of architecture characteristics (Chapter 7).
  • Monolith / distributed: a monolith deploys the whole system as one unit; a distributed system has many units cooperating over a network.
  • Synchronous / asynchronous communication: synchronous means sending a request and waiting for the reply; asynchronous means sending a message and carrying on, with any reply arriving later.
  • Elasticity: the ability to handle sudden bursts of traffic by scaling out quickly and back in again.
  • Architecture decision record (ADR): a short document capturing an architectural decision, its context, and its reasoning (Chapter 19).
  • Fitness function: an automated check that keeps verifying the system still meets some architecture characteristic (Chapter 6).

Where it sits

The closing chapter of Part II, "Architecture Styles." Chapter 9 laid the foundations and Chapters 10–17 covered eight styles one by one; this chapter puts them back on a single decision table, and leads into Part III's "Architecture Decisions" (Chapter 19). In practice it maps to every new-system kickoff, and every architecture review that asks "should we move to microservices?"

What problem this chapter tackles

After eight styles, the reader holds eight star-rated scorecards but no method for using them on real requirements. The two common failures are concrete. One is chasing fashion: "everyone is doing microservices," so a five-person team ends up running 40 services and spends most of its time on deployment pipelines and cross-service debugging. The other is defaulting to habit: you use what you know, and a system that must absorb 10x traffic spikes becomes a layered monolith that can only scale as a whole.

What the chapter offers is an explainable way to choose: which inputs to start from, what order to decide in, and what to hand over at the end, so that "why this style" can be restated, challenged, and recorded.

The core ideas, in depth

① Architecture "fashion" shifts: see the forces first

The book opens with a cold shower: the industry's preferred styles drift over time, not for aesthetic reasons but because conditions change. It lists several forces:

  • Observations from the past: the pains of one generation shape the next. The coupling pain of share-everything SOA (Chapter 16) led directly to microservices' "prefer duplication."
  • Changes in the ecosystem: the landscape of tools and platforms keeps reshuffling; yesterday's best answer may be unmaintained today.
  • New capabilities: new technology can make a previously uneconomic style viable, the way containers and automated operations made "lots of services" affordable.
  • Acceleration: the pace of change itself keeps rising.
  • Domain and technology changes: businesses evolve, merge, and pivot; technology stacks evolve too.
  • External factors: for example, rising license fees for commercial software push teams toward open-source alternatives.

The lesson: judge a style by whether its preconditions hold for you today, not by how popular it is today.

② Decision criteria: not just technology

The book lists the inputs an architect should have before choosing a style:

  • The domain: the important aspects of the business, especially those that affect architecture characteristics. You needn't be a domain expert, but you need enough to talk with the business.
  • Architecture characteristics that impact structure: out of the key characteristics found in Chapters 4–5, the few that actually decide the skeleton.
  • Data architecture: database design is often led by others, and existing schemas and integration points constrain the structure.
  • Organizational factors: budget, cloud-vendor dependence, planned mergers and acquisitions. A company expecting frequent acquisitions needs open, easy-to-integrate solutions.
  • Knowledge of process, teams, and operations: the book's example is that a team without agile engineering practices will struggle with styles that depend on them, such as microservices.
  • Domain/architecture isomorphism: some problems have a natural "shape" that matches a topology. A highly customizable product matches microkernel's core plus plug-ins; conversely, a problem with a high degree of semantic coupling fits poorly with highly decoupled distributed architectures, like forcing tightly interrelated business logic into microservices.

③ The decision path: three questions, in order

With the inputs in hand, the book gives an order for deciding:

Question one: monolith or distributed. Look through the lens of Chapter 7's architecture quantum. If a single set of architecture characteristics covers the whole system, a monolith is suitable (though other factors may still push you toward distribution). If different parts of the system need different characteristics, say one part needs extreme elasticity and another extreme reliability, you need a distributed architecture so each quantum can meet its own. This comes first because, as Chapter 9 showed, the moment you cross the network you sign the bill for the eight fallacies of distributed computing.

Question two: where does data live. A monolith usually uses one (or a few) relational databases. A distributed system must decide which services persist which data, and think through how data flows across the system. This question has to consider both structure and behavior.

System Design · Day 3 Database Selection — once you know where data lives, picking the store by access pattern and consistency needs

Question three: synchronous or asynchronous between services. The book's default is synchronous by default, asynchronous when necessary. Synchronous calls are easier to design, implement, and debug. Asynchronous messaging has unique benefits for performance and scale, but brings a string of headaches: data synchronization, deadlocks, race conditions, and debugging. So reach for async only when you need to absorb spikes, decouple, or defer the reply.

Output: an architecture topology showing the chosen style (often a hybrid) and the quantum boundaries; architecture decision records for the decisions that took the most effort; and fitness functions protecting the important principles and characteristics so they don't quietly erode in later iterations.

④ Case study one: the monolithic Silicon Sandwiches

For the monolith example the book reuses Silicon Sandwiches, an architecture kata that appears throughout earlier chapters: online ordering for a sandwich chain, with per-store promotions and menu customizations. Analysis shows a single quantum is enough, so two monolithic candidates are compared:

  • Modular monolith: components partitioned by domain, one database, one deployment unit; simple and cheap.
  • Microkernel: the common workflow goes in the core system and store-specific customizations become plug-ins; different devices (web, mobile) come in through their own adapter layer, which the book calls Backends for Frontends (BFF). It turns "customizability," the key characteristic, into structure.

The point isn't which one wins. It's that the same requirements admit two reasonable monolithic answers, and the trade-off comes down to how heavy the customization needs are.

⑤ Case study two: the distributed Going, Going, Gone

The second kata is an online auction platform, Going, Going, Gone: bidders watch a live video stream and bid online while a live auctioneer runs the sale. As Chapter 7 already showed, the characteristics clearly aren't uniform. The bidder-facing bid capture and streaming must survive unpredictable crowds, so it needs elasticity and scalability; auction sessions, bid tracking, and payment need reliability and correctness, because losing a single bid is an incident.

Since different parts need different characteristics, question one's answer is distributed. The book's design splits components into domain services forming several quanta, and buffers with asynchronous messages between steps like bid capture and bid tracking: however many people are watching, bids just queue up and the reliable side processes them at its own pace. The result is a hybrid of microservices and event-driven architecture, which is the reality the chapter wants readers to accept: most real systems are hybrids.

For a sense of scale: an in-process call typically takes well under a microsecond, a network round trip inside one data center about 0.5 ms. Chain six synchronous services into one request and you add several milliseconds of network alone, with tail latency compounding on top. That's why question one deserves real thought: if one set of characteristics is enough, staying monolithic is the cheapest choice.

Why it matters, in real systems

This chapter is the hands-on version of the book's "everything is a trade-off," and it's the layer interviewers probe hardest after "how would you design this system": why this style and not another. Public records show choices in both directions that line up with the book's path:

Industry evidence · practices and claims from public sources (paraphrased, text citations only)
  • Martin Fowler (2015): in "MonolithFirst" he observed that almost all the successful microservice stories he'd heard started with a monolith that got too big and was broken up, while most systems built as microservices from scratch ended up in serious trouble. That matches the book's "first ask whether one set of characteristics is enough." martinfowler.com, bliki "MonolithFirst," 2015 (paraphrased)
  • Shopify (2019): its engineering blog describes how, instead of breaking its huge Rails monolith into microservices, it restructured it into a modular monolith with components organized by business domain and enforced boundaries. Shopify Engineering, "Deconstructing the Monolith," 2019 (paraphrased)
  • Istio (2020): in release 1.5 the service mesh project merged its separate control-plane components into a single process, istiod, explaining that the components were always released and operated together, so splitting them only added complexity. Istio blog, "Introducing istiod," 2020 (paraphrased)
  • Amazon Prime Video (2023): its tech blog reported that an audio/video quality monitoring tool, originally orchestrated from distributed components, cut infrastructure cost by about 90% after moving to a single process. That was a local choice for one tool, not a rejection of services in general. Prime Video Tech blog, 2023 (paraphrased)

In a review, three questions audit a design: "Which parts truly need different architecture characteristics?" "Who owns each piece of data?" "What does this async link buy, and is it worth the debugging cost?" In an interview, you can restate it as: "Identify the key characteristics, use quantum analysis to decide whether to distribute, then settle data ownership and communication; synchronous by default, async when necessary; record the reasoning in ADRs and guard the characteristics with fitness functions."

Pitfalls and debates

  • "Choosing a style = choosing a name": the book's output is a topology, ADRs, and fitness functions. Real systems are mostly hybrids, and forcing a single label misleads.
  • "Distributed is more advanced": distribution is the price you pay because parts need different characteristics, not an upgrade. When one set suffices, a monolith is cheaper, faster, and easier to test.
  • "Async is better": async buys scale and decoupling but brings data synchronization, races, and debugging pain; the book's default is synchronous.
  • Looking only at technology, not the organization: adopting microservices without automated deployment and agile engineering practices often yields a distributed monolith.
  • Limits of its time: written in 2020, the book's criteria are qualitative, and weighting them is left to the architect's judgment; two careful architects can reach different, equally reasonable conclusions. The later revival of the "modular monolith" neatly confirms the chapter's opening point: fashion swings, the criteria don't.

Key takeaways

① There is no best style; the answer is always "it depends," a synthesis of characteristics, domain, and strategic goals.

② Architecture fashion drifts: past pain, ecosystem, new capabilities, domain and external factors all push. Judge by whether the preconditions hold today.

③ Decision criteria go beyond technology: domain, structural characteristics, data architecture, organizational factors, team and operational maturity, domain/architecture isomorphism.

④ Question one: does one set of characteristics fit the whole system? Yes means monolith; parts needing different ones means distributed.

⑤ Question two: where does data live. Question three: synchronous by default, async only when necessary.

⑥ The output is a set of three: a topology (often a hybrid), ADRs, and fitness functions.

⑦ Sandwich shop: one quantum, modular monolith and microkernel both defensible. Auction platform: several quanta, a microservices plus event-driven hybrid.

⑧ Interview line: "Set the key characteristics, decide monolith or distributed by quanta, then data and communication, and close with ADRs and fitness functions."
