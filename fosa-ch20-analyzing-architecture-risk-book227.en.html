Analyzing Architecture Risk: Turning "I'm a Bit Worried" into a Score You Can Rank

Fundamentals of Software Architecture · Ch 20 · Mark Richards & Neal Ford · 2020

中文 →

What is this chapter about?

You run an online shop and a big sale is coming next month. The boss asks the tech lead: "Where is it most likely to break?" The lead says: "Everything looks fine, I'm just a bit worried about the database." That answer is useless. How worried? More worried than about anything else? Should we spend money on it? This chapter is about turning that vague unease into something you can rank and take to the boss when you ask for a budget.

An analogy: the weather forecast

A forecast never just says "bad weather tomorrow". It tells you two things: will it rain, and how hard. A small chance of drizzle you ignore; a big chance of a storm cancels the trip. Judging a system works the same way: how likely is something to go wrong, and how bad is it if it does? Score each, combine them, and you know what to watch first.

Why the old way is hard

Risk used to be one architect's gut-feel list. Three problems: gut feelings can't be compared ("a bit worried" versus "quite worried"); one person always has blind spots; and the list goes into a drawer while the system keeps changing for three more months.

How it works

Move one is scoring: rate each worry low, medium or high on "how likely" and "how bad", and combine them into one score, like the red-amber-green lights on a medical report.

Move two is one big table: across the top are parts of the business, such as sign-up, checkout and shipping; down the side are the qualities you care about, such as coping with crowds, staying up, keeping data correct. Every cell gets a score, and the rows and columns that glow red jump out. A little arrow next to a cell shows whether it got better or worse since last time.

Move three is group fault-finding: send a diagram of the system to a few colleagues and ask each of them to put sticky notes on it alone first: "I'm worried about this bit", plus a score. Then everyone sticks their notes on one shared diagram. The most valuable spots are where scores disagree wildly: if one person gives the top score and everyone else gives a low one, that person knows something the others don't. Finally, agree on fixes, price them, and go talk to the boss.

What you get, and how to choose

Risk stops being one person's private anxiety and becomes a list the whole team can see, rank and track, and spending finally has a basis. It is not a one-off: when the system changes, do it again. The honest cost: the scores are still human guesses. It looks like maths, but it is really a structured conversation.

One line to remember

Analyzing architecture risk = score how likely times how bad, lay the scores out in one table, then have everyone look alone, compare together, and agree on fixes.

The one-liner

You think risk analysis is an architect writing a list from experience. FoSA Chapter 20 turns it into routine work that is quantified, collaborative and continuous: the risk matrix turns one worry into a score from 1 to 9; the risk assessment lays scores out as a heat map of "architecture characteristic × business domain" with trends; and risk storming has the team find risks alone, reconcile disagreements, and then negotiate mitigation with costs attached.

Glossary

  • Architecture characteristics: the non-business qualities a system must deliver, such as availability, scalability, performance, security and data integrity.
  • Architecture risk: the likelihood and consequence of some characteristic falling short somewhere, e.g. "if the order database dies, nobody can check out".
  • Availability: the share of time a system serves requests correctly, usually written as "a number of nines".
  • Elasticity: the ability to absorb sudden traffic spikes; scalability is the ability to grow steadily with the user base.
  • Single point of failure: one component whose failure takes the whole path down, like a database with only one instance.
  • Load balancer: the component that spreads requests across several servers.
  • Fitness function: an automated check that keeps measuring whether the system still meets some architecture characteristic (Chapter 6).
  • User story / iteration: a small slice of requirements in agile development, and the one-to-two-week cycle in which it is built.
  • Groupthink: the tendency in group discussion to drift toward the majority or the most senior voice and stop voicing dissent.

What problem this chapter tackles

The book opens by saying that every architecture has risk, around availability, scalability, data integrity and more, and that analyzing it is one of the architect's key activities. The hard part isn't knowing that risk exists. It is three things: how big (can risks be compared?), where (one person's view has blind spots) and how it stands now (the system keeps changing).

What happens if you don't solve this? Take an e-commerce site handling around a million orders a day with an availability target of 99.9%, which allows about 43 minutes of downtime a month. If the order database is a single instance and recovery takes an hour, one outage burns through the whole month's budget. As long as that risk lives only as the architect's "slight worry", it never gets prioritized or funded. When it does happen on sale day, the postmortem finds that someone had noticed long ago; nobody had asked them.

The core ideas, in depth

① The risk matrix: two dimensions, one product

The risk matrix underpins every tool in the chapter. It asks two questions: overall impact (how bad if it happens) and likelihood (how likely it is to happen), each rated low 1, medium 2, high 3, and multiplies them. 1–2 is low risk, 3–4 medium, 6–9 high (5, 7 and 8 never appear).

Two operating details from the book are worth remembering. First, rate impact before likelihood: asking "how bad would it really be?" first stops people from downplaying a serious problem because "that's unlikely, surely". Second, unproven or unfamiliar technology automatically gets the top score of 9: if you don't even know how it fails, the matrix can't be applied, so treat it as the biggest risk until proven otherwise.

Why multiply, and why only three levels? Once two fuzzy judgments become numbers they can be ranked, compared and aggregated; and three levels are coarse enough to stop arguments over "is this a 6 or a 7?", which would only manufacture false precision. Example: the order service runs on a single database instance and its loss stops all checkouts, impact 3; it has failed once in two years, likelihood 2; score 6, high risk.

② The risk assessment: from one point to a full picture

One score describes one point. A risk assessment rolls them into a report: rows are risk criteria (usually architecture characteristics such as scalability, availability, performance, security, data integrity), columns are business domains or services (e.g. customer registration, checkout, order fulfillment, shipping), and each cell is scored with the risk matrix.

The value is in summing both ways: by row, data integrity totals 22, the weakest characteristic in the system; by column, checkout totals 29, the riskiest domain. The book adds two refinements. One is filtering: show only the high-risk cells and leave the rest blank. In a report for management, less is more; a screen full of 1s and 2s buries the real 9s. The book frames this as improving the signal-to-noise ratio. The other is direction: a plus or minus sign, or an arrow, beside a score shows whether it improved or worsened since the last assessment. Direction is best derived from continuous measurement via fitness functions: if a weekly automated load test pushes the checkout endpoint's p99 latency from 300 ms to 800 ms, the arrow points up, no gut feel needed.

③ Risk storming: alone first, then together, then mitigate

The assessment gives you scores, but where do they come from? One person scoring alone will have blind spots. Risk storming is a collaborative exercise for finding architectural risk within one particular dimension. The book splits it into three steps.

Preparation: the architect sends an invitation with the architecture diagram and the single dimension to analyze this time (say, availability only). One dimension per session keeps everyone's attention on the same class of problem. Participants are usually the architect plus senior developers and tech leads: developers know the implementation best, and taking part gives them a sense of ownership of the risk.

Identification: each person scores areas of the diagram with the risk matrix alone, without discussion, on sticky notes. Why alone? Because once a meeting starts, the first speaker, especially the most senior one, pulls everyone's judgment toward theirs: groupthink and anchoring.

Consensus: everyone puts their notes on one shared diagram. Areas where scores agree are confirmed quickly; the real payoff is in the disagreements. Three people give the load balancer a 2, one gives it a 6; only in discussion does it emerge that this one person knew the load balancer isn't clustered. Disagreement isn't noise; it signals that someone holds information the others lack.

Mitigation: change the architecture where scores are high: add redundancy, split a database, introduce caching, isolate entry points. Every option costs something, so this step often means negotiating with business stakeholders: if the full fix is too expensive, fall back to a cheaper compromise that still drops the score a level. The book's running example is a nurse-hotline diagnostics system, analyzed in successive rounds for availability, elasticity and security, each exposing different hot spots; one security mitigation was to give each type of user its own API gateway, so the self-diagnosis entry point can't reach medical records (paraphrasing the book's example).

④ Apply it to user stories, and keep doing it

The same matrix works for agile story risk analysis: likelihood becomes "this story won't finish this iteration", impact becomes "what slips if it doesn't". High-scoring stories get watched and split first.

One idea runs through the whole chapter: risk drifts. New features, doubled traffic and upgraded dependencies all move the scores. So risk storming and assessments should be repeated regularly, for instance before each major milestone or big sale, not filed away after one pass.

Full mitigation (e.g. a highly available database cluster)Takes a 9 down to around a 2Most expensive; often rejected by the business

Multiplying scores has one blind spot you must know: impact 3 × likelihood 1 and impact 1 × likelihood 3 both score 3, yet the first is "rare but fatal" and the second "common but minor". For risks with irreversible consequences, such as data loss or a full outage, the product systematically underweights the tail. A common practical rule: list every impact-3 item separately, however unlikely.

Why it matters, in real systems

The tools in this chapter cost almost nothing to start (a diagram, a stack of sticky notes, a 3×3 grid), yet they answer the most common follow-up in interviews and reviews: "What's the biggest risk in your design, and how would you mitigate it?" Answering in the chapter's structure (give the score, impact and likelihood, then the mitigation and its cost, then how you'll keep measuring) beats "I think the database might be a bottleneck" by a mile.

Industry evidence · practices and claims from public sources (paraphrased, text citations only)
  • Google (2016): the SRE book's "Embracing Risk" chapter argues that a service should be reliable enough, not as reliable as possible, and uses an error budget to turn acceptable unavailability into a number that makes the trade-off between reliability and release velocity explicit. It is the industrial version of turning availability risk from a worry into a figure. Google, Site Reliability Engineering, Ch. 3 "Embracing Risk", O'Reilly, 2016 (paraphrased)
  • Netflix (from 2011): Chaos Monkey randomly terminates instances in production to keep verifying that the system survives single-point failures. Rather than estimating likelihood on a grid, let the failure actually happen and calibrate the score against the result. Netflix Tech Blog, "The Netflix Simian Army", 2011 (paraphrased)
  • Gary Klein (2007): proposed the premortem: assume the project has already failed and have each person independently write down why before pooling the answers, the same logic as risk storming's solo identification step. The article cites research that this "prospective hindsight" raises the ability to correctly identify reasons for an outcome by about 30%. Harvard Business Review, "Performing a Project Premortem", September 2007 (paraphrased)

To start with your own team: two weeks before a big sale or major release, pick one dimension (most teams start with availability), send the diagram to four or five core developers and book half a day; afterwards publish an assessment showing only the high-risk cells, each with a mitigation, a cost and an owner, and re-run it at the next milestone, using arrows to show the trend.

Pitfalls and debates

  • Treating scores as objective measurements: a 1–9 score is structured subjective judgment; its value is in starting the conversation and setting priorities, not in decimal precision.
  • Equal products mean equal risks: rare-but-fatal and common-but-minor score the same; irreversible items need separate treatment.
  • Skipping solo identification and going straight to a meeting: the time saved buys groupthink, and the single most important step of risk storming is gone.
  • Assessing every dimension at once: attention gets diluted and each dimension only gets a surface look; the book does one at a time.
  • Averaging away disagreement: the widest gaps are exactly where to ask "what do you know that we don't?"
  • Identifying without pricing: a mitigation without a cost can't be decided; a risk list without mitigations is just an anxiety list.
  • Assess once, then archive: risk drifts with features, traffic and dependencies; the trend arrow says more than any single score.
  • Origins and era: risk storming is commonly credited to Simon Brown, who popularized it; the book adopts and refines it. The book dates from 2020; many teams now measure availability risk continuously with SLOs, error budgets and chaos experiments, and run risk storms remotely on online whiteboards. The spirit is the same.

Key takeaways

① Every architecture has risk; the goal of analysis is to make it comparable, locatable and trackable.

② Risk matrix: impact × likelihood, each 1–3; 1–2 low, 3–4 medium, 6–9 high; rate impact first, then likelihood.

③ Unproven technology automatically scores 9: if you don't know how it fails, you can't score it.

④ Risk assessment: rows are characteristics, columns are domains; row and column totals pinpoint the weakest characteristic and the riskiest domain.

⑤ Reports for management keep only high-risk cells; arrows show trends, ideally derived from fitness functions.

⑥ Risk storming in three steps: identification (alone) → consensus (disagreement is information) → mitigation (negotiate with costs); one dimension per session.

⑦ The same matrix applies to delivery risk in user stories; risk drifts, so repeat at every milestone.

⑧ Multiplied scores underweight "rare but fatal"; list irreversible high-impact items separately.
