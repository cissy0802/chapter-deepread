What is this chapter about?

Behind the shopping, video and ride-hailing apps you use every day, there are engineers who break the system on purpose, a little, to find weak spots before real outages do. This chapter asks: should they tell anyone else what they find? The author's answer is blunt: yes — and they should write it up the way scientists publish an experiment.

A strange thing first

Inside one company, three teams can fall into the same pothole within three months: each forgot to set "how long to wait before giving up" on a slow service. Every team worked hard, fixed it on its own, and wrote a summary nobody else read. The pothole wasn't hard. What failed was that the news never travelled.

Why the old way was hard

  • Embarrassment. Admitting "our system couldn't cope" feels like admitting you did a bad job, so findings stay quietly inside the team.
  • Write-ups nobody can redo. Even when shared, it's often just "we tested a network cut, all fine" — nobody knows what was cut, for how long, or what was watched, so nobody can repeat it.
  • Everyone builds their own tools. Each company writes its own "breaking things" kit from scratch: duplicated effort, and nobody else checks it for mistakes.

The core intuition: publish drills like science experiments

The author borrows science's old rule: a good paper says what you expected, what you did, and what actually happened, so others can redo it and catch your mistakes. Applied to failure drills, that means three things:

  • Open minds: treat "the system didn't hold up" as shared learning material, not someone's fault.
  • Open tools: share the breaking tools the way people share recipes — everyone improves them and everyone can see exactly what they do.
  • Open experiments and results: write each drill up as a card — the guess, how you broke things, how far the damage could spread, when you'd stop immediately, what happened, and what you changed. With the card, another team can rerun it on their own system.

What it buys you / how to choose

A pothole one team spent a week finding can be avoided by another team in five minutes of reading; a conclusion many people have redone is far more trustworthy than one team's word. You don't have to go all the way at once: share cards inside the company first, and talk to the outside world later.

One honest cost: openness takes writing effort, and some details (security holes, for instance) really can't be shared right away.

One line to remember

Fall into each pothole only once: write failure drills as cards others can rerun, and share the results like scientific findings.

In one sentence

You think chaos engineering's output is "a few bugs fixed"; in fact its most valuable output is knowledge — and knowledge only compounds when it flows and others can reproduce and challenge it. This chapter argues for importing science's norms of openness into chaos engineering: a collaborative mindset, open-source tools, reproducible experiment designs, and shareable results.

Glossary

  • Chaos experiment: deliberately injecting a fault (a crash, latency, a network cut) within controlled limits to see whether the system stays healthy.
  • Steady state: a measurable business metric under normal conditions, such as orders per second, used to tell whether anything went wrong.
  • Hypothesis: a prediction written before the experiment, such as "checkout success stays flat when the recommendation service slows down"; the experiment tries to disprove it.
  • Blast radius: the most users or machines one experiment can affect, e.g. 1% of traffic.
  • Abort condition: how far a metric may drift before injection stops and everything is restored.
  • Reproducibility: someone else following your described steps and conditions reaches the same conclusion.
  • Peer review: colleagues checking whether the method and reasoning hold up.
  • Open source: software whose code is public, so anyone can read, modify and contribute to it.
  • Postmortem: a write-up after an incident or drill covering what happened, why, and what changes.

The problem this chapter solves

The previous dozen chapters cover how to run one experiment well. But a mid-sized company may have dozens of teams and hundreds of services, each experimenting. If every team's findings stay in its own weekly report, three kinds of waste appear:

  • Repeated potholes: missing timeouts, retry storms, single-zone dependencies keep reappearing across teams, each time rediscovered at the cost of another experiment — or a real incident.
  • Untrustworthy conclusions: "we tested a network cut, no problem" — which path? for how long? watching what? A conclusion nobody can reproduce is one nobody dares rely on and nobody can refute.
  • Reinvented tooling: each company builds its own injection framework, nobody audits what it actually does to production, and nobody contributes improvements.

Left unsolved, chaos engineering stays a craft of a few heroic teams, and organizational resilience does not grow with the number of experiments.

The core ideas, in depth

1. A collaborative mindset: a finding is not someone's failure

The chapter opens with mindset. When a team discovers "our service can't handle a dependency timeout", the first instinct is often to hide it — for fear of looking incompetent, or because it feels like private business. The author contrasts this closed mindset with a collaborative one, which treats every disproved hypothesis as a shared organizational asset and invites other teams to look, ask and poke holes.

This continues the book's recurring "blameless" theme: if exposing weaknesses gets punished, people stop exposing them, and chaos experiments become theatre. Openness requires safety — make sure saying "we were wrong" carries no penalty, and sharing follows.

2. Open science: why borrow science's rules

Chaos engineering has called itself an experimental discipline from the start: define a steady state, form a hypothesis, inject real-world events, try to disprove the hypothesis. The author pushes that self-image one step further — if it is the scientific method, it should accept science's norms of openness: public methods, stated conditions, results others can reproduce and review.

The open science movement argues for publishing methods, data and results for a simple reason: a conclusion only stands once someone has independently redone it; a closed conclusion can be wrong with nobody able to notice. Chaos experiments face exactly the same problem — one run is one sample, and a different time, traffic shape or dependency version can flip the conclusion.

3. Open source: open tools are the foundation of trust

The second layer is tooling. Chaos tools carry a special trust problem: you are letting them break things in production. With a closed-source injector you can't see which network rules it changes, which processes it kills, or whether it leaves debris when it fails; open source makes that behavior auditable. Community contributions also mean each kind of injection (instances, network, containers, cloud APIs) need not be rewritten at every company.

The starting point is concrete: Netflix open-sourced Chaos Monkey in 2012, and injection tools kept appearing as open source after that. Open source also brings the chance to standardize the experiment format — once an experiment is a declarative file, it can be shared, versioned and code-reviewed.

5. Experiment findings, shareable results

The bottom half of the card is the result. The author stresses writing results so others can read and use them: what the hypothesis was, whether it was disproved, what was observed, the inferred cause, what was changed, and whether the fix was rechecked. Share the uneventful experiments too — "hypothesis held, all fine" is valuable, because it tells others that path has already been verified.

The audience can widen in stages: the team, then the whole company (a searchable experiment library, regular show-and-tells), then the outside world (engineering blogs, conference talks, open-source experiment templates). Each ring outward adds more people who can find your mistakes, and the conclusion gets harder.

Key trade-offs and choices

More openness is not automatically better; the trade-off is the gains from reuse and review versus the cost of writing and the risk of exposure.

How far to open up: gains and costs

LevelGainCost / riskWhen
Team onlyNo extra cost, people speak freelyKnowledge trapped; other teams repeat the potholesEarly days, experiments still unstable
Company-wide libraryCross-team reuse; fix a class of bug everywhere at onceNeeds a common card format and an owner for the index; depends on blameless cultureThe main battlefield for most orgs
Open-source tools and templatesAuditable behavior; community adds injection typesMaintenance burden; projects age or get abandonedTooling mature and general
Public resultsIndustry-wide peer review; hiring and reputationMay expose architecture and security details; needs review and redactionConclusion rechecked, details redactable

Tooling: open source, commercial, or in-house

RouteStrengthCost
Open sourceAuditable code, shareable experiment format, no license feeYou add operations and safety guardrails yourself; community activity varies
Commercial platformReady to use; abort, permissions and audit usually built inCost; experiment format may be vendor-bound and hard to share outside
In-houseFits internal architecture; can inject per requestMost expensive; no outside review, the easiest to become a closed island

Rule of thumb: start with a company-wide card format and shared library — small investment, biggest reuse payoff; prefer tools with open formats so you can exchange experiments later; save public disclosure for conclusions that are rechecked and redactable.

Why it matters / in real systems

This looks like a "culture chapter", but in engineering terms it is concrete: experiments as code. Once an experiment is a declarative file it can live in version control, go through code review and be replayed in a pipeline — the precondition for Ch16's continuous verification. Asked in an interview "how would you roll out chaos engineering across an organization?", the strong answer isn't "run more experiments" but "standardize the experiment format, build a shared library, keep postmortems blameless, make conclusions reproducible."

Databases have a benchmark for open science: the Jepsen project (its open-source repository's tagline: "Breaking distributed systems so you don't have to") runs fault-injection tests against distributed databases and publishes both test code and analyses; many vendors have fixed bugs or rewritten consistency claims in their docs as a result — the outside pressure that "reproducible + publicly reviewed" can create. (Ch19 on chaos for databases returns to this style of work.)

Evidence from industry

  • Netflix open-sourced Chaos Monkey and framed its value as behavior change. The repository opens: "Exposing engineers to failures more frequently incentivizes them to build resilient services." Netflix · Chaos Monkey README (github.com/Netflix/chaosmonkey)
  • The flip side: open-source projects age. Netflix's Simian Army repository now states "The Simian Army project is no longer actively maintained", with Chaos Monkey moved to a standalone service. Open tools aren't forever; check who maintains them. Netflix · SimianArmy README (github.com/Netflix/SimianArmy)
  • Shopify pushed an open-source network-fault proxy into everyday development. Toxiproxy says: "We've been successfully using it in all development and test environments at Shopify since October, 2014." Open tools aren't only for production drills; they can reach every developer's tests. Shopify · Toxiproxy README (github.com/Shopify/toxiproxy)
  • "Shared experiments" have become infrastructure. The CNCF project LitmusChaos describes its ChaosHub as "a central hub where the application developers or vendors share their chaos experiments". LitmusChaos README (github.com/litmuschaos/litmus)
  • Opening the experiment format. Chaos Toolkit states its mission as "to provide an open API to chaos engineering in all its forms"; experiments are declarative JSON / YAML files that can be shared and replayed. Chaos Toolkit README (github.com/chaostoolkit/chaostoolkit)

Pitfalls and debates

  • "Open means publish everything on the internet." The chapter's weight is actually inside the organization; public disclosure is the last ring and needs redaction.
  • "Sharing the tools is enough." Open-source tooling solves one layer; what's actually scarce is reproducible designs and results. Everyone on the same tool, each writing unreproducible conclusions, is still a set of islands.
  • "A failed rerun means the original was wrong." Not necessarily: the system, versions or traffic may have changed. That's exactly why environment and versions must be recorded — without them a failed rerun can't be interpreted.
  • Tension between security and openness. Some results are vulnerability information; fix and assess risk before disclosing. The chapter says little about this.
  • Without a blameless culture, openness becomes theatre. Sharing only the "successful" experiments while hiding disproved hypotheses misleads more than sharing nothing.
  • Advocacy over data. The chapter offers no quantitative evidence linking openness to resilience gains, and the author works for a chaos engineering vendor; read with that in mind.

Takeaways

① The most valuable output of chaos experiments is knowledge, and knowledge compounds only when it flows.

② The cost of closedness: repeated potholes, unreproducible conclusions, reinvented tools.

③ Openness requires blamelessness: if "we were wrong" isn't punished, sharing follows.

④ If chaos engineering claims the scientific method, it should accept science's openness: public methods, reproducible, reviewable.

⑤ Open-source tools make "breaking production" auditable and open the way to a standard experiment format.

⑥ A reproducible card: steady state, hypothesis, magnitude, blast radius, abort condition, environment and versions, result, fix.

⑦ "Environment and versions" is the most-omitted row, and it decides whether a failed rerun can be interpreted.

⑧ Open up in rings: team → company library → open source → public; the main battlefield is inside the company.

⑨ Experiments as code are the precondition for continuous verification (Ch16).
