Design a Payment System

What is this chapter about?

You tap "Pay now" in a shopping app, and a second later the page says "payment successful." This chapter builds what runs inside that second: it takes money from the buyer, and one day hands money to the seller — not a penny more, not a penny less.

One strange thing first

This system is remarkably slow. A million transactions a day works out to about a dozen per second — less traffic than a mid-sized forum. And yet it is the hardest chapter in the book to get right.

Because the difficulty was never speed. It is that "did the money move?" must have a definite answer.

Why the old way is hard

You press pay, the phone spins for thirty seconds, and a line appears: network error. Were you charged? You don't know. Neither does the server. The request may never have arrived — or the bank may have taken the money and only the receipt got lost on the way back.

The system now has two options. Treat it as failed, and the customer may be charged for nothing. Treat it as succeeded, and the merchant may ship for nothing. Worse, the customer will press the button again — and one purchase becomes two charges.

The mechanism, intuitively

① Give every payment a ticket number. However many times the same ticket is handed over the counter, the clerk does the work once and simply copies out the first receipt again. "I'm not sure it went through, so let me try again" turns from a dangerous act into a safe one.

② Write every amount down twice. Every movement of money lands on two accounts at once, one side down and the other up, and the whole book always sums to zero. Miss an entry and the sum gives it away — money is conserved by the shape of the bookkeeping, not by the care of the bookkeeper.

③ Compare books every night. Your record of the day, line by line, against the record the payment company sends you. Matches pass. Mismatches get pulled out: the ones that are just a fee can be fixed by rule, and whatever is left goes to a human. This step is not insurance — it is a necessity, because the first two tricks never cover everything.

④ Card numbers never enter your house. The card form belongs to the payment company; the number goes straight into their machines and you get back a stand-in code. What you cannot store, you cannot leak.

What it costs

All of this turns "paying" from a single act into a process with states: it can sit in "processing," it can land in a retry queue, and every day it leaves behind a small tail that a human must settle by hand — that inconvenience is the price of being correct.

One thing to remember

A payment system is absurdly slow and absurdly hard, because its only job is to make "did the money move?" have a definite answer. A timeout tells you neither success nor failure, so every payment carries a ticket number that is honoured once, which makes retrying safe again; every amount is written on two sides, so a missing entry cannot hide; and every night the books are compared against the payment company's, so whatever the first two missed gets pulled out and fixed by hand. The card number, meanwhile, is never let into your machines at all.

In one sentence

On the surface this chapter wires up a payment. What it actually teaches is how to make "did the money move?" have exactly one answer, inside an outside world you do not control and cannot rerun. First counter-intuition: this is the lowest-throughput system in the book — a million transactions a day is roughly 11.6 TPS, which an ordinary relational database handles in its sleep. Not one design decision here is about performance. Second: correctness is not bought by being careful, it is bought in three stacked layers — idempotency keys make retries safe, a double-entry ledger makes money conserved and auditable, and daily reconciliation concedes outright that the first two still leak, so the books get counted again every night.

Glossary

  • Pay-in / pay-out: money flowing from the buyer into the platform is pay-in; the platform settling money out to sellers is pay-out.
  • PSP (payment service provider): companies like Stripe, Braintree or Adyen that connect to banks and card schemes on your behalf — you just call their API.
  • Card scheme: the clearing networks (Visa, Mastercard) that link the acquiring bank to the issuing bank.
  • Payment event / payment order: one checkout is a payment event; each seller inside that checkout gets its own payment order, which succeeds or fails on its own.
  • Ledger: the authoritative record of every movement of money, kept with double-entry bookkeeping — one transaction, two entries, debits equal credits.
  • Wallet: a seller's account balance — a view derived by summing the ledger.
  • Idempotency: doing an operation once and doing it many times produce the same result. An idempotency key is the unique id issued for one such operation.
  • Exactly-once: not a guarantee any network gives you. It is assembled from "at-least-once retries + idempotency."
  • Reconciliation: comparing your own ledger, line by line, against the settlement file the PSP sends each day, then correcting the differences.
  • PCI DSS: the card industry's security standard for cardholder data; anything that stores, processes or transmits card numbers falls inside its compliance scope.
  • DLQ (dead letter queue): where messages go after retries keep failing, to wait for a human.
  • TPS: transactions per second.

Where it sits

Chapter 11 of Volume 2, and the first half of the book's "money must not be wrong" thread: the next chapter, Digital Wallet, pushes this chapter's ledger all the way to event sourcing, CQRS and a Raft-replicated state machine, while Ch7 (Hotel Reservation) is the other face of the same family — concurrent contention over finite inventory. In the real world this is e-commerce checkout, the charge at the end of a ride, a SaaS subscription renewal, and anything that calls an outside institution where money genuinely moves.

What the chapter is solving

Put the scale on the table first: a million transactions a day is 1,000,000 ÷ 86,400 ≈ 11.6 TPS. In this book that number is nearly comic — Ch4's rate limiter fields a million QPS, Ch10's leaderboard peaks at 2,500 QPS, and the payment system runs in double digits. Every reflex about caching, sharding and going async fails here at once: there is no performance problem to solve.

The real problem is singular, and it comes from outside your boundary. A payment crosses five doors — your service → PSP → acquiring bank → card scheme → issuing bank — and you own the first. When the call times out, a timeout is neither success nor failure. It is a third state: unknown. And money hates nothing more than unknown.

What happens if you don't solve it? All three of these: ① double charges — one retry, two debits; ② lost bookkeeping — the PSP charged the card, your ledger never recorded it, the seller never gets paid; ③ month-end doesn't balance — the gap is real, but nobody can say which transactions it is made of. The first two hurt users; the third hurts the company: a payment system that cannot explain its own discrepancies is, to finance and to regulators, not a system at all.

The core, properly explained

① The pay-in flow: one checkout, several payment orders that stand on their own

The first decision people skip is hiding in the data model: "a checkout" and "a payment" are not the same object. Three items in a cart may come from three sellers, so one checkout (a payment event, keyed by checkout_id) splits into three payment orders (keyed by payment_order_id), each making its own PSP call and each succeeding or failing alone.

Why not bundle them into one charge? Because they are owed to different payees, and you have no transaction that spans the PSP — when the second fails, the first has already landed at a bank. There is no rollback, only compensation.

The division of labour is worth memorising too: the payment service receives the payment event and runs risk and anti-money-laundering checks; the payment executor executes a single payment order against the PSP; only after that do the ledger record entries and the wallet update the seller's balance. The two unremarkable columns on the payment order — ledger_updated and wallet_updated — are the most engineering-minded detail in the chapter: they turn "did this step happen?" into queryable state, so an order stuck halfway can be swept up by a program instead of guessed at by a person.

② Idempotency keys: turning "unknown" into "safe to try again"

This is the heart of the chapter. No network gives you exactly-once: don't retry and you may lose the payment, retry and you may duplicate it. So in engineering, exactly-once = at-least-once (retry) + idempotency (a retry has no new effect).

The mechanism: the caller generates a UUID per payment and puts it in an HTTP header (Idempotency-Key: 8f3c…); the server, before doing anything real, inserts that key atomically under a database unique constraint. If the insert succeeds you are first and may proceed; if it fails, someone is already doing it or has done it — return the first result, or reply 409 and let the caller ask again shortly.

Three details decide whether this works, and each is an interview watershed:

  • The order cannot be reversed. Calling the PSP and recording the key afterwards is not idempotency — crash in between and you have a double charge. The claim must happen before the side effect.
  • Store the response, not the fact of arrival. A boolean cannot tell a retry whether the first attempt succeeded; the original status code and body belong in the same row.
  • Keys are scoped. Caller → payment service, service → executor, executor → PSP: each hop retries on its own. Wherever the retry happens, that is where idempotency must live.

It also blocks an attack for free: with a nonce inside the key, a replay attack fails — resending the identical request is simply treated as a retry.

③ Card numbers never reach your servers: hosted checkout and tokenization

This section is the art of not doing something. The moment a card number passes through your process, your logs or your database, the whole system falls inside PCI DSS scope. So the standard answer is to outsource the collection step entirely to the PSP: its hosted checkout (a full-page redirect) or its embedded component (iframe / JS SDK), with the card going from the browser straight to the PSP and only a token coming back to you — valid solely under your merchant account, useless to a thief elsewhere. What you cannot store, you cannot leak.

The cost is that the flow becomes asynchronous: the user completes payment on the PSP's page (possibly via a 3DS challenge) and the result arrives by webhook. So you must handle the webhook landing before the user gets redirected back, and you must verify the callback signature — otherwise anyone can post "payment succeeded" to your endpoint.

④ Double-entry: money is conserved by the format, not by carefulness

The ledger uses double-entry bookkeeping: every transaction writes two rows, one debit and one credit of equal amount, and the whole book always sums to zero. The buyer's account down 100, the platform's payable-to-seller account up 100 — both written together.

It looks like an accountant's old habit, but it is a hard engineering property: it turns "is anything missing?" into a question a program can answer — a non-zero sum means something broke, and the entries lead you to which transaction it was. Keep only a balance table instead and a missed update stays silent forever.

Which yields a second rule: the wallet is not the source of truth; the ledger is. A balance is a derived view that can be recomputed — the same principle as Ch10's in-memory leaderboard being a rebuildable derived view.

⑤ Consistency: no transaction crosses the boundary, so be idempotent first and reconcile after

The payment service, the executor, the ledger and the wallet are four services, and the PSP is outside your company entirely. No transaction on this path can wrap all of them. The two textbook options fare badly: two-phase commit (2PC) has participants vote and then commit together, and a dead coordinator can leave them holding locks — while the PSP will never take part in your vote; Saga runs local transactions in order and compensates in reverse on failure (refund, reversal), at the price of hand-written compensation and intermediate states that are visible to users.

So the book's default is the third path: make every step idempotent, retry on failure, and leave the rest to reconciliation. Failure follows a standard pipeline: the payment order goes into a retry queue (exponential backoff with jitter, so clients don't retry in lockstep and flatten the PSP), and whatever still fails lands in a dead letter queue for a human. Meanwhile the PSP's daily settlement file is compared to the ledger line by line: matches pass; classifiable differences (fees, cut-off timing, FX) generate adjusting entries by rule; unclassifiable ones go to a manual queue — that last handful always exists, and admitting it and giving it a proper channel is the most mature thing in this chapter.

Why it matters / in real systems

The transfer value goes far beyond payments: anything that calls an external system with an irreversible side effect is the same question — sending an SMS, issuing an invoice, dispatching a shipment, wiring money to a third party, deducting credits from a model API. And the test is always the same: ask "what if this happens twice?" — if the answer is unacceptable, you need an idempotency key; then ask "how would I notice one went missing?" — if you cannot answer, you need reconciliation.

Common mistakes and open questions

  • Treating payments as a throughput problem. Drawing caches and shards first means you never did the arithmetic: 11.6 TPS needs no performance design at all. This chapter tests how you handle uncertainty.
  • Treating a timeout as a failure. Resending under a fresh order id is the most common cause of double charges — a retry carries the same idempotency key; it does not start a new payment.
  • Recording the key after the fact. "Call the PSP, then save the key if it worked" is fake idempotency: a crash in between breaks it. The claim must precede the side effect, and it must be atomic (a unique constraint), never read-then-write.
  • Storing only "this key has been seen." A matching retry must be handed back the original result; otherwise the caller still doesn't know whether the charge went through, and will send it again.
  • Assuming idempotency removes the need to reconcile. Idempotency governs the duplicates you emit. It does nothing about PSP-side fee differences, cut-off attribution, partial refunds, or orphan transactions you have no record of. Reconciliation is the backstop, not redundancy.
  • Storing money in floating point. Use integer minor units (cents) or fixed-point decimals, always with a currency. The book spends little ink here; production spends a lot.
  • What the chapter leaves out: the full refund and chargeback flow, multi-currency and FX, subscriptions and instalments, the long pending states that 3DS introduces, and risk rules themselves — risk appears here as a single box in a diagram.

Takeaways

① Payments are the lowest-throughput system in the book: 1M/day ≈ 11.6 TPS. There is no performance problem, only a correctness problem.

② The cause is the control boundary: your service → PSP → acquirer → card scheme → issuer, and you own only the first door.

③ A timeout is not a failure — it is a third state, "unknown." Retry blindly and you double charge; give up and you lose the order.

④ One checkout splits into N payment orders (one per seller), and no transaction can wrap them: compensation exists, rollback does not.

⑤ Exactly-once = at-least-once + idempotency. Claim the key atomically before executing, and store the first response with it.

⑥ Wherever the retry happens, that is where idempotency goes; a key with a nonce blocks replay attacks for free.

⑦ Double-entry makes conservation of money machine-checkable: two rows per transaction, sum zero; the wallet is only a derived view of the ledger.

⑧ Card numbers stay out of your servers: hosted checkout or embedded component plus tokenization, shrinking PCI DSS scope to a minimum.

⑨ No transaction crosses company lines: the default answer is idempotency + retry + daily reconciliation, not 2PC or Saga.

⑩ The failure pipeline: retry queue → dead letter queue → human with an audit trail. A handful of differences will never be explainable; giving them a proper channel is what maturity looks like.
