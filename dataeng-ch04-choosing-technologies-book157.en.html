Choosing Technologies Across the Data Engineering Lifecycle

Fundamentals of Data Engineering · Ch 4 · Reis & Housley · 2022

中文 →

What is this chapter about?

Every dashboard you see at work sits on top of a chain of tools that move, store and crunch data. There are hundreds of such tools, and every vendor claims to be the fastest and cheapest. This chapter doesn't recommend a brand. It teaches you which questions to ask before you pick one.

A strange thing first

Plenty of small teams copy what big tech does, build a whole self-run system, and end up with three people fixing machines all day instead of doing the actual work. Meanwhile some very large companies move out of the cloud into their own data centers and save tens of millions a year. The same choice is smart for one company and a disaster for another.

Why the old way hurt

  • Tool first, needs later. A team reads a hot blog post, switches technology, and by the time it doesn't fit, they can't get out.
  • Only the price tag. Open-source software is free, but someone has to install it and fix it at 3 a.m. That labor is the real bill.
  • Led by benchmark numbers. Vendor comparisons tend to use whatever conditions flatter the vendor, far from your real situation.

How it works, intuitively

  • Draw the house, then buy the tools. First decide how data should flow, how fast, and for whom. Then pick tools that do that, not the other way round.
  • Cook or order takeout. Cooking (building it yourself) lets you season exactly as you like, but you shop and wash up. Takeout (buying a service) is easy, but someone else writes the menu. Only cook the dish that is your house specialty and nobody else can make.
  • Count the full bill, and the road not taken. A tool costs more than its monthly fee: there is labor and learning time. And choosing it means giving up other paths that might be better, so keep a way to switch.
  • Some things last decades; some are gone in three years. Basic abilities like storing files and querying tables will stay, so lean on them. Trendy tools on top come and go, so be ready to swap them.
  • Pay per ride or buy a pass. If you use something now and then, paying per use is cheapest. If you use it heavily every day, a flat plan or your own equipment wins.

What it buys you / how to choose

Small team? Use ready-made managed services as much as possible and spend your energy on the business. Only when you are big enough that the bill itself becomes a major problem should you take some of it in-house. One honest cost: the more you lean on ready-made services, the easier life is, and the harder it becomes to leave that vendor.

Remember this

Choosing technology isn't picking the coolest tool. It is setting the architecture first, then picking the option that saves your team the most effort, can be swapped out, and whose full bill adds up.

In one sentence

You think technology selection is about which tool is stronger. The chapter opens with a different claim: architecture is strategic; tools are tactical. Decide the architecture first (what and why), then choose tools (how). A tool is judged not by how cool it is but by the value it adds to the business, the effort it saves the team, and what it will cost to replace it later.

Glossary

  • Managed service: software that a cloud provider or vendor installs, upgrades, backs up and scales for you; you just use it.
  • Open source (OSS): software whose source code is public and free to use and modify, such as Spark, Kafka or Airflow.
  • SaaS: software as a service, used by subscription through a web page or API, with nothing to deploy yourself.
  • CapEx / OpEx: capital expenditure (buying servers or building a data center up front) vs operating expenditure (monthly cloud bills and subscriptions).
  • Vendor lock-in: your data and processes are tied to one vendor's proprietary formats or interfaces, so switching is very expensive.
  • Egress fee: what a cloud charges per gigabyte to move data out; moving data in is usually free.
  • Serverless: compute billed per call or per execution time, with no machines to manage, such as AWS Lambda or Google BigQuery.
  • Container: a lightweight, isolated package of a program plus its dependencies that runs the same way on any machine.
  • Benchmark: a fixed set of tasks used to score and compare system performance.

What problem this chapter tackles

Data tooling exploded through the 2010s: ingestion, storage, transformation, orchestration and BI each have dozens of options with near-identical marketing. The authors see three typical failures: teams fall in love with a tool and back-fill the architecture; small teams copy big-tech self-built stacks and spend their time on operations rather than output; and people look only at the price tag, ignoring labor, migration and lock-in.

The consequences are concrete. A five-person data team running its own Hadoop or Kafka cluster can lose half its capacity to upgrades, scaling and night-time alerts. A three-year bet on one cloud's proprietary service may reveal itself as wrong only when the data has grown too big to move. The chapter offers a set of selection criteria that apply across the whole lifecycle, so choices can be explained and reversed.

The core ideas, in depth

1. Start with people: team size, speed to market, interoperability

Team size and capabilities come first. Small teams, or teams with thinner engineering skills, should lean on managed services and SaaS and spend scarce people on the business. The authors warn explicitly against cargo-cult engineering: seeing a big company publish its architecture and assuming that copying it yields the same results. They have a hundred people maintaining it; you don't.

Speed to market: a data team's value lies in delivering useful data quickly and continuously. The authors favor technology that is good enough and ships fast over endless evaluation in pursuit of perfection. Interoperability: every tool must connect upstream and downstream, so ask first which standard interfaces and formats it supports, such as JDBC/ODBC database connectivity, REST APIs, or open file formats like Parquet. A tool that interoperates poorly turns the connections themselves into a permanent project.

2. Cost: total cost of ownership, opportunity cost, FinOps

Total cost of ownership (TCO) = direct costs (servers, subscriptions, cloud bills) + indirect costs (operations staff, training, downtime). An open-source license is free, but if it needs two engineers full time, that is easily several hundred thousand dollars a year in hidden spend at US salaries. There is also CapEx vs OpEx: the cloud turns large up-front capital spend into monthly operating spend, buying the flexibility to scale down or walk away at any time.

The chapter's sharper idea is total opportunity cost of ownership (TOCO): picking one stack means forgoing the benefits of every alternative. The authors call technology that is easy to enter and hard to leave a bear trap. If a choice prevents you from moving to something better in a few years, its true cost is far above its invoice. So ask during evaluation: if this fails in two years, how long and how much to replace it?

They also push Chapter 3's FinOps further: cloud bills are dynamic, so engineering, finance and the business must watch cost continuously, the way you watch latency.

3. Today vs the future: immutable vs transitory technologies

The authors split technology in two. Immutable technologies are the time-tested foundation: object storage, networking, servers, SQL, bash, the C language, the x86 instruction set. Transitory technologies arrive fast and vanish fast; front-end JavaScript frameworks are the classic example. They invoke the Lindy effect: the longer a technology has already been around, the longer it tends to keep being around.

The strategy that follows: choose the best tool for today, but build on immutable technologies and keep the transitory layer replaceable. For example, keep data in open formats on object storage; the query engine on top can be A today and B tomorrow without moving the data. The authors advise re-evaluating tools periodically rather than once and for all.

4. Where it runs: on-premises, cloud, hybrid, multicloud

On-premises: buy and run your own machines, plan capacity for peak in advance, and scale in months. Cloud: rent on demand and reach thousands of cores in minutes. But the authors warn that the cloud is not someone else's data center: a lift and shift of an on-prem architecture is often more expensive. You need to rebuild the cloud way, e.g., object storage instead of always-on clusters, and shut things down when done.

They spend real time on cloud economics: providers slice storage and compute into products with different durability and availability and price them like financial derivatives, each with its own terms. Data goes in free and comes out for a fee; egress charges give data "gravity," and the bigger it is, the harder it is to move. Hybrid cloud commonly means analytics in the cloud and core operational systems on-prem. Multicloud lets you pick each provider's best service and reduce dependence on one vendor, at the price of egress fees and multiplied engineering complexity.

On cloud repatriation (moving back out), the authors are restrained: it makes sense only for companies at very large scale whose core product is storage or networking itself. The examples they name are Dropbox and Cloudflare. For almost everyone else, start in the cloud by default.

5. Build vs buy: open source, commercial open source, proprietary

The principle: build only where it gives you a competitive advantage; hand the rest of the "undifferentiated heavy lifting" to someone else. The buy side splits three ways:

  • Community-managed OSS: when evaluating, ask about mindshare, maturity, where to get help when things break, who maintains it, whether the roadmap is public, how much effort self-hosting takes, and whether you can contribute back.
  • Commercial OSS (COSS): the project's core team sells a managed version plus enterprise features, e.g., Databricks for Spark or Confluent for Kafka. Someone has your back and the open-source exit stays open; check the feature gap between the managed and open versions.
  • Proprietary walled gardens: closed products from independent vendors, or a cloud's own services such as BigQuery or Redshift. Best integration with that cloud and fastest start, but the deepest lock-in. Check whether pricing is transparent and whether an exit path exists.

6. Monolith vs modular, serverless vs servers

Monolith: one system does many jobs. Simple and easy to reason about, but upgrading one part touches everything. The authors single out the worst form, the distributed monolith: many machines and services on the surface, but shared dependencies that force them to upgrade together, typically many jobs crammed onto one Hadoop cluster and pinned to one version. Modular: snap replaceable components together through open formats (like Parquet) and standard interfaces, then wire them into pipelines with an orchestrator (like Airflow). The authors lean modular overall because it makes each component a two-way door; the cost is that integration and orchestration complexity lands on your team.

Serverless vs servers: serverless bills per invocation and suits small, infrequent, discrete workloads: zero operations, zero cost when idle. But its unit price is far above an always-on machine of equal power, so at high volume the bill overtakes. It also caps execution time, memory and language runtimes. The authors' suggested order: use serverless where you can; once you outgrow it, move to containers plus orchestration (e.g., Kubernetes); manage servers directly last. The key is to model usage first and find the crossover point.

The benchmark wars: the authors warn that vendor benchmarks are often rigged: absurdly small datasets used to test "big data" performance, a tuned product compared with a rival on default settings, speed compared but never cost. The remedy is to test with your own data and your own queries: caveat emptor. Finally, Chapter 2's six undercurrents (security, data management, DataOps, data architecture, orchestration, software engineering) are also a selection checklist: a tool with a crude permission model and no metadata or monitoring hooks loses points however fast it is.

Why it matters / in real systems

This chapter is the question list for a technology-selection review. Asked "why Snowflake instead of our own Spark cluster?", a good answer doesn't quote benchmarks. It states how many people the team has, how tight the deadline is, what replacing the tool in two years would cost, whether the data sits in an open format or a vendor format, and which side of the cost crossover usage falls on. The modern data stack (managed ingestion like Fivetran, Snowflake or BigQuery as the warehouse, dbt for transformation, Airflow for orchestration) is essentially the modular practice of "small teams buy as much as possible and connect through open interfaces."

Industry and open-source evidence

  • Amazon Prime Video: serverless overtaken at volume. Its audio/video quality-monitoring service was first built as a distributed serverless system on AWS Step Functions and Lambda. At scale it hit scaling bottlenecks and high orchestration and data-transfer costs; the team rebuilt it as a monolithic process on ECS and, per its engineering blog, cut infrastructure cost by more than 90%. That is the right-hand side of the crossover in Figure 2. Source: Prime Video Tech blog, "Scaling up the Prime Video audio/video monitoring service and reducing costs by 90%," 2023
  • Dropbox: only worth moving back at scale. Dropbox built its own storage system, Magic Pocket, and around 2016 moved the great majority of user data off Amazon S3 onto its own infrastructure. Storage is its core product, which is exactly the chapter's case for when repatriation makes sense. Source: Dropbox engineering blog, Magic Pocket series, 2016
  • Apache Airflow: the glue of a modular stack. The project describes itself as "a platform to programmatically author, schedule, and monitor workflows," says that workflows defined as code "become more maintainable, versionable, testable, and collaborative," and states plainly that "Airflow is not a streaming solution": know a tool's boundary before you pick it. Source: apache/airflow README, GitHub
  • Apache Parquet: the foundation of interoperability. Described as "an open source, column-oriented data file format designed for efficient data storage and retrieval," motivated by making compressed columnar data "available to any project in the Hadoop ecosystem." Keep data in such an open format and the engines on top stay swappable. Source: apache/parquet-format README, GitHub
  • dbt: the shape of commercial open source. The open repository is released under Apache 2.0, and its README explains that the commercial distribution adds dbt-specific customizations to that repository under a separate product license: the open-vs-managed split that Table 2's "Managed COSS" row tells you to check. Source: dbt-labs/dbt-core README, GitHub

Pitfalls and debates

  • "Open source is free." The license is; operations, upgrades and troubleshooting are not, and they are often the largest line in TCO.
  • "Big tech does it, so should we." Cargo cult: their architecture fits their scale and headcount.
  • "Moving to the cloud means moving the machines." Lift and shift is usually pricier; go cloud-native with on-demand compute and separated storage.
  • "Serverless always saves money." Only at low frequency and small scale; under steady heavy traffic the unit-price penalty compounds.
  • "Multicloud avoids lock-in, so default to it." It swaps lock-in risk for egress fees and complexity, a bad trade for most teams.
  • "Highest benchmark wins." Vendor benchmarks pick flattering data sizes and configs; trust only tests on your own data.
  • Limits of the moment and debate. The book came out in 2022. Since then companies such as 37signals have publicly described leaving the cloud for their own hardware, and the repatriation debate is louder than in the book, yet the authors' view that it suits only large companies with unusual cost structures remains the mainstream consensus. GPU costs from AI workloads have also reopened "build or rent" as a hot question.

Key takeaways

① Architecture is strategic, tools are tactical: architecture first, then tools; judge by business value, team effort and the cost of replacing.

② Small teams favor managed services and SaaS; beware cargo-cult copying of big tech; good enough and fast beats perfect.

③ TCO = direct costs + indirect costs like operations staff; TOCO reminds you that one path forecloses others, so avoid the easy-in, hard-out "bear trap."

④ Build on immutable technology (object storage, SQL, open formats) and keep the transitory layer replaceable.

⑤ Default to the cloud, but use it the cloud way; egress fees give data gravity; repatriation fits only big companies whose core business is storage or networking.

⑥ Build only for competitive advantage; OSS, COSS and proprietary trade control for ease, subscriptions for backing, and lock-in for speed.

⑦ Modularity keeps each component reversible; beware the distributed monolith; serverless → containers → servers, guided by the cost crossover.

⑧ Trust only benchmarks on your own data; the undercurrents (security, data management, DataOps, orchestration) belong on the selection checklist too.
