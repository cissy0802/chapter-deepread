Design Nearby Friends

System Design Interview Vol 2 · Ch 2 · Alex Xu & Sahn Lam · 2022

中文 →

What is this chapter about?

Apple has Find My. Facebook once had a feature called Nearby Friends: open a map and see which of your friends happens to be close by, with a little note saying "updated 3 minutes ago". This chapter builds that from nothing. It looks like the same kind of problem as the previous chapter — finding nearby restaurants — but the answer turns out to be almost the opposite.

A strange thing first

To find restaurants, the last chapter numbered every shop in the world and filed them into one enormous ledger sorted by location; you show up, you look it up. This chapter is looking for people, and people move every half minute. That ledger would need three million edits a second — you could never keep up. Stranger still: the right answer here is not a faster ledger. It is no ledger at all.

Why the old way breaks

Do it the old way and your phone has to ask the server, every half minute, "where are my few hundred friends right now?" — and the server has to go leaf through the ledger for each one. With a hundred million people asking at once, the servers struggle, and your phone battery does not survive until lunch. Uber worked this way early on, and has said publicly that the first thing to collapse was not the servers but the battery.

The idea: stop looking, start subscribing

Think of each person as a little broadcast channel. You and your few hundred friends each open one; you subscribe to theirs, they subscribe to yours. Whoever moves shouts into their own channel — "I'm over here now" — and only the people who subscribed can hear it. Nobody looks anything up. The locations come to you.

So who measures the distance? Whoever hears the shout. Your friend calls out, the server holding your connection hears it, holds up a ruler and checks: is he within five miles of me? If yes, show him; if not, pretend you never heard. The system never has to answer the big question "who in this city is near whom" — only a long series of tiny ones: "is he near me?"

Locations are written on notes that fade. Each person's current position is one sticky note that disappears by itself after forty seconds. The elegance is in what that buys: someone closes the app, steps into a lift, runs out of battery — their note simply evaporates, and they fade off their friends' maps on their own. Nobody has to sweep up.

What it costs, and when to use it

The whole thing works because of one precondition: you already know which few hundred people you care about. So it only handles friends. If you want to find strangers around you — a set nobody knows in advance — you have to go back to the previous chapter's big ledger. Real products run both.

And the cost is real: every phone must hold a wire open to the server at all times. The connection itself consumes resources, and even shipping a new server version means waiting for the old wires to empty out.

One line to remember

To find where your friends are, don't search — give everyone a broadcast channel, let movers shout, and let listeners measure the distance themselves. Write each position on a note that vanishes in forty seconds, so anyone who drops offline fades off the map on their own. Finding strangers is what the previous chapter's ledger is for.

In one sentence

You would expect Nearby Friends to be the previous chapter's proximity service with a different data source. The two chapters in fact run in opposite directions: Ch 1 indexes motionless places into a geohash grid and waits for you to query it, whereas here the objects move every 30 seconds and the index is stale the moment it is built — so the whole chapter's answer is don't search at all. It seizes on one precondition — your friends are a known set of a few hundred people — and demotes a geospatial search into publish/subscribe along the friend graph: locations are pushed out, and distance is computed at the receiving end. The second counterintuitive claim: this location data is supposed to rot on arrival. It goes into a cache with an expiry, dropped messages are never resent, and putting a durable queue like Kafka on the hot path is a liability rather than an asset.

Glossary

  • Long-lived connection / WebSocket: a two-way channel that stays open once established. Plain HTTP only lets the client ask and the server answer; WebSocket lets the server speak first, at any time.
  • Publish/subscribe (pub/sub): a "listen if you care to" messaging pattern — a publisher drops a message into a channel, everyone subscribed to that channel gets a copy, and the publisher never needs to know who is listening.
  • Fan-out: how many recipients one message must be copied to. One location update going to 200 online friends is a fan-out of 200.
  • Stateful service: a machine holding facts like "this user's connection belongs to me" in its own memory, so another machine cannot take over; a stateless service can serve any request from any box.
  • TTL (time-to-live): a lifespan attached to a piece of data; it deletes itself when the clock runs out, with nobody sweeping up.
  • QPS: queries per second, the basic unit of load.
  • Consistent hashing: a way of spreading keys over N machines so that adding or removing one moves only a small share of keys (the subject of Vol 1 Ch 5).
  • Service discovery: a small authoritative registry every machine can consult (usually etcd or ZooKeeper) recording which machine currently owns what.
  • Geohash: encoding a latitude/longitude pair as a string where a shared prefix means physical proximity — the star of the previous chapter.
  • Cassandra: a wide-column database that scales out by partition key and takes appends extremely fast, well suited to enormous streams of history.
  • Heartbeat: a small packet the client sends periodically to say "I'm still here, the connection is alive".

Where it sits

This is Ch 2 of System Design Interview Vol 2, immediately after Ch 1's proximity service: the previous chapter handles things that don't move (restaurants, petrol stations), this one handles people who do, and Ch 3 on Google Maps carries the geography line into road graphs and navigation. Its real-world counterparts are Apple's Find My, the car crawling across your Uber map, the green dot on a Discord avatar. It is also the first time the book puts a stateful connection tier on the table.

The problem it solves

The requirement is small: open the app, see friends within 5 miles with their distance and an "updated N minutes ago" stamp, refreshed every 30 seconds. The scale is not: one billion registered users, of whom perhaps 10% use the feature — 100 million active users.

Do the arithmetic first. One report per person per 30 seconds gives 100M ÷ 30 ≈ 3.3 million location updates per second. For comparison, the previous chapter's proximity service ran at roughly 5,000 read QPS with almost no writes — this chapter's write volume is three orders of magnitude higher, and every write has to spread to a group of people immediately. The old approach hits three walls:

First, the index cannot take it. A geohash grid index only works when the indexed objects rarely move; 3.3 million coordinate changes a second means 3.3 million index deletes-plus-inserts a second, and write amplification will flatten any B-tree.

Second, polling cannot take it. Have each client ask "where are my friends" every 30 seconds and you get 3.3 million queries a second, each pulling a few hundred friends' coordinates. Uber did exactly this early on, and later said publicly that the first thing to fail on that path was not the servers but phone batteries.

Third, and most easily missed: the server needs the right to speak first. When a friend moves, the server learns of it first, yet HTTP's shape is "client asks, server answers" — the server has no standing to open its mouth. To make "my friend moves and my screen updates" possible, you first need a channel the server can write into unprompted.

The core content, properly

The flip: when the candidate set is known, you needn't search

The previous chapter asked "what restaurants are around me" — the candidate set is unknown, it could be any shop on earth, so the world must be indexed first. This chapter looks like it asks "which friends are around me", but it is really asking something of a different nature: among my few hundred friends, who is close to me right now. The candidate set is known in advance, and it holds hundreds, not billions.

Once you see that, the problem changes species: no geospatial index is needed, only a friend list (which you already have) and a route to push the location down. A search problem has been demoted to a delivery problem — that is the chapter's one core trick, and every component below merely paves that route. State the cost up front: it fails completely for strangers, since you cannot pre-subscribe to someone you have not met.

The connection tier: giving the server a voice, and paying for it

The first component is a WebSocket server: every online user holds an open two-way channel to it, so the server can push down whenever it likes. The price is that this tier is stateful — "user B's connection is held by box 7" exists only in box 7's memory. Three bills follow:

  • Machine count is set by connections, not QPS. One box tops out at a few hundred thousand open sockets (bounded by memory and file descriptors), so 100 million online users means hundreds of boxes minimum — even if nobody says a word.
  • Deploys and scale-ups get slow. A stateless service can be swapped in seconds; a connection tier cannot. You steer new connections away and then wait for the old ones to drain.
  • Reconnect storms. When a box dies, its several hundred thousand clients all reconnect at once; without randomised backoff (jitter), that surge takes down whichever box inherits them.

Pub/sub: one channel per person

The second component is the route the location travels: give every user a channel, channel:{user_id}, meaning "this person's location broadcast", and let each of A's online friends subscribe to it via whichever WS server holds them. One update then runs as in Fig 2: phone A reports its coordinates → the server writes the cache and publishes to channel:A → every WS server subscribed to that channel receives a copy → each one computes the distance per relevant connection it holds and pushes down only those within 5 miles.

Why distance is computed at the receiving end. The radius is each recipient's own business: B cares how far A is from B, C cares how far A is from C, and the same broadcast has a different answer for each. Computed at the receiver, the publisher never needs to know where anyone is, and the pub/sub layer in the middle degenerates into a pure pipe — it does not understand geography and does not need to.

The scale of the fan-out. Each update is copied to every online friend of the publisher. Taking a few hundred friends each with roughly a tenth online at any moment, the fan-out multiplier sits in the tens; multiply by 3.3 million updates a second and total system deliveries land on the order of hundreds of millions per second. That number explains both why pub/sub has to become a cluster and why no step on this path may be heavy — one extra disk write, one extra network hop, multiplied by that, is a catastrophe.

Where locations live: a cache designed to rot, plus a history bypass

The third component is storage, and it splits into two halves of entirely different character.

The hot half is just a key-value cache: user_id → (lat, lng, timestamp) in Redis, with a TTL set slightly longer than the refresh interval (40 seconds against a 30-second cadence). Here sits the prettiest detail in the chapter: one mechanism solves two problems. It makes stale positions expire by themselves — you never see a two-hour-old coordinate presented as "now" — and it makes offline detection free. A user closes the app, steps into a lift, runs out of battery; no component has to notice, no "went offline" message is needed, the entry evaporates after 40 seconds and friends' maps quietly drop the dot. Offline logic you never wrote is offline logic that cannot be wrong.

The cold half is location history, appended to something like Cassandra (partition key user_id, clustering key the timestamp). What matters is that it stays off the hot path: history is useless for live display and serves only replay and analytics, so it goes asynchronously and tolerates lag, and its hiccups must never propagate back into the push path — in practice it lands in Kafka first and is consumed into storage. A sense of cost, too: 3.3 million records a second at tens of bytes each is a dozen-odd terabytes of raw writes per day, which makes "do we keep history, and for how long" a product question that has to be answered here, not a technical footnote.

Growing pub/sub into a cluster

A single Redis cannot absorb 3.3 million PUBLISH calls a second and the hundreds of millions of deliveries behind them, so pub/sub has to be a cluster. The unit of sharding is the channel: consistent-hash channel:{user_id} across N servers and record ownership in service discovery (etcd or ZooKeeper), which a WS server consults before subscribing.

The cost model is not what intuition suggests: a channel is merely an entry in a "channel → subscriber list" map, and one with no subscribers does not exist at all — so even with a billion user IDs, the number of real channels equals only those online and subscribed to. The expense is entirely CPU: every message walks the subscriber list and is delivered copy by copy, so bigger fan-out is what costs, while the channel count barely matters.

Channels migrate during scale-out and failure, and a few location messages can be lost in that window. Losing them is perfectly acceptable here, for a hard reason: a location is an idempotent full snapshot, not a delta, so the next 30-second report repairs the state on its own, and the consequence of a drop is that one dot moves half a minute late. That judgement drives the whole of the next section.

Adding "nearby strangers": now the previous chapter earns its keep

If the requirement grows into "show any nearby user, not just friends", this design runs out — you cannot pre-subscribe to someone you don't know. The right answer is to run both: friends keep the pub/sub push, strangers go back to the previous chapter's grid index — the cache maintains "geohash cell → the online users currently in it", and a query takes the union of your own cell and its neighbours. Because only online users are held and the set entries carry a TTL as well, this index is far smaller than "all users".

Why it matters / real systems

What actually transfers out of this chapter is not the Nearby Friends product but the general shape of state broadcast: a crowd of clients holds long connections, a change to some entity fans out along a known relationship graph, and each receiver decides whether to display it. Swap "location" for "presence" and you get the green dot on an avatar; swap it for "order status" and you get the courier crawling across a delivery app; swap it for "document cursor" and you get the coloured flags in collaborative editing.

The high-scoring moves in an interview are equally fixed: do the 3.3M QPS arithmetic first to show why the previous chapter's approach is out; name the known candidate set and convert a search problem into a delivery one; point out that the TTL also buys offline detection; and be willing to argue that reliable delivery is not wanted here — that last one separates someone who memorised an architecture diagram from someone who thought about it.

Pitfalls and open questions

  • Assuming this path needs reliable delivery. A location is an idempotent snapshot; a drop is repaired next cycle. Wrapping it in a durable queue buys latency, cost and a replay capability you will never use. But be clear about the boundary: the judgement only holds for idempotent state broadcast — move the same architecture onto chat messages and it is simply wrong, because there a lost message is lost forever.
  • Treating the TTL as mere housekeeping. Here it carries semantics: expired means offline. Set it shorter than the refresh interval and online friends flicker; set it far longer and departed users linger on the map.
  • Underestimating the operational tail of a stateful tier: draining deploys, reconnect storms, and load balancers' helplessness with long connections — once established, a connection is never rebalanced, so load skews persistently and a freshly added box can sit idle for hours.
  • A fixed 30-second interval is the battery's enemy. Real products go adaptive: back off or stop while stationary, tighten up while moving fast. The book's fixed cadence exists to keep the arithmetic clean.
  • Counter-evidence from the product side. Facebook's Nearby Friends launched in 2014 and was shut down on 31 May 2022, officially for low usage. The engineering works fine; what stopped it was willingness to share — far fewer people want to broadcast a live location than you would guess. For systems like this, the controls over who sees you, how precisely, and for how long often decide the outcome more than the architecture does.
  • Dated in one respect. The book was written in 2022 and assumes you build it yourself; managed WebSocket gateways from the cloud providers have since turned this tier into something purchasable, and at a scale of hundreds of connection boxes "build or buy" is a sum worth redoing.

Key takeaways

① In one line: when the candidate set is known, don't search — flip "nearby friends" from a geospatial query into publish/subscribe along the friend graph.

② Scale dictates shape: 100M active users × one report per 30s = 3.3 million location updates per second, which rules out both indexing and polling at once.

③ Long connections are mandatory: HTTP gives the server no right to speak first, hence the WebSocket tier — stateful, and sized by connection count rather than QPS.

④ One channel per person: channel:{user_id}, subscribed on a friend's behalf by whichever server holds them; the pub/sub layer is a pure pipe with no notion of geography.

⑤ Distance is computed at the receiver: the same broadcast has a different answer per recipient, so receiver-side computation is what lets everyone hold their own radius and lets the middle stay ignorant.

⑥ The TTL is semantics, not cleanup: a 40-second expiry retires stale positions and delivers offline detection at the same time — offline logic you never wrote cannot be wrong. Storage splits accordingly, with the history stream written asynchronously off the hot path.

⑦ Reliable delivery is not wanted here: a location is an idempotent snapshot that self-heals in 30 seconds, so Kafka on the hot path is a liability — swap in chat messages and the judgement instantly reverses.

⑧ Pub/sub is consistent-hashed by channel into a cluster with service discovery tracking ownership; the expense is CPU fan-out, not channel count. Finding strangers means returning to Ch 1's geohash grid — real products run both.
