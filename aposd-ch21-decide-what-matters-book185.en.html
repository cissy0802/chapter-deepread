Decide What Matters

A Philosophy of Software Design · Ch 21 · Decide What Matters · Ousterhout · 2nd ed 2021

中文 →

What is this chapter about?

Open the camera on your phone and you can shoot right away: focus, exposure and white balance are all decided for you, and the one big thing on screen is the shutter button. That is this chapter's idea: good design first separates what matters from what doesn't, then puts what matters front and center and quietly tucks the rest away.

A curious fact first

The author added this chapter specifically for the second edition. The first twenty chapters cover deep modules, information hiding, comments, names… Looking back, he saw that all of that advice is really one thing: every rule is a way of telling what matters from what doesn't.

Why the old way was hard

Programmers tend to make one of two mistakes. One is treating everything as important: a fifty-page manual, every switch put in front of the user, and the one thing that really matters drowns. The other is hiding the important thing in the wrong place: something users must know is buried in a corner, discovered only when it bites.

How it works, intuitively

  • Look for leverage. Important things share a trait: solve one and many other problems go away; understand one and many other things become easy. Like moving house — place the big furniture first and the small things find their spots.
  • Keep the important things few. Whatever you can decide for users, don't make them worry about — like a camera's auto mode; most people never touch the pro settings.
  • Make what matters easy to see. Put it where everyone looks first, let it show up again and again, and arrange everything else around it, like the atrium of a mall that every shop faces.
  • Hide what doesn't. Not delete it — put it in a cupboard. Those who need it can find it; everyone else never sees it.

What it gives you / how to choose

Code, documents, a presentation — same move: first ask "what are the two or three things that really matter here," then organize the rest around them. One honest cost: there is no formula for deciding what matters; you often have to guess first and adjust as you go, and the first guess is rarely complete.

Remember it in one line

Separate what matters from what doesn't; keep what matters few and visible, and hide everything else.

In one sentence

You might think the first twenty chapters are twenty unrelated tricks; the chapter new to the second edition ties them into one: one of the most important parts of good design is separating what matters from what doesn't — structure the system around what matters and make it visible; minimize the impact of what doesn't on the rest of the system and hide it.

Glossary

  • Module: a unit of code with an interface and an implementation — a class, a service, or a subsystem.
  • Interface: what users of a module must know — method signatures, parameters, behavioral contracts.
  • Implementation: the code behind the interface that does the work; users don't need to know it.
  • Deep module: from Chapter 4 — a module with a small interface that does a lot behind it.
  • Information hiding: from Chapter 5 — sealing design decisions inside a module so nothing outside sees or depends on them.
  • Default: the value a system uses when the user doesn't specify one, so most users never face the option.
  • Cognitive load: from Chapter 2 — how much a developer must know before making a change.
  • Unknown unknowns: from Chapter 2 — things you don't know you need to know; the worst symptom of complexity.

Where it sits

Chapter 21, new in the second edition (2021), after Chapter 20 "Designing for Performance" and before Chapter 22 "Conclusion." It adds no new technique; it gives every earlier technique a common root. In practice it maps to every "what should this interface expose" or "what should this design doc lead with" decision.

What problem this chapter solves

After twenty chapters the reader holds a long list of rules: make modules deep, hide information, define errors out of existence, comment what isn't obvious, choose precise names… The trouble is that a long list of rules is itself a burden, and in concrete cases the rules pull against each other: should this parameter be exposed? Does this detail belong in the interface comment?

This chapter offers a deeper test that still points a direction when the rules run out or conflict: decide what matters first. Skip that and the typical result is "equal weight everywhere" — a class has 20 public methods, only 3 of which you need day to day, yet they're listed and commented just like the other 17, so a newcomer has to read all of them to learn which 3 count. That's how cognitive load piles up.

The core ideas, explained

1. How to tell what matters: look for leverage

The author's clue is leverage: something matters if solving it also solves many other problems, or if knowing it makes many other things easy to understand.

Intuitively, the important things are the decisions that, once made, settle much of the rest. Take the text editor from Chapter 6: what matters is a general pair of operations — insert or delete text at a position. "Handle the backspace key" or "delete the selection" are UI-level uses; with the general pair in hand, each takes a few lines. Do it the other way — one method on the text class per UI action — and the interface fills up with things that don't matter.

The judgment isn't made once. Early in a design you may not be able to tell, so make a guess, organize the system around it, and revise as you use it — the same attitude as Chapter 11's "Design It Twice": judgment grows out of comparison.

2. Keep the important things few

The most overlooked point in the chapter: the fewer things that matter, the simpler the system. Every extra thing users must care about is extra cognitive load. So the goal isn't only finding what matters but actively making more things not matter.

Earlier chapters are concrete tools for exactly this: deep modules pull details into the implementation (Ch 4); information hiding keeps design decisions inside the module (Ch 5); pulling complexity downward means a configuration parameter the system can compute itself shouldn't be handed to users (Ch 8); defining errors out of existence means callers never have to think about that case (Ch 10). A sensible default for the common case is the same idea — most users never need to know the option exists.

CS Papers · On Decomposing Systems into Modules (Parnas 1972) — the original information-hiding paper: draw module lines around decisions likely to change, and lock the unimportant inside

3. How to emphasize what matters: prominence, repetition, centrality

Once you've found what matters, readers need to see it at a glance. The author gives three tools:

  • Prominence: put it where it's most likely to be seen — interface documentation, names, parameters of heavily used methods. An important concept belongs in a class or method name, not in a comment on line 300.
  • Repetition: key ideas that show up again and again in the code get remembered.
  • Centrality: put the most important things at the heart of the system, where they determine the structure around them. Think of an operating system's device driver interface: hundreds of drivers are written against it, so once it's fixed, the shape of the whole driver subsystem is fixed.

Conversely, things that don't matter should be hidden as much as possible: kept out of the interface and off the hot path, findable in the implementation or a corner of the docs by those who need them, invisible to everyone else.

4. Two common mistakes

The author names two errors that point in opposite directions:

  • Treating too many things as important. This is the most common one. The result is clutter: interfaces stuffed with methods and parameters users don't need, so modules go shallow; docs that flag every line as "note," so readers stop reading. Chapter 4's complaint about Java file reading — you must wrap the stream in a separate buffering stream yourself — belongs here: buffering should be the default, yet every user has to think about it.
  • Failing to recognize what really matters. The result is important information hidden or missing: a constraint users must know isn't in the interface, so it becomes one of Chapter 2's "unknown unknowns"; or important functionality isn't provided, so every user works around it and rebuilds it.

Guard against both together. Guard only against the first and you hide what should be exposed; only against the second and you put everything on stage. The test stays the same: what is the least a user must know to do the common task?

5. Thinking more broadly

The author ends by pushing the principle beyond software: writing technical documentation or giving a talk works the same way — decide what the audience most needs to take away, then make everything else serve it. In his view, much of what we call design "taste" is the ability to tell what matters from what doesn't.

The test: each time you add something users must care about, ask is it worth making every user remember it? If not, default it, push it down, or define it away. Google's style guide, below, turns that question into an explicit rule.

Why it matters / in real systems

The chapter's value is a meta-test for the whole book. Reviewing an interface, first ask "what are the two or three things that matter most for this class, and are they in its name and the first lines of its docs?" Writing a design doc, list the three key decisions before anything else. For every new config option, explain why it can't have a default. Asked in an interview "how do you judge whether an API is well designed," starting from "the least a user must know" lands better than reciting REST conventions.

Industry evidence · public record

  • Google · C++ Style Guide states among its goals that "Style rules should pull their weight": the benefit of a rule must be large enough to justify asking all engineers to remember it — and, it says, this principle mostly explains the rules Google doesn't have. The same section says "Optimize for the reader, not the writer." That's "keep the important things few" in practice: every rule spends everyone's attention. Source: google/styleguide · cppguide.html
  • Go (Google) · Rob Pike, "Go Proverbs" (Gopherfest 2015) includes "Gofmt's style is no one's favorite, yet gofmt is everyone's favorite" — one shared tool turns code formatting from a recurring argument into something that doesn't matter — and "Make the zero value useful" — a type that works without initialization means users never have to think about that step. Source: go-proverbs/go-proverbs.github.io

Pitfalls and debates

  • "Doesn't matter" ≠ "delete it." Most unimportant things still need to exist; they just shouldn't occupy users' attention. Hide them, default them — don't cut the feature.
  • Importance depends on the reader. A buffer size that doesn't matter to an app developer may matter a lot to someone tuning performance. The usual answer is layering: a simple default interface for most, an advanced entry point for the few.
  • It isn't an algorithm. "Look for leverage" is a clue, not a formula; two senior engineers can answer differently for the same system. Critics may call the chapter too "philosophical" to test — the author's answer is to guess, then correct through use.
  • Don't turn "prominent" into "everywhere." Repetition emphasizes, but copying the same information into ten places means ten edits per change — Chapter 2's change amplification again. Repeat concepts and names, not code.
  • A default is a decision too. If what you hide is chosen badly, everyone is affected without knowing it; pick defaults for the most common case and document them where they can be found.
  • Edition note. This chapter is new in the second edition and absent from the first; the author offers an extract of the new chapters on his home page.

Key takeaways

① One of the most important parts of good design: separate what matters from what doesn't, and organize the system around what matters.

② Find importance through leverage: solving one solves many; understanding one explains many.

③ You can guess first and correct with use — the same attitude as "Design It Twice."

④ Keep the important things few: deep modules, information hiding, pulling complexity down, defining errors away, and good defaults are all tools for this.

⑤ Three ways to emphasize: prominence (names, interface docs, hot-method parameters), repetition, centrality.

⑥ Hide what doesn't matter — don't delete it.

⑦ Two mistakes: treating too much as important (more common; shallow modules), and missing what really matters (unknown unknowns).

⑧ Evidence: Google's C++ guide says rules must pull their weight; Go uses gofmt to make formatting not matter and zero values to skip initialization.

⑨ The principle reaches past code: writing and talks work the same way; much of "taste" is telling what matters from what doesn't.
