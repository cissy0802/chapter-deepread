Scope of Architecture Characteristics: The Boundary Is Not the System, It Is the Architecture Quantum

Fundamentals of Software Architecture · Ch 7 · Mark Richards & Neal Ford · 2020

中文 →

What is this chapter about?

The previous chapters taught you to pick the few qualities a system must have: handle crowds, stay up, be easy to change. This chapter asks: do you set one list of qualities for the whole system, or a different list for each part? Think of an auction website. The part where viewers watch the live stream and place bids can see tens of thousands of people rush in at once; the part that settles payments with sellers handles a few hundred a day. Holding both to the same standard is either wasteful or not enough.

An analogy: departments in a hospital

In a hospital, the emergency room must be staffed around the clock and see patients within minutes. The check-up clinic can close on weekends. The pharmacy's top priority is never handing out the wrong pill. Nobody would require every department to run like the ER — far too expensive. But if the ER has to stand and wait for the lab's results during surgery, the lab must be just as on-call as the ER, because the ER is stuck until it answers. This chapter is about figuring out which parts of a system count as the same department and must meet the same standard.

Why the old way was hard

People used to assume a system was one big block: shipped together, sharing one database, so its qualities could only be set once. Once systems were split into many small services, that changed, but there was no ready-made ruler for where the lines go. Looking at which piece of code calls which is too fine. Counting how many programs get shipped is too coarse — two programs that share one database are still tied together.

The core idea: see who is tied to whom

The book offers a ruler called the "architecture quantum": the smallest whole chunk that can be shipped on its own and do its job on its own. To tell whether two pieces are the same quantum, ask three things. First, can they ship separately — counting the database each one can't live without, so sharing a database makes them one chunk. Second, are they doing the same job — say, everything about customer details. Third, does one have to wait on the spot for the other's answer? Like a phone call: if the other side doesn't pick up, you just hang there, so both sides need matching stamina. Switch to leaving a voicemail, and the other side can reply later without holding you up — the two come apart.

What it buys you / how to choose

With this ruler you can set qualities chunk by chunk: the bidding part is built to "never stall under a crowd," the payment part to "never lose a cent." If the whole system measures out as one chunk with one set of needs, building it as one block is simplest; only when the parts need very different things is splitting worth it. The honest cost: to let chunks follow different standards, you must replace "wait on the spot" with "leave a message," and the system gets harder to trace and harder to keep perfectly in sync at every moment.

One line to remember

Qualities aren't set for "the whole system" but for each chunk that ships on its own and waits on itself — draw the chunks right first, and the qualities follow.

One sentence

You think architecture characteristics are properties of "the system." In fact their scope is the architecture quantum: an independently deployable unit with high functional cohesion whose parts are bound together by synchronous calls. FoSA Chapter 7 uses this ruler to answer "where does this set of characteristics apply?" and, along the way, gives the first test for monolith versus distributed — one set of characteristics means one quantum; only several clearly different sets call for several quanta.

Glossary

  • Architecture characteristics: from Chapter 4 — requirements that aren't business features, shape the structure, and are critical to success, such as scalability and reliability.
  • Deployment: putting a packaged program onto servers and running it; "independently deployable" means it can go live without other parts going live with it.
  • Monolith: the whole application packaged as one deployment unit, usually attached to one database.
  • Microservices: a system split into many small services, each deployed on its own and usually owning its data; covered in Chapter 17.
  • Coupling and cohesion: coupling is how much two parts depend on each other; cohesion is whether everything inside one part serves the same purpose (Chapter 3).
  • Synchronous / asynchronous call: synchronous means sending a request and waiting in place for the reply, like a phone call; asynchronous means dropping off a message and moving on, like a voicemail.
  • Message queue: middleware for asynchronous communication; the sender drops messages in, the receiver takes them out at its own pace.
  • Elasticity: the ability to handle sudden bursts — scaling out automatically when traffic spikes and back in afterwards; distinct from scalability (keeping performance as load grows steadily).

Where it sits

Part I, "Foundations," Chapter 7 — the last of the four chapters on characteristics: Chapter 4 defines them, Chapter 5 identifies them, Chapter 6 measures and governs them, and this one sets their scope. It also bridges to Part II: from Chapter 9 the book covers architecture styles, and Chapter 18, on choosing a style, uses the number of quanta directly as the monolith-versus-distributed test. In practice: should this service be split, which services can scale independently, which calls should move onto a message queue.

The problem this chapter solves

In the monolith era, characteristics were naturally system-wide: one package, one database; it all got faster together or scaled together. In a distributed world, the needs of different parts of one system can differ by orders of magnitude. Take the book's auction system Going, Going, Gone (the architecture kata from Chapter 5): nationwide online auctions, participants numbering in the thousands or effectively unbounded, bids as close to real time as possible. When an auction opens, traffic on the live-stream-and-bidding side may jump a hundredfold in an instant, while the auctioneer side is a single person on stage who must never drop off.

Without a fix? Either you apply the highest bar everywhere — to give bidding its elasticity, the whole system is built to scale out on demand with full redundancy, and cost and complexity explode together — or you apply the average bar everywhere, and bidding collapses at peak. The sneakier third failure: the architect believes the system is already split and assigns two services different characteristics, but they call each other synchronously or share a database, so they are still welded together — two sets on paper, one set at runtime. The code-level coupling metrics from Chapter 3 (afferent and efferent coupling and the like) are too fine-grained to see this kind of binding. This chapter supplies an architecture-level unit for drawing the line.

The core ideas, in depth

① Start from connascence: what "tied together" means

The book first revisits connascence from Chapter 3: two components are connascent if changing one requires changing the other to keep the system correct. It comes in two kinds. Static connascence is visible in source code — sharing a type, agreeing on parameter order. Dynamic connascence appears only at runtime — order of calls, timing, values that must agree. The key insight here: the most dangerous bindings at the architecture level are often not in the code but at runtime. Two services can have completely separate code, yet if one calls the other synchronously, their fates are joined while running. On top of that, business concepts themselves bind parts of a system together semantically, creating functional cohesion. Code-level metrics see neither kind of binding.

② The three-part definition of an architecture quantum

The book's definition: an architecture quantum is an independently deployable artifact with high functional cohesion and synchronous connascence. Taking each part in turn:

Independently deployable: a quantum contains everything it needs to run on its own, the database included. So a monolith with one database has a quantum count of 1; microservices that each own a database may form several quanta. Conversely, services deployed separately but sharing one database can't live without that database, so they remain one quantum — which is exactly why many "microservice migrations" end up as a distributed monolith.

High functional cohesion: everything inside a quantum serves one business purpose. The book aligns this with the bounded context of domain-driven design (DDD): each context carries its own code and data, its concepts are internally consistent, and duplication across contexts is preferred to sharing. "One service per bounded context" in microservices is drawing quanta by functional cohesion.

Synchronous connascence: synchronous calls exist inside the quantum, or among the services that make it up. The book's argument is direct: if service A calls service B synchronously, the two can't differ wildly in operational characteristics (scalability, performance, availability and so on) — if the caller is far more scalable than the callee, timeouts and reliability problems follow. A synchronous call creates dynamic connascence for its duration: while one waits on the other, their operational characteristics must match.

③ Sync vs async: which line the quantum boundary follows

The third clause hands the architect a practical lever: communication style sets the quantum boundary. If two services talk by synchronous request–response, they are one quantum for the duration of the call and their operational characteristics must line up. Put a message queue between them and the sender returns as soon as the message is dropped off; a slow receiver just makes the queue longer instead of making the sender time out — asynchronous communication cuts one quantum into two, each free to have its own scalability and availability targets.

What this insight does is promote "sync or async" from an implementation detail to an architecture decision: choosing sync declares that two services share one set of scalability and availability targets; only async actually buys the freedom to govern them separately.

④ Case study: the auction system as three quanta

The book returns to Going, Going, Gone. Examining it part by part, the architect finds that different parts need clearly different characteristics and draws three quanta: Bidder feedback (the bid stream and video stream; availability, scalability, performance), Auctioneer (the live auctioneer; availability, reliability, scalability, elasticity, performance, security), and Bidder (online bidders and bidding; reliability, availability, scalability, elasticity).

The value is not the three boxes themselves but that each set of characteristics constrains only its own box: the auctioneer side's strict demands on security and reliability needn't spread to bidder feedback, and bidder feedback's performance demands needn't drag the auctioneer side along. Here the book only draws quanta and does not yet pick a style; turning quanta into an architecture is left for later chapters.

⑤ The quantum count is the first fork in choosing a style

The chapter's most practical corollary: if the whole system needs only one set of architecture characteristics, a monolith is usually enough; if parts need clearly different characteristics, you need several quanta, which pushes toward a distributed architecture. In other words, distributed is not "more advanced" — it is the price paid so that different parts can have different characteristics. This plants the key question of Chapter 18 early.

The core judgment is first ask whether the characteristics diverge, then ask whether to split — not the other way round. After splitting, check that the quantum boundaries are real: shared libraries, synchronous call chains and tables shared across services all quietly weld several quanta back into one.

Why it matters / in real systems

Industry evidence · claims from public write-ups (paraphrased)
  • Amazon Prime Video (2023): its audio/video quality-monitoring service was first assembled from distributed components (an orchestration service, serverless functions, intermediate results in object storage). The team said it hit scaling limits and high cost at scale; after moving the components into a single process, infrastructure cost fell by more than 90%. In this chapter's terms: components handing data to each other frame by frame, with the same characteristics, were one quantum all along. Prime Video Tech blog, "Scaling up the Prime Video audio/video monitoring service and reducing costs by 90%", 2023
  • Segment (2018): it had split out a separate service per data destination, more than 140 of them. In "Goodbye Microservices" the team described how these services all did the same kind of work, and the operational load and diverging shared-library versions from splitting them slowed delivery, so they merged them back into a single service. Segment Engineering blog, Alexandra Noonan, "Goodbye Microservices: From 100s of problem children to 1 superstar", 2018

Both cautionary tales point to one conclusion: the reason to split should be "different characteristics," not "services should be small." In an architecture review, after drawing the service diagram, draw a quantum diagram on top: connect shared libraries, sync calls and shared tables, and see how many truly independent boxes remain. In an interview, when asked "why split (or not split) this service," answering "does its scalability and availability target differ from its neighbors'? If so, I cut it into its own quantum with async messaging; otherwise I merge it" is exactly this chapter's skill.

Pitfalls and debates

  • "Number of deployment units = number of quanta" is wrong: shared databases and tight synchronous call chains merge several deployment units into one quantum. Many "microservice" systems have exactly one quantum.
  • Synchronous connascence doesn't mean "sync requires identical": the book says operational characteristics can't differ to extremes; light, infrequent sync lookups can be softened with timeouts, circuit breakers and caches, at the price of extra complexity.
  • Async is not free decoupling: it trades runtime binding for eventual consistency, duplicate messages and painful troubleshooting; and the message format is still a contract that needs coordination when it changes.
  • The definition keeps evolving: in their later book Software Architecture: The Hard Parts (2021), the authors refine it to look at both static and dynamic coupling, a finer test than this book's version.
  • Functional cohesion is a judgment call: there's no formula for cutting bounded contexts; one business can be cut several reasonable ways.
  • Don't treat quanta as a goal for splitting: a quantum is an analysis tool for describing the scope of characteristics; more quanta does not mean a better architecture.

Takeaways

① The scope of architecture characteristics is not the whole system but the architecture quantum.

② Quantum = independently deployable + high functional cohesion + synchronous connascence.

③ The database counts: a monolith plus one database is 1 quantum; services sharing a database are still 1 quantum.

④ During a synchronous call, both sides' operational characteristics must match; the slow side sets the cap.

⑤ Async messaging splits one quantum into two, buying independent scaling at the cost of eventual consistency and harder debugging.

⑥ Code-level coupling metrics are too fine to see runtime and business-semantic bindings.

⑦ Auction case: bidder feedback, auctioneer and bidder are three quanta, each with its own characteristics.

⑧ One set of characteristics → a monolith is usually enough; several different sets → that's when you need distributed.

⑨ Interview line: "First see whether characteristics diverge, then decide whether to split; once split, hold the boundary with async."
