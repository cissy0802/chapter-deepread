Design a Stock Exchange

What is this chapter about?

You tap "buy" on your phone and a few milliseconds later it says "filled". The machine that paired you with a seller is a stock exchange, and this chapter teaches you how to build one. It is the strangest chapter in the book: the previous twelve teach you to spread work across more machines. This one goes the other way — it pulls the most critical work back into a single thread on a single machine.

An analogy first

Picture a farmers' market with two lists on the wall: everything people are willing to pay, and everything people are willing to accept, each sorted best-first. Someone shouts "I'll pay 10". The clerk checks the sell list for anything at 10 or cheaper: if there is, they pair it on the spot; if not, the slip goes up on the wall in price order to wait. Among equal prices, whoever arrived first goes first. That wall is the "order book", and that rule is "price first, then time".

The hard part isn't the crowd

You would guess an exchange is hard because of volume. It isn't. The book budgets a billion orders a day; spread over a six-and-a-half-hour session that's about forty-three thousand a second — lighter than a big company's own monitoring system.

The hard parts are three others: be absurdly fast (a hair slower and someone else takes the price you wanted); never let anyone cut the line (first come, first served is a legal obligation here, not a nicety); and never get a single one wrong, while being able to rewind the tape frame by frame, because a regulator can always ask what exactly happened in that one second.

Its answer: let only one person work

If ten clerks edited that wall at once, they'd have to take turns — and who actually got there first would be genuinely hard to say. Once the order is fuzzy, line-cutting has already happened and nobody can reconstruct it afterwards. So the exchange does something plain: one clerk, and only one.

But it puts a ticket machine in front of that clerk. However many lanes orders arrive on, each takes a number at the door — 1, 2, 3 — written into a ledger, and they go in strictly by number. Two good things follow at once. The order is nailed down by the numbers, so cutting in is impossible. And replaying that ledger reproduces the market exactly as it was at any instant — on a different machine you get an identical result, which is why a standby can take over at any moment.

As for "won't one clerk be slow" — no. The work is all in memory: glance at two lists, cross out a few lines. The British exchange LMAX has publicly said its single thread handles six million orders a second. A machine's time goes into the network and the operating system, not into the matching. The cost is real, though: one stock's wall lives on one machine, so you grow only by giving different stocks to different machines — you cannot scale a single stock.

One line to remember

An exchange doesn't win with more machines. It wins with one queue order nobody can alter: take a number at the door, let one thread work strictly by number, and keep the ledger so you can rewind. Speed, fairness and forensics all grow out of that one move.

In one line

The closing chapter of Vol 2, and the only problem in the book whose answer is "don't make it distributed": an exchange's matching core must be single-threaded, in-memory and deterministic — because what it needs is not throughput but microsecond latency, strict price-time priority, and order-by-order replayability. Every distributed technique (replicas, scaling, recovery) is pushed outside that thread, behind a sequencer that flattens concurrent input into one immutable stream.

Glossary

  • Order book: every resting, unfilled order for one symbol, with the buy side and sell side each sorted by price.
  • Limit vs market order: a limit order says "buy only at X or better"; a market order takes whatever the best price is, right now.
  • Matching: pairing an incoming buy against resting sells (or vice versa) under the exchange's rules.
  • Price-time priority: better price wins; at equal price, earlier arrival wins. This is the exchange's definition of fairness.
  • Spread: the gap between best bid and best ask; a tighter spread means a more liquid market.
  • Market data: the price and depth information the exchange broadcasts, offered at three levels — L1, L2, L3.
  • Event sourcing: store the stream of events that caused state changes, not the current state; state is what you get by replaying the stream.
  • Determinism: the same input stream produces byte-identical output, however many times you run it and on whatever machine.
  • Colocation: brokers rent racks in the exchange's own data hall, cutting out the milliseconds of long-haul transmission.

Where it sits

The last chapter of Vol 2 and, by common consent, the hardest. It is where two earlier chapters meet: Ch4 (building a message queue) taught you the append-only log, Ch12 (digital wallet) taught you event sourcing plus a replicated state machine, and this chapter pushes both to the latency extreme. The real-world counterparts are NYSE, Nasdaq, CME and LMAX — and anything else that needs determinism, replay and microseconds: matching, real-time risk checks, lockstep game servers.

What the chapter is solving

Start with the numbers, because they invert the usual intuition. The book budgets roughly 100 symbols and a billion orders a day; the NYSE session runs 6.5 hours ≈ 23,400 seconds, giving ≈ 43,000 QPS on average and ≈ 215,000 QPS at a 5× peak.

Within this book that is not a big number — Ch5's monitoring system ingests tens of millions of metrics per second, Ch6's ad-click aggregation runs in the millions. So throughput was never the difficulty. Three other constraints are:

  • Latency: a competitor 100 microseconds faster takes the level you wanted. Those 100 microseconds are the product.
  • Fairness: price-time priority must hold strictly. First-come-first-served is not UX here, it is a regulated commitment.
  • Correctness and auditability: nothing lost, nothing duplicated, nothing wrong — and any trading day must be replayable order by order, so that when a regulator asks why the price was what it was at 11:11:03, you can show them.

What happens when it isn't solved? In 2012, Nasdaq's IPO matching application hit a race condition during the Facebook listing and left tens of thousands of orders stuck for over two hours — see §6.

The core, properly explained

The order book: one data structure carries the chapter

The order book is the matching engine's entire state: two sides, each side sorted into price levels, and within a level the orders held in a first-in-first-out queue. The matching rule is one sentence: an incoming order first takes the best levels on the opposite side, and whatever it can't fill rests on its own side at its price.

The engineering trick is to combine three structures: a price-ordered map of levels (to read the best price), a doubly-linked list inside each level (to keep FIFO and allow removal from the middle), and a hash map from order id to list node. With those, placing, cancelling and reading the best price are all constant time — a cancel doesn't scan the queue, it unlinks the node directly. This is why matching can run in microseconds: it is not an algorithmic problem at all, it is a few dozen memory accesses.

Single-threaded deterministic matching: the step that runs backwards

Intuition says: 43,000 QPS, so spin up threads and match in parallel. The chapter says no, and the reason is not performance but correctness:

  • Multiple threads mutating one book need locks, and lock acquisition order is non-deterministic — which of two same-price orders fills first would drift with the scheduler, which breaks time priority outright.
  • Non-deterministic means non-replayable: rerun the same input and you may get a different result, and incident forensics and regulatory audit lose their foundation instantly.
  • Contention is expensive in its own right: a contended cache miss costs hundreds of nanoseconds, which dominates work that would otherwise finish in a dozen memory accesses.

So the answer is: one book per symbol, one thread per book, lock-free and entirely in memory. Fast enough? LMAX, which put its whole business logic on one thread, has publicly claimed 6 million orders per second on that thread (see §6) — more than an order of magnitude above the 215,000 peak needed here. Scaling is purely horizontal by symbol: 100 symbols spread across many machines, but a single symbol cannot be scaled. That is the real price of this design, and why it does not suit workloads where one hot key must grow without limit.

The sequencer: flattening concurrency into one immutable stream

The matching thread is single-threaded, but intake can't be — gateways must authenticate, run risk checks and verify balances in parallel. So which of the concurrently arriving orders goes first? The answer is a sequencer between gateway and engine: it stamps every incoming event with a globally monotonic sequence number, appends it to an append-only log, and the engine consumes strictly in that order.

One component, three problems solved. (1) Fairness — precedence is nailed down by the number, so the matching thread cannot let anyone cut in. (2) Event sourcing — the stream is the ledger, and the engine's memory is merely a projection of it. (3) Replicated state machine — a standby engine fed the same stream necessarily reaches the same book, so failover needs no memory copying. This is exactly Ch12's wallet idea, reused along the latency axis.

Replay: recovery, audit and release regression share one path

Because matching is deterministic, replay becomes the universal tool. After a crash, start from the latest snapshot and replay the delta to be back where you were in seconds. When a regulator asks about the book at a given instant, replay to that sequence number. Before a new engine build ships, feed it yesterday's real stream and diff the output order by order — any behavioural drift shows up immediately. A system that stores current state and mutates fields has to build three separate mechanisms for those three jobs.

Market data: L1 / L2 / L3 trade information for bandwidth

Every match emits a state change, and the market data publisher turns those into three broadcast tiers. L1 carries only best bid/ask with sizes and the last trade. L2 exposes aggregated size for the top N price levels. L3 is the order-by-order feed — you see each resting order's place in the queue. The deeper the tier, the more bandwidth and processing it costs, which is why retail apps subscribe to L1/L2 while market makers and high-frequency firms pay for L3. Candlestick charts are not a separate system either: they are the market-data stream aggregated into time windows.

Squeezing out microseconds: first find where they go

Measure before optimising. Lay out one order's journey and the orders of magnitude are startling:

So the chapter's optimisation list is almost entirely about getting out of the operating system's way — what the book calls mechanical sympathy, writing code that goes with the hardware's grain. Kernel bypass (NIC data straight to user space, skipping the kernel stack and one copy). CPU pinning (nail a thread to one core so migration doesn't wipe its caches). Busy-wait instead of blocking (spin, and trade CPU for the microseconds a context switch costs). Lock-free ring buffers (a pre-allocated fixed array reused in a circle — no allocation, no locks; the LMAX Disruptor is the canonical one). Avoiding garbage collection (object pools and off-heap memory, so a GC pause never lands mid-match). Cache-line padding (separate hot neighbouring variables so two cores don't fight over one line). One layer further out sits colocation: the exchange rents racks to brokers, and shorter cable wins — which is why exchanges cut every customer's cable to the same length.

Why it matters / in the wild

This "sequence, single-thread, replay" pattern isn't confined to exchanges: real-time risk engines, lockstep game servers, anything where several parties must agree on one event order and answer for it later, share the same skeleton. In interviews it is a rare inverted question — when a prompt insists on fairness, auditability and microsecond latency, being able to say "this is where you should not distribute; pull the core into one thread behind a sequencer" is worth far more than reciting sharding again.

EVIDENCE FROM INDUSTRY

  • LMAX, a UK exchange, runs all of its business logic on a single thread, in memory, with event sourcing, and has publicly claimed 6 million orders per second on that thread — direct support for the chapter's claim that single-threading is not a performance concession. Martin Fowler, "The LMAX Architecture", 2011 ↗
  • The LMAX Disruptor technical paper reports measurements for its lock-free ring buffer: over 25 million messages per second with latencies under 50 nanoseconds on moderately clocked processors — the origin of the "mechanical sympathy" toolkit above. Thompson et al., "Disruptor" technical paper ↗
  • Nasdaq — the counter-example: during the 2012 Facebook IPO, its IPO Cross application allowed cancellations while the cross price was being calculated, failing validation and recalculating in a loop; tens of thousands of orders sat stuck for over two hours. The SEC fined Nasdaq $10 million in 2013 — then the largest penalty against an exchange — and pointed at the system design and the decisions taken once the limitation surfaced. SEC press release 2013-95, 29 May 2013 ↗

Pitfalls and disputes

  • Treating it as a throughput problem: 43,000 QPS doesn't rank in this book, and adding machines buys nothing. The giveaway that someone has misread the question is opening with sharding and caching.
  • Assuming one thread is slow: matching is a few dozen memory accesses, ~1 µs; one same-datacenter round trip is ~500 µs. The CPU time parallelism would save rounds to zero against the network — while spending determinism to get it.
  • Treating determinism as a nice-to-have: it is the shared foundation of recovery, audit and safe rollout. The cost of the Nasdaq incident wasn't the downtime; it was not being able to say what had happened during those two hours.
  • Fairness itself is contested: whether colocation and a microsecond race are "fair" is still argued. The counter-case is IEX, a US exchange that coils fiber to impose a deliberate 350 µs delay on every order — a "speed bump" premised on the view that erasing the speed advantage is what fairness means. That collides head-on with this chapter's default that lower is always better.
  • The book's edges: it models a single exchange with about 100 symbols, and leaves out cross-venue routing and national best bid/offer, circuit breakers, market-maker obligations, and the layer where matching is pushed into FPGAs. It gives interview-grade depth, not a production design.

Takeaways

1. The scale: ~100 symbols, a billion orders a day, a 6.5-hour session → about 43,000 QPS, peaking near 215,000 — throughput is not the hard part.

2. The three real constraints are microsecond latency, strict price-time priority, and order-by-order replay for audit.

3. The order book = price-ordered levels + a FIFO linked list per level + an order-id hash map; place, cancel and best-price reads are all constant time.

4. Matching must be single-threaded, in memory and deterministic — locks make fill order drift with the scheduler, destroying fairness and replayability together.

5. The sequencer solves three things with one component: it fixes fair ordering, it is the event-sourcing log, and it makes a standby engine reach the same state from the same stream.

6. Replay is the universal tool: crash recovery, regulatory audit and order-by-order regression before a release all run off that one stream.

7. Scaling means sharding by symbol only; a single symbol cannot grow and there is no global order across symbols — the real price of this design.

8. Market data comes in L1 / L2 / L3, trading depth directly for bandwidth; candlesticks are a derived aggregation of that stream.

9. Latency lives almost entirely in the network and kernel (~500 µs round trip vs ~1 µs of matching), hence kernel bypass, pinning and spinning, lock-free ring buffers, GC avoidance and colocation.

10. Take this away: an exchange isn't buying machines, it's buying one queue order nobody can alter.
