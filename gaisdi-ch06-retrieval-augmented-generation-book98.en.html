What is this chapter about?

You ask your company's AI assistant how much you can expense for a hotel, and it answers fast and fluently — but that rule changed last month. How could it possibly know? This chapter is about the machinery that makes a machine look things up first and only then speak.

A strange thing first

Someone who doesn't know the answer usually says so. This kind of machine doesn't. It answers anyway, and the made-up answer sounds exactly as smooth as a true one. Worse, you can't ask where it got that. It can't tell you either — everything it knows is smeared across trillions of words it once read, and it cannot point at any particular sentence.

Why the old ways don't work

Two obvious routes both fail. One is to teach it all over again: fold the new documents into its textbooks and have it reread them. That's reprinting a whole dictionary to change one phone number — expensive, slow, and the documents change again next week. The other is to spread everything on the desk: haul in the entire filing room. The desk is only so big; and even when it just about fits, finding one sentence in a roomful of paper is hard, and whatever sits in the middle of the pile is the most likely to be skipped.

How it actually works

The trick that works is to hire a librarian, and split the job in two.

Step one happens on quiet days: build the card catalogue. Cut every document into cards ahead of time, then file them by meaning — cards about the same thing sit together even when the wording is completely different ("accommodation policy" and "hotel spending cap" land in the same drawer). None of this happens while anyone is waiting.

Step two happens the moment you ask: the librarian grabs the hundred most similar cards, skims them down to the five that really bear on your question, and hands those five to the person who answers, along with your question. That person has nothing but the five cards, and exactly one rule: answer only from what is written on them, and mark which card each sentence came from. When no card fits, the correct answer is "it's not in the files."

What you gain, and what it costs

Three benefits arrive immediately: a document edited today takes effect today, with no retraining; every answer can be traced back to the original text and checked; and files this person isn't allowed to see get blocked at the card-grabbing step. The cost is a single sentence, but a hard one: the whole thing is capped by that card-grabbing step — pick the wrong cards and the more fluent the answer, the worse it is, because it is wrong in a way that reads like truth.

Remember this one line

What it produces is not just an answer, but an answer plus where it came from. You think this is about topping up what the machine knows; really it converts "speaking from memory" into "reading off the page" — and from then on, every sentence has somewhere you can go and check.

In one line

You think retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is the cheap substitute for "teaching the model more," but what it actually ships is a checkable evidence trail: knowledge you can swap out the same day, sources you can click back to, and failures you can localise to one stage. Fine-tuning and giant context windows give you none of the three. And the engineering conclusion worth carrying out of this chapter is a single sentence: a RAG system's quality ceiling is set by retrieval; the generator only applies a discount beneath it.

Glossary

  • Parametric memory: knowledge pressed into the weights during training; the model answers from "recall" and can point at no source.
  • Embedding: a piece of text represented as a list of numbers (commonly 384–1536 of them); similar meanings land on similar numbers.
  • Chunking: cutting long documents into passages that can be retrieved on their own.
  • Vector database: storage built to hold embeddings and quickly return "the most similar few."
  • Sparse retrieval / BM25: classic keyword scoring — a hit requires the words to match.
  • Dense retrieval: matching by distance between embeddings, so different wording can still hit.
  • ANN (approximate nearest neighbour): instead of comparing every vector, use an index structure to find the probable nearest few — trading a little recall for orders of magnitude of speed.
  • HNSW: a layered graph ANN index; a query starts on a sparse top layer and hops downward, closing in on the target.
  • recall@k: the fraction of questions whose answer-bearing chunk appears in the top k returned — the metric to watch on the retrieval side.
  • Reranker / cross-encoder: a model that scores question and passage jointly; far more accurate and far slower, so it only ever reorders a short candidate list.
  • Grounding: making the answer follow the supplied material rather than the model's own memory.
  • Faithfulness: whether every sentence of the answer is actually supported by the passages it was given.

What this chapter is solving

Parametric memory has three sins, and none of them is fixed by making the model bigger.

One: it goes stale. The weights freeze on the training cutoff date, while a knowledge base has documents changing every day. Re-running a fine-tune for one policy update takes weeks end to end, from data prep to evaluation and rollout; documents change by the minute. Four or five orders of magnitude apart — not a gap tuning can close.

Two: it can't cite. When the model says "the cap is $120," that number is smeared across trillions of tokens of training text and traces to no sentence anywhere. In legal, medical and financial settings, a correct answer you cannot verify is worth exactly as much as a wrong one — you can't tell which one you are holding.

Three: private data can't get in. Internal documents, a user's own files, material with access tiers — none of it should or can be pressed into one set of shared weights. Weights have no ACL (access control list): once memorised, knowledge cannot be un-remembered for just some people.

What happens if you don't solve it? The model keeps answering fluently about things it does not know, and nowhere in the pipeline is there anything to check against. Which is the upgraded form of the previous chapter's conclusion: the dangerous failure of a generative system is not silence, it is fluent wrongness.

The core, properly explained

① Two phases: arrange the knowledge offline, only fetch it online

One hard boundary splits the pipeline in two, and once you see it, every later trade-off explains itself.

Offline (indexing): documents → chunks → an embedding per chunk → write into the vector index. Turning a million documents into ten million passages, embedding them and building the index is a batch job measured in hours — because nobody is waiting.

Online (answering): question → embed → retrieve → rerank → assemble the prompt → generate. The whole thing has to fit inside one or two seconds, and generation alone often eats more than a second, leaving retrieval plus reranking perhaps 100–300 milliseconds — and that budget, not the data size, is the real constraint behind every index choice below.

② Chunking: the most underrated decision in the whole chain

The unit of retrieval is not the document, it is the chunk. How you cut all but decides whether anything can be found.

The instinct is "smaller is sharper": one chunk, one topic, an undiluted embedding, an easier match. But small chunks tear a complete argument in half — handed the first half of a sentence, the model is more likely to invent the second. Too large and a single chunk carries five or six topics, so the embedding averages out into a vector that resembles nothing, scores poorly, and wastes prompt budget besides.

The usual landing zone is 200–500 tokens per chunk with 10%–20% overlap (so an answer straddling a cut isn't lost). But three things matter more than those numbers: cut along structure (headings and paragraph boundaries, not a fixed character count that saws a table in half); carry metadata (title, section path, date, access labels — used for filtering as well as ranking); and contextualise — prepend the section heading or a one-line summary so the chunk stands on its own. That last one targets a concrete failure: a chunk reading "this cap rises to $150 from next month" is, in isolation, about nothing identifiable, and will never match a query.

③ Retrieval: keywords, vectors, and merging the two

The two routes fail in almost perfectly complementary ways.

BM25 (keywords) is unbeatable on exact strings: error code ERR_5021, part number X-200, proper names, statute numbers. Embedding those is meaningless; literal matching never misses. Its fatal flaw is paraphrase: the user asks "how do I get my money back," the document says "refund procedure," no shared terms, score zero.

Dense retrieval (vectors) is the mirror image: it crosses wording differences to catch meaning, but derails on rare proper nouns — an embedding model that has never seen that part number treats it as an ordinary string and returns a pile of chunks that merely look technical.

Hybrid retrieval merges the two rankings (commonly with reciprocal rank fusion, which weights by position rather than score and so sidesteps two incompatible score scales). It is the default in most production systems; the price is two indexes to keep in sync and one more fusion weight to tune.

A second layer is how you find the nearest few among ten million vectors. Scoring every one gives 100% recall but costs hundreds of milliseconds to seconds at that scale — the budget is gone. ANN buys speed with structure: HNSW builds a layered neighbour graph, and a query starts on the sparse top layer and hops down, landing in single-digit milliseconds, at the risk of missing the true nearest neighbour. How much it misses is governed by one dial, the search width; turn it up and recall and latency rise together. It is a continuous dial, not a switch — set it as part of your SLO instead of copying a default.

④ Reranking and assembly: a hundred candidates down to five

Retrieval uses a bi-encoder: question and passage are encoded separately, which is precisely why passage vectors can be computed offline and stored — and also why the two sides never meet, leaving only a coarse similarity to compare.

A reranker inverts that: question and passage go through the model together, so it can judge whether this passage actually answers this question. The cost is that nothing can be precomputed — every candidate is a fresh forward pass — which makes it one to two orders of magnitude slower than the bi-encoder and usable only on a short list. Hence the standard funnel: ANN pulls 100, the reranker scores them, 3–5 reach the prompt.

Assembly hides two more details. First, position matters: material parked in the middle of a long context is the most likely to be overlooked, so the strongest passages belong at the start and the end rather than laid out in similarity order. Second, every chunk costs tokens and costs latency: 5 chunks × 400 tokens = 2000 tokens, paid again on every single question. Going from k=5 to k=20 rarely moves accuracy much, while the bill and time-to-first-token move for real.

⑤ The generation side: grounding, citations, refusal

Once the passages arrive, generation's job is not to shine but to restate under constraint. Three concrete requirements: state in the prompt that the answer must come only from the supplied material; attach passage ids per sentence, so citations can be validated by code rather than admired by humans; and allow "I don't know" — when retrieval scores fall below threshold or passages contradict each other, the correct output is "not covered by the material."

That last one is the most counterintuitive product decision in the system: the refusal rate is a dial to be set deliberately, not a defect to be driven to zero. Enterprise Q&A would rather refuse a few more percent of questions than ship one invention nobody can spot.

Why it matters / in real systems

Three landing zones already run at scale: enterprise knowledge-base Q&A (internal documents plus access filtering — where the commercial value concentrates), search-style answers with citations (clickable sources under the answer, so users verify for themselves), and customer and technical support (tickets, manuals and change logs indexed together, so an answer traces to a specific passage of a specific document). The third reveals what this really is: not a smarter model, but the infrastructure that wires an organisation's existing written assets into the generation path.

Pitfalls and open arguments

  • Evaluating generation only. The most common mistake. Measure two stages separately: recall@k on retrieval, faithfulness and refusal rate on generation. A bad answer is, nine times in ten, a retrieval miss — not a dim model.
  • Mistaking "similar" for "relevant." Vector search always returns k results, even when the corpus holds no answer — there is no "no such record" output. Without your own score threshold, the model will answer from a pile of irrelevant passages.
  • Believing smaller chunks are always better. Small chunks buy hit rate and sell completeness; an argument torn in half invites the model to invent the missing half.
  • Citations as decoration. A passage id in the output does not mean the sentence came from that passage. Citations must be checked by code, or they are just a wrapper around a hallucination.
  • Enforcing permissions in the prompt. "Do not reveal HR documents" stops nothing. Filtering belongs in retrieval: unauthorised passages should never enter the candidate set.
  • Silent index staleness. A document changed, its embedding didn't, and the system confidently serves the old answer with no anomaly anywhere. Index freshness deserves the monitoring you give a data pipeline.
  • The open argument: will long context eat RAG? As windows grow, "just paste everything" looks ever more plausible. Three things haven't changed: you pay tokens for the whole corpus on every question, latency grows linearly with the material, and no window holds an entire knowledge base anyway. The likelier outcome is convergence — retrieval narrows the field, a big window then holds more candidates.
  • Period limits. The chapter's spine is the single-shot form: one question, one retrieval. Practice since has moved heavily toward multi-turn and agentic retrieval (the model decides whether and what to look up again), and toward structured routes like GraphRAG for corpus-level questions. The underlying conclusion has not moved: retrieval sets the ceiling.

Takeaways

① What RAG really ships is not an answer but a checkable evidence trail: minute-level freshness, clickable sources, and failures you can localise.

② Parametric memory's three sins — staleness, no citations, private data locked out (weights have no ACL) — survive any increase in model size.

③ The pipeline splits in two: indexing runs offline in hours; answering fits in 1–2 seconds, of which retrieval plus reranking gets 100–300 ms.

④ Retrieval sets the ceiling and generation discounts from it; a passage never retrieved cannot be rescued downstream.

⑤ Chunking is the underrated decision: 200–500 tokens with 10%–20% overlap is typical, but cutting along structure, carrying metadata and prepending the section heading matter more than the numbers.

⑥ BM25 and dense retrieval fail complementarily — one dies on paraphrase, the other on rare proper nouns; hybrid is the production default.

⑦ ANN trades recall for speed through a continuous dial; measure a brute-force recall@k baseline first, then argue about what the trade is worth.

⑧ The funnel shape is fixed: pull 100, rerank, keep 3–5. Rerankers are one to two orders of magnitude slower, so they only ever touch a short list.

⑨ Two assembly rules: strongest passages at the ends (the middle gets skipped), and a larger k reliably raises cost and latency while rarely raising accuracy.

⑩ Refusal rate is a dial you set on purpose; citations must be machine-validated; permissions must be enforced at retrieval.
